    Origins and evolution of industrial organization

    In 1936, T. P. Wright observed that every time the number of aircraft built doubled, the hours per aircraft dropped to 80 %. With that rule, unit one hundred takes less than a quarter of the time of the first.

    Scientific management Fayol Assembly line Toyota system Learning curve

    01The precursors

    Industrial organization was born with the factory. In The Wealth of Nations (1776), Adam Smith described how, in a pin factory, dividing the work into simple operations multiplied output: each worker gained dexterity, did not lose time switching tasks and could use specialized tools. In 1832, Charles Babbage added that the division made it possible to pay for each task according to the skill it demanded. The Industrial Revolution —the steam engine, textile mechanization, interchangeable parts— put those ideas into practice on a large scale.

    02Scientific management and general management

    AuthorContribution
    Frederick W. TaylorThe Principles of Scientific Management (1911): study each task with a stopwatch, set the best method and a standard time, select and train the workers, pay for performance
    Frank and Lillian GilbrethMotion study: break work down into elementary motions and eliminate the unnecessary ones
    Henry L. GanttThe bar chart for planning and controlling production
    Henri FayolGeneral and Industrial Management (1916): the functions of forecasting, organizing, commanding, coordinating and controlling, and principles such as unity of command
    Henry FordThe moving assembly line (1913): the work goes to the worker; mass production of a standardized product

    The studies at the Hawthorne plant of Western Electric, between 1924 and 1932, in which Elton Mayo took part, showed that productivity also depended on social factors and on the attention that work groups received: the human relations school was born.

    03From statistical quality to the Toyota system

    Walter Shewhart, at Bell Laboratories, invented the control chart in the 1920s; W. Edwards Deming and Joseph Juran brought statistical control and quality management to postwar Japanese industry. At Toyota, Taiichi Ohno developed a production system aimed at eliminating waste: making just in time what the next process asks for, with kanban cards, and stopping the line when a defect appears. In the West it was spread from the late 1980s as lean production. Today Industry 4.0 adds sensors, connectivity, data and flexible automation to those ideas.

    04The learning curve

    In 1936, T. P. Wright published a study on the cost of building aircraft: every time the cumulative quantity doubled, the labor hours per unit were multiplied by a constant factor, the learning rate \( r \). The time of unit \( n \) is:

    \[ T_n = T_1\, n^{b}, \quad b = \log_2 r \] With \( r \) = 80 %, \( b \approx -0.32 \): unit 2 takes 80 % of the first, unit 4 takes 64 %, unit 8 takes 51 %. The curve is used to budget production runs, set prices and negotiate contracts. Typical values range from 70 % in complex manual tasks to 95 % in highly automated processes.

    05In the laboratory

    Activity 1 · Your own learning

    Assemble the same simple circuit ten times on a breadboard, time each assembly and fit the learning rate.

    Activity 3 · Waste

    06Common mistakes

    • Reducing Taylor to the stopwatch. His proposal included methods, selection, training and incentives.
    • Believing that lean production is just keeping little inventory. It is a complete system of flow, quality and continuous improvement.
    • Applying the learning curve without limit. At some point the time settles at the one set by the machine or the process.
    • Confusing the learning rate with the reduction per unit. It is the reduction each time the quantity doubles.

    07Self-assessment

    With \(T_1 = 100\text{ h}\) and \(r = 80\,\%\), how long does unit 8 take?

    \( 100 \cdot 0.8^3 = 51.2 \) h.

    And unit 100?

    About 22.7 h.

    What management functions did Fayol define?

    Forecasting, organizing, commanding, coordinating and controlling.

    What is a kanban?

    A card or signal with which the next process asks the previous one to produce or deliver parts: production is pulled by demand.
