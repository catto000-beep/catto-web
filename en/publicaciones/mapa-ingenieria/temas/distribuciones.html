    Random variables and distributions

    Thermal noise is normal, the arrival of photons at a detector is Poisson and the time to failure of a component is exponential. Choosing the wrong distribution gives answers that look reasonable and are not.

    Random variable Expected value Normal Poisson Exponential

    01From events to numbers

    A random variable is a function that assigns a number to each outcome of the experiment. That step, which looks bureaucratic, is what makes it possible to calculate averages, variances and everything else: events cannot be added, but numbers can.

    DiscreteContinuous
    ValuesCountable: 0, 1, 2…A whole interval
    Described by\( p(x) = P(X = x) \)Density \( f(x) \)
    \( P(X = a) \)Can be positiveAlways zero
    Probability\( \sum p(x) \)\( \int f(x)\,dx \): it is an area

    The third row is uncomfortable and important: for a continuous variable, the probability of an exact value is zero. Not because it is impossible, but because there are infinitely many possible values. What makes sense to ask is the probability of falling in an interval, and that is the area under the density.

    \[ E[X] = \mu = \sum x\,p(x) \ \text{ or } \int x f(x)\,dx \qquad V[X] = \sigma^2 = E[(X-\mu)^2] = E[X^2] - \mu^2 \] The expected value is the probability-weighted average: the value that the mean of many repetitions tends to. The variance is the spread, and its root —the standard deviation— has the same units as the variable.

    02The five distributions of electronics

    There are dozens of distributions, but in electronics five of them show up again and again. Each one models a different mechanism, and choosing the wrong one gives answers that look reasonable and are not.

    DistributionModelsWhere it shows up
    BinomialHow many successes in \( n \) trials Good parts in a batch; yield of a wafer
    PoissonHow many rare events in an interval Photons at a detector, failures per year, defects per wafer
    UniformAll values equally likely Quantization error of a converter
    NormalSum of many small effects Thermal noise, manufacturing tolerances
    ExponentialTime until the next event Useful life with a constant failure rate

    03Why the normal distribution shows up everywhere

    It is not a coincidence or a convention: it is a theorem. The central limit theorem says that the sum of many independent contributions tends to a normal distribution, almost regardless of what each one looks like.

    \[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2} \qquad\qquad z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \] Standardizing with \( z \) turns any normal distribution into the standard one, with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. That is why a single table is enough for all of them.
    IntervalProbabilityIn practice
    \( \mu \pm 1\sigma \)68.27 %The standard uncertainty
    \( \mu \pm 2\sigma \)95.45 %Close to the usual 95 % confidence
    \( \mu \pm 3\sigma \)99.73 %Control limits of a process
    \( \mu \pm 6\sigma \)99.9999998 %Two defects per billion

    The thermal noise of a resistor is the perfect example: it is the sum of the motion of an astronomical number of carriers, each contributing very little, and the result is an almost perfect Gaussian. Its rms value is \( \sqrt{4kTRB} \) —thermodynamics showing up again—, but its shape comes from the central limit theorem.

    Be careful with the peak value

    A Gaussian has no maximum: however small, any value has a nonzero probability. That is why talking about the “peak value” of noise makes no sense without saying with what probability. The practical convention is to use the peak-to-peak at 99.7 %, which is six standard deviations, or sometimes 6.6 sigma for 99.9 %. A design that assumes the noise never exceeds three sigma will fail, every now and then, exactly 0.27 % of the time.

    04In the lab

    Activity 1 · Noise is Gaussian

    Digitize the noise of an amplifier with its input shorted and build the histogram of the samples. Verify the bell shape, calculate the standard deviation and compare it with the theoretical thermal noise of the input resistance.

    Activity 2 · Poisson with a weak light source

    Count the pulses from a photodiode under very dim light in fixed intervals, many times. Build the histogram of counts and verify that the variance equals the mean, which is the signature of a Poisson distribution.

    Activity 3 · Quantization error

    Digitize a slow ramp with a converter of few bits and calculate the error relative to the ideal ramp. Build its histogram, verify that it is uniform and compare its standard deviation with the theoretical \( q/\sqrt{12} \).

    05Common mistakes

    • Calculating \( P(X = a) \) for a continuous variable. It is always zero: you have to ask about an interval.
    • Confusing density with probability. The density can be greater than 1; the probability is the area.
    • Using the normal distribution to count rare events. That is what Poisson is for, especially with few counts.
    • Always assuming a constant failure rate. The exponential holds in the useful life, not in infant mortality or wear-out.
    • Talking about the peak value of noise. Without an associated probability it means nothing.
    • Applying the central limit theorem with few terms. It needs many, and none of them dominating.

    06Self-assessment

    What is \( P(X = 5) \) if \( X \) is continuous?

    Zero. For a continuous variable only intervals have nonzero probability.

    Which distribution models thermal noise?

    The normal, because of the central limit theorem: it is the sum of a huge number of small, independent contributions.

    What is the signature of a Poisson distribution?

    That the variance equals the mean.

    What is the probability of falling within \( \mu \pm 3\sigma \)?

    99.73 %. 27 cases in every ten thousand fall outside.

    What is the standard deviation of the quantization error?

    \( q/\sqrt{12} \), with \( q \) the converter’s step size, because the error is uniform over one step.

    When does the exponential distribution hold for useful life?

    As long as the failure rate is constant: the flat part of the bathtub curve.
