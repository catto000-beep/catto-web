    Electrical materials

    A 1 mm copper wire has the same resistance at 50 Hz as at DC, but at 1 MHz it has four times more: the current crowds into a 66 μm layer next to the surface.

    01Conducting materials

    A conductor is chosen for its resistivity \( \rho \), its temperature coefficient \( \alpha \), its mechanical strength, its ease of soldering and its cost. The physics of conduction was covered in electrical properties of matter; here what matters is the material as an input to a product:

    Materialρ at 20 °C (10⁻⁸ Ω·m)α (1/K)Use
    Silver1.590.0038Contacts, coatings, conductive pastes
    Annealed copper1.7240.00393Cables, windings, printed circuit board traces
    Gold2.440.0034Contacts that do not oxidize, chip bonding wires
    Aluminum2.830.0040Overhead lines, integrated circuit metallization
    Manganin, constantan≈ 45almost zeroPrecision resistors and shunts
    Nichrome≈ 110very lowPower resistors and heating elements

    The resistance of a conductor changes with temperature in an almost linear way:

    \[ R = \frac{\rho\,\ell}{A}, \quad \rho(T) = \rho_{20}\,[1 + \alpha\,(T - 20\ °\mathrm{C})] \] Copper increases its resistance by 0.4 % per degree: a winding that goes from 20 to 100 °C has 31 % more resistance. The same effect can be used to measure the winding temperature from its resistance.

    Resistive alloys, such as manganin or constantan, have high resistivity and an almost zero temperature coefficient, and so they are used in precision resistors and in the shunts used to measure current.

    02Dielectrics and insulators

    A dielectric is characterized by its relative permittivity \( \varepsilon_r \), which decides the capacitance; its dielectric strength, the maximum field before breakdown; its loss factor \( \tan\delta \), the fraction of energy that is dissipated in each cycle; and its volume and surface resistivity.

    MaterialApproximate εrUse
    Air1Variable capacitors; strength of about 3 kV/mm
    PTFE (Teflon)2.1Coaxial cables and microwave boards, with very low losses
    Polypropylene2.2Low-loss film capacitors
    FR-4 (glass and epoxy)4.2 to 4.8General-purpose printed circuit boards
    Alumina9.8Ceramic and hybrid substrates
    Barium titanatethousandsHigh-capacitance ceramic capacitors, which vary with voltage and temperature

    The maximum temperature of an insulator defines its thermal class according to IEC 60085: 105 °C (A), 120 °C (E), 130 °C (B), 155 °C (F) and 180 °C (H), among others. Above that temperature the insulator ages quickly; as a rule of thumb, its life is cut in half for every 8 to 10 °C of excess.

    03The skin effect

    In alternating current, the magnetic field inside the conductor induces currents that oppose the flow of current through the center, and the current concentrates near the surface. The current density falls off exponentially inward, with a penetration depth:

    \[ \delta = \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi f \mu}} \] In copper, δ is 9.3 mm at 50 Hz, 0.66 mm at 10 kHz and 66 μm at 1 MHz. When the radius is much greater than δ, the current flows in a ring of thickness δ and the resistance grows as the square root of the frequency.

    For a round wire the exact resistance comes from Bessel functions; when the radius \( r \) exceeds \( \delta \) several times, it is approximated by:

    \[ \frac{R_{ac}}{R_{dc}} \approx \frac{r}{2\delta} + \frac{1}{4} \] Added to this is the proximity effect between neighboring conductors, which in multilayer windings can be greater than the skin effect.

    That is why at high frequency Litz wire is used, many fine insulated strands twisted so that each one occupies every position; silver-plated conductors, because the current flows only on the surface; and tubes instead of bars.

    Activity 2 · Skin effect

    With an LCR meter, measure the series resistance of a solid-wire inductor and of a Litz one between 1 kHz and 100 kHz, and compare.

    Activity 3 · Dielectrics

    Measure the capacitance and dissipation factor of polypropylene, polyester and ceramic capacitors of the same nominal capacitance, at several frequencies.

    05Common mistakes

    • Calculating the losses of a high-frequency winding with the DC resistance. The skin and proximity effects multiply them.
    • Forgetting the temperature coefficient of copper. A hot winding dissipates more than in the cold calculation.
    • Taking the permittivity of FR-4 as constant. It varies with frequency, manufacturer and resin content.
    • Running an insulator above its thermal class. It does not fail right away, but its life is greatly shortened.

    06Self-assessment

    What resistance do 100 m of 1.5 mm² copper have at 20 °C, and at 70 °C?

    1.15 Ω at 20 °C and 1.38 Ω at 70 °C.

    What is the penetration depth in copper at 10 kHz?

    About 0.66 mm.

    What maximum temperature does a class F insulator allow?

    155 °C.

    Why does Litz wire reduce the AC resistance?

    Each strand is thinner than the penetration depth and the twisting makes all of them carry the same current.
