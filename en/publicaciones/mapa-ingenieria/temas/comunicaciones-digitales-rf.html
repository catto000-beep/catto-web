    Software-defined radio and digital communications

    A 20 MHz band centered at 70 MHz can be digitized with a 56 MS/s converter, less than its highest frequency. The converter, well chosen, acts as a mixer.

    Software-defined radio Bandpass sampling Nyquist zones OFDM MIMO

    01Software-defined radio

    In a classic receiver, each function is a circuit: the channel filter, the demodulator, the carrier recovery. In a software-defined radio (SDR), the analog front end is reduced to amplifying, sometimes translating, and digitizing; everything else is done by a processor, an FPGA or a computer. Changing the standard, the bandwidth or the modulation is changing the program. The same blocks of receivers and transmitters are still there, but on the digital side of the converter:

    Digital blockFunction
    Numerically controlled oscillator and digital mixerBring the channel down to baseband, with perfectly balanced I and Q
    Decimating filters (CIC and FIR)Select the channel and reduce the sampling rate
    SynchronizationRecover the carrier, the symbol clock and the frame
    Equalizer and demodulatorCorrect the channel and decide the symbols
    Channel decoderCorrect errors using the code

    The processing relies on the hardware of digital signal processing, and the modulations and their error rates are those of noise in digital modulations.

    02Bandpass sampling

    The sampling theorem calls for sampling at twice the maximum frequency, but for a bandpass signal, which occupies only from \( f_L \) to \( f_H \), twice the bandwidth is enough, if the sampling frequency is chosen well: the alias of the band falls cleanly in the first Nyquist zone, without overlapping itself. This is undersampling, which replaces a mixer with the converter itself:

    \[ \frac{2 f_H}{n} \le f_s \le \frac{2 f_L}{n - 1}, \quad n = 1, 2, \dots, \left\lfloor \frac{f_H}{B} \right\rfloor \] Each n gives a valid interval; with even n the band appears inverted, which is corrected in the processing. The theoretical minimum frequency is 2B, but in practice margin is left for the filters and the clock tolerance. The converter has to have analog bandwidth up to \( f_H \) and a clock with low phase noise, because the jitter is multiplied with the signal frequency.

    03Current digital systems

    Mobile communications, Wi-Fi and digital television combine everything seen: adaptive QAM modulations, OFDM with hundreds of subcarriers that turn a channel with echoes into many flat channels, LDPC or turbo codes close to the Shannon limit, multiple antennas (MIMO) that multiply the capacity by exploiting the different propagation paths, and an RF front end that is, in large part, a software-defined radio.

    05Common mistakes

    • Sampling a bandpass signal at any frequency greater than 2B. Only the valid intervals work.
    • Forgetting the bandpass filter before the converter. Noise and signals from all the Nyquist zones fold onto the band.
    • Using a noisy clock to undersample. Jitter degrades the signal-to-noise ratio in proportion to the signal frequency.
    • Forgetting the spectrum inversion in even zones. The frequency has to be inverted in the processing.

    06Self-assessment

    What sampling frequencies work for a 60 to 80 MHz band with n = 2?

    From 80 to 120 MHz.

    With fs = 100 MHz, where does that band appear and how?

    Between 20 and 40 MHz, inverted.

    What is the theoretical minimum sampling frequency for a 20 MHz band?

    40 MHz, if the band is located at an integer position relative to its width.

    What advantage does the digital mixer have over an analog one?

    Its I and Q branches are exactly equal: there is no imbalance or carrier leakage.
