    Power in transmission lines

    An antenna with SWR 3 at the end of 20 m of RG-58 looks like SWR 1.5 from the transmitter. The equipment is satisfied, but less than a third of the power reaches the antenna.

    Incident and reflected power Attenuation Mismatch loss SWR at the transmitter Power rating

    01The power that travels along the line

    In a transmission line the power travels in two waves: the incident one, which goes from the generator to the load, and the reflected one, which comes back. Since the characteristic impedance is real in a low-loss line, each wave carries its own power and what the load receives is the difference:

    \[ P_{inc} = \frac{|V^+|^2}{2 Z_0} \] \[ P_{ref} = |\Gamma_L|^2\, P_{inc} \] \[ P_L = P_{inc}\,\left(1 - |\Gamma_L|^2\right) \] With peak amplitudes. A directional power meter, the instrument of every transmitter, measures the incident and the reflected power separately; the SWR comes from their ratio.

    With an SWR of 2, 11% of the incident power is reflected; with an SWR of 3, 25%. That power is not necessarily lost: it goes back toward the generator and, if the generator is not matched, it is reflected again toward the load. What is lost is what the line itself dissipates on each trip.

    02The losses of the line

    A real line attenuates the wave as it advances. The attenuation is given in decibels per 100 m, with the line matched, and it has two causes with different dependence on frequency:

    \[ \alpha_{dB} \approx k_1 \sqrt{f} + k_2\, f \] The first term is the resistance of the conductors, which grows with the square root of frequency because of the skin effect; the second is the dielectric, which dissipates in proportion to frequency and dominates at microwaves.

    03Mismatch and losses together

    If the line has losses and the load is also mismatched, the reflected wave is attenuated too on its trip back. This has two consequences. The first: the total loss is greater than that of the matched line, because the power that goes and comes back travels along the cable more than once. With \( a \) the attenuation of the matched line as a power ratio (\( a = 10^{A/10} \)):

    \[ |\Gamma_{in}| = \frac{|\Gamma_L|}{a} \] \[ \frac{P_{in}}{P_L} = \frac{a^2 - |\Gamma_L|^2}{a\,(1 - |\Gamma_L|^2)} \] The first expression gives the reflection seen from the transmitter, attenuated twice. The second is the total loss in the line, between the net power that goes in and the power the load receives; with \( \Gamma_L = 0 \) it reduces to \( a \).

    The second consequence is misleading: the cable improves the SWR that the transmitter measures. An antenna with an SWR of 3 at the end of 20 m of RG-58 at 146 MHz looks like SWR 1.5 from the equipment. The transmitter is satisfied, but less than a third of the power reaches the antenna. That is why the SWR is measured, when possible, at the antenna feed point.

    04How much power a cable can take

    A cable has two power limits. The thermal one: what it dissipates heats it, and the dielectric softens above a certain temperature; since the losses grow with frequency, the power rating drops with it. The voltage one: with standing waves, the voltage maxima are \( (1 + |\Gamma_L|) \) times those of the matched line, and the dielectric can break down. Manufacturers give the power rating with the line matched and a derating factor for each SWR.

    For a transmitter, mismatch is also a risk: the reflected power returns to the output stage and the transistors can receive voltages higher than the design values. That is why solid-state transmitters automatically reduce their power when the SWR exceeds a threshold.

    05Where it appears in electronics

    CaseWhat decides the useful power

    Television antenna with amplifierThe amplifier goes on the mast, ahead of the cable losses

    The complete link, with the gain of the antennas and the path loss, is put together in the unit on antennas; the cable losses enter that budget by subtracting decibels, just as in the microwave links of the technician program.

    07Common mistakes

    • Trusting the SWR measured at the equipment. A very lossy cable artificially improves it.
    • Believing that all the reflected power is lost. What is lost is what the line and the generator dissipate, not the reflected power itself.
    • Using the attenuation at one frequency for another. It grows with frequency, and at 2.4 GHz a thin cable is useless.
    • Ignoring the voltage limit at high SWR. A cable that withstands the matched power can break down with a badly mismatched load.

    08Self-assessment

    What fraction of the incident power is reflected at SWR 3?

    \( |\Gamma| = 0.5 \), so \( |\Gamma|^2 = 25 \)%.

    How much power reaches the matched load through a cable with 6 dB of loss if 100 W go in?

    \( 100 / 10^{0.6} \approx 25 \) W.

    An antenna with SWR 3 is at the end of a cable with 4 dB of loss. What SWR does the transmitter measure?

    \( |\Gamma_{in}| = 0.5 / 10^{0.4} \approx 0.2 \), and the SWR is \( 1.2 / 0.8 = 1.5 \).

    Why does the attenuation of a coaxial cable grow faster than the square root of frequency at microwaves?

    Because the dielectric loss grows in proportion to frequency and comes to dominate over that of the conductors.
