    Techniques for preventing accidents

    For every serious injury there are hundreds of incidents that hurt no one. They are the cheapest information for prevention, and the one that is almost never recorded.

    Bird’s pyramid Swiss cheese Causal tree Frequency rate Severity rate

    01Accidents have causes

    Prevention starts from a simple idea: an accident is not bad luck but the result of causes that can be identified and eliminated. Three models shaped the way we think about it:

    ModelCentral ideaConsequence for prevention
    Heinrich’s domino (1931)A chain of factors ends in the injury; unsafe acts and conditions are the central dominoRemoving one domino stops the chain
    Bird’s pyramid (1969)For every serious injury there are many minor ones, plus property damage and hundreds of incidents without consequencesIncidents are warnings: recording them makes it possible to act sooner
    Reason’s Swiss cheese (1990)Every barrier has holes; the accident occurs when they line upSeveral independent barriers, and a search for the latent failures of the organization

    Reason’s model changed the focus: the operator’s error is usually the last failure in a series that began in design, maintenance or organizational decisions. Blaming the person does not eliminate those latent failures.

    02Analytical and operational techniques

    Prevention techniques are divided into analytical ones, which look for the causes, and operational ones, which act on them:

    • Safety inspections: planned walk-throughs with checklists to detect hazardous conditions before they cause an accident.
    • Accident and incident investigation: the causal tree, developed in France in the 1970s, starts from the event and asks, for each antecedent, what was necessary and sufficient for it to occur, until it reaches facts that can be corrected. It looks for facts, not culprits.
    • Job analysis: dividing the task into steps and listing the hazards and controls for each one.
    • Accident statistics: measuring in order to compare periods and sectors, and to see whether the measures work.
    • Training, signage, standards and procedures: the operational techniques covered in industrial hygiene and safety and in the unit on safe work procedures.

    03Accident rates

    Counting accidents is not enough: a large company has more accidents than a small one even if it is safer. The rates relate them to the exposure:

    \[ I_I = \frac{\text{lost-time accidents}}{\text{workers}} \times 10^3 \] \[ I_F = \frac{\text{lost-time accidents}}{\text{hours worked}} \times 10^6 \] \[ I_G = \frac{\text{workdays lost}}{\text{hours worked}} \times 10^3 \] The incidence rate \( I_I \) counts accidents per thousand workers; the frequency rate \( I_F \), per million hours, corrects for the length of the working day; the severity rate \( I_G \), workdays lost per thousand hours. The average duration of the absences is the ratio of workdays lost to accidents.

    04In the laboratory

    Activity 1 · Causal tree

    Take an accident described in a public report and build its causal tree as a group. Identify which of the facts could have been corrected and with what measure.

    Activity 2 · Inspection

    Design a checklist for an electronics laboratory, apply it and classify the findings by severity.

    Activity 3 · Incident log

    Propose a short form for recording no-injury incidents in the workshop and discuss how to prevent it from being used to look for culprits.

    05Common mistakes

    • Ending the investigation at human error. It is the starting point for asking why it was possible.
    • Comparing numbers of accidents between companies of different sizes. Rates are what get compared.
    • Ignoring incidents. They are the base of the pyramid and the cheapest information for prevention.
    • Taking Bird’s ratio as a law. It is an average from one study; what matters is the shape, not the numbers.

    06Self-assessment

    A company of 400 workers had 10 lost-time accidents. What is its incidence rate?

    25 per thousand workers.

    If those workers each worked 1800 hours, what is the frequency rate?

    \( 10/720\,000 \times 10^6 \approx 13.9 \).

    What does the Swiss cheese model propose for prevention?

    Several independent barriers, and correcting the latent failures of the organization, not just the last error.

    What question guides the construction of a causal tree?

    For each fact: what was necessary and sufficient for it to occur.
