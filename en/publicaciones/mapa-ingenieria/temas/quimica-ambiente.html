    Environmental chemistry

    One kilogram of sulfur hexafluoride escaped from an etch chamber warms as much as twenty-three and a half metric tons of carbon dioxide. It is inert, it is not toxic and it does not touch the ozone layer: the problem is that it never goes away.

    01The atmosphere as a reactor

    Environmental chemistry studies reactions that take place in systems that nobody designed and that nobody can opt out of. Two of them get confused all the time and it pays to separate them from the start, because they have different causes, a different altitude and a different solution.

    Ozone holeGreenhouse effect
    WhereStratosphere, 15 to 35 kmTroposphere, the first 10 km
    What happensThe ozone that filters ultraviolet is destroyed The infrared radiation that would escape is trapped
    ResponsibleChlorofluorocarbons, halonsCO₂, CH₄, N₂O and the fluorinated gases
    StatusRecovering since the Montreal Protocol Increasing

    The last row matters: the ozone hole is the example that a global atmospheric problem can be reversed. The mechanism was identified —a single chlorine atom destroys tens of thousands of ozone molecules in a catalytic cycle—, the culprits were banned and the layer is recovering. It will take until the middle of the century, but it is happening.

    \[ \mathrm{Cl} + \mathrm{O_3} \rightarrow \mathrm{ClO} + \mathrm{O_2} \qquad \mathrm{ClO} + \mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl} + \mathrm{O_2} \] Chlorine comes out of the second reaction intact and starts over: it is a catalyst, and that is why a tiny amount is enough to do enormous damage. It is the same idea of catalysis from the kinetics unit, played against us.

    02The gases used by the electronics industry itself

    Making chips consumes fluorinated gases for plasma etching and for cleaning the deposition chambers. They are chemically inert, nontoxic and do not touch the ozone layer. The problem is another one: they absorb infrared with extraordinary efficiency and almost never degrade.

    \[ \text{CO}_2\text{ equivalent} = m \times \mathrm{GWP}_{100} \] The global warming potential compares, over one hundred years, the effect of one kilogram of a gas with that of one kilogram of CO₂. It combines how much it absorbs with how long it lasts in the atmosphere.
    Lab · global warming potential

    The process gases of the electronics industry, with their IPCC values. Choose one and the mass emitted, and the calculation translates it into CO₂ equivalent and into kilometers driven by a car. The scale is logarithmic because otherwise they would not fit.

    NF₃ and the fine print

    Nitrogen trifluoride entered the industry precisely as a “clean” replacement for other perfluorinated compounds, because it decomposes better in the plasma and leaves less unreacted residue. But what escapes without decomposing has a potential of 16,100, and its use grew with the production of displays and solar panels. It is a good reminder that “less bad than the previous one” is not the same as harmless, and that the balance has to be worked out with numbers.

    03Electronic waste

    An electronic device at the end of its life is both hazardous waste and a deposit. Both for the same elements.

    ElementWhere it comes fromWhy it is a concern
    LeadOld solder, tube glassNeurotoxic; it accumulates and is not eliminated
    MercuryBacklight lamps, some relaysIt gets methylated and moves up the food chain
    CadmiumOld batteries, pigments, some semiconductorsKidney damage, highly persistent
    Hexavalent chromiumAnticorrosion coatingsConfirmed carcinogen
    Brominated compoundsFlame retardants in circuit boardsWhen burned they give dioxins

    The RoHS directive restricts six of these substances in devices sold in Europe, and its most visible consequence for anyone who solders was the change to lead-free solder: the tin–silver–copper alloy melts about 30 °C higher than the old tin–lead one, wets less well and demands a higher tip temperature. The WEEE directive is the other half: it requires collecting and treating the device at the end of its life.

    A ton of boards against a ton of ore

    A ton of printed circuit boards contains considerably more gold than a ton of ore from an active mine. That turns recycling into urban mining, and explains why the real problem is not technical but logistical: the challenge lies in collecting the material, not in recovering the metal. It also explains the worst scenario, which is open-air burning to recover the copper: it releases dioxins from the brominated compounds and heavy metals into the smoke.

    04Water, which is the other input

    A semiconductor plant consumes millions of liters of ultrapure water per day, and returns it loaded with what it carried off: acids, dissolved metals, solvents. Effluent treatment applies in a chain almost everything in this subject.

    StageWhat it doesWhich unit explains it

    Precipitation of metalsConverts them to insoluble hydroxidesSolubility product

    The third column is the close of the subject: none of those stages is a new topic. The general chemistry covered in the second level is exactly what is applied so that a chip factory does not ruin the watershed where it is located.

    05In the lab

    Activity 1 · Neutralize and precipitate

    Prepare a simulated effluent with acidified copper sulfate. Neutralize with sodium hydroxide while following the pH and observe at what value the copper hydroxide precipitates. Compare with what its \( K_{sp} \) predicts.

    Activity 2 · Compare two solders

    Solder the same joints with tin–lead alloy and with tin–silver–copper, recording tip temperature and the appearance of the joint. Discuss what changed in the process as a result of the regulatory requirement.

    Activity 3 · Inventory of a device

    Disassemble a device no longer in use and take an inventory of its materials: metals, plastics, batteries, display. Classify each fraction by destination —recyclable, hazardous, inert— and estimate what percentage of the mass is recoverable.

    06Common mistakes

    • Confusing the ozone hole with the greenhouse effect. Different altitude, different cause, different solution.
    • Believing that an inert gas is harmless. Perfluorinated compounds are inert, and for that very reason they last millennia in the atmosphere.
    • Comparing masses instead of CO₂ equivalent. A kilo of SF₆ is not comparable with a kilo of CO₂.
    • Assuming that lead-free means no problems. It melts higher, wets less well and brought defects of its own such as tin whiskers.
    • Treating electronic waste as ordinary trash. It is hazardous waste and at the same time a resource.
    • Forgetting the atmospheric lifetime. GWP measures not only how much a gas absorbs, but also how long it stays.

    07Self-assessment

    Why does one chlorine atom destroy so much ozone?

    Because it acts as a catalyst: it comes out of the cycle intact and starts over tens of thousands of times.

    What does GWP measure?

    How much a kilogram of a gas warms compared with a kilogram of CO₂ over one hundred years, combining absorption and persistence.

    Why are perfluorinated compounds a concern if they are inert?

    Precisely for that reason: nothing degrades them, they last thousands of years and absorb infrared very efficiently.

    What did RoHS change in workshop practice?

    The solder: lead-free alloy melts about 30 °C higher and wets less well, so it requires a higher tip temperature.

    What is urban mining?

    Recovering metals from electronic waste, which has more gold per ton than the ore of an active mine.

    Why is the ozone hole closing?

    Because the mechanism was identified and the Montreal Protocol banned chlorofluorocarbons. It is the case of a global problem that can be reversed.
