    Electromagnetic waves

    A charge at rest does not radiate, and neither does one moving at constant velocity. An accelerated charge does, and an alternating current in a conductor is exactly that: all of antenna engineering comes from there.

    Poynting Near field Friis Link budget Photon

    01What an electromagnetic wave looks like

    From Maxwell’s equations in vacuum comes a wave with a very rigid structure: the two fields are perpendicular to each other and both perpendicular to the direction of propagation. It is transverse, needs no medium and its two fields oscillate in phase.

    \[ E = c\,B \qquad\qquad \vec{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec{E}\times\vec{B} \qquad\qquad I = \langle S\rangle = \frac{E_0^2}{2Z_0} \] The Poynting vector \( \vec{S} \) gives the power per unit area and points in the direction of propagation. Its time average is the intensity, and \( Z_0 = 377\ \Omega \) is the impedance of vacuum.

    That \( E = cB \) explains why people almost always talk about the electric field and almost never about the magnetic one: in SI units the number for \( B \) is three hundred million times smaller, and on a charge the electric force dominates completely except at relativistic speeds.

    The wave pushes

    An electromagnetic wave also carries momentum, so when it strikes a surface it exerts radiation pressure: \( P = I/c \) if it is absorbed and double that if it is reflected. With sunlight in Earth orbit —1361 W/m²— that is about 4.5 micropascals, nothing for a person and enough to move a solar sail of square kilometers, or to slowly shift the orbit of a satellite if it is not compensated.

    02What radiates and what does not

    A stationary charge has a field but does not radiate. One moving at constant velocity does not either. An accelerated charge radiates, and from that comes all of antenna engineering: an alternating current in a conductor is charge accelerating back and forth.

    ZoneDistanceHow the field falls offWhat happens
    Reactive near fieldLess than \( \lambda/2\pi \)As \( 1/r^3 \) The energy goes back and forth: nothing is radiated
    Far fieldMore than \( 2D^2/\lambda \)As \( 1/r \) The energy leaves and never comes back

    The first row is the one that explains why a 50 Hz installation does not radiate: with a wavelength of 6000 km, everything one could possibly wire is in the near field, and there the energy oscillates between the conductor and space without escaping. It is also the principle of wireless charging and of a transformer: near-field coupling, not radiation.

    \[ \mathrm{EIRP} = P_t\,G_t \qquad\qquad P_r = \frac{P_t G_t G_r \lambda^2}{(4\pi d)^2} \] The Friis equation. The received power falls with the square of the distance —that is geometry, not absorption— and also with the square of the frequency, because at higher frequency the receiving antenna has less effective area for the same gain.

    03Energy and photons

    The wave description is enough for almost everything in this unit, but when the frequency rises a lot the other side appears: energy travels in packets.

    \[ E = h f = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \qquad h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s} \qquad E[\mathrm{eV}] = \frac{1240}{\lambda[\mathrm{nm}]} \] The last form is the one worth keeping in your head: a 1240 nm photon has 1 eV. From there follows directly the color of an LED according to its band gap and the wavelength limit that a photodiode can detect.
    Material\( E_g \)LED \( \lambda \)Color
    GaAs1.42 eV873 nmInfrared: remote controls
    GaAsP1.9 eV653 nmRed
    GaP2.2 eV564 nmGreen
    InGaN2.7 eV459 nmBlue

    The table is the unit on chemical bonds seen from the viewpoint of optics: the color of an LED is its band gap. And silicon, at 1.12 eV, emits at 1107 nm, in the infrared, but it also has an indirect band gap and so is a terrible emitter: other compounds are used to make light. As a detector, on the other hand, it works very well up to those 1100 nm, and that is the reason a silicon photodiode sees nothing beyond.

    04In the lab

    Activity 1 · Verifying the inverse-square law

    With a low-power transmitter and a field-strength meter, record the received signal at increasing distances in an open space. Plot in dB against the logarithm of the distance and verify the slope of 20 dB per decade.

    Activity 2 · Gain of an antenna

    Compare the signal received with a whip antenna and with a pointed directional one, at the same distance. Calculate the difference in dB and compare it with the stated gain.

    Activity 3 · The spectrum of an LED

    With a simple spectrometer, measure the emission wavelength of LEDs of different colors. Calculate the photon energy and compare it with the tabulated band gap of the semiconductor of each one.

    05Common mistakes

    • Believing that the magnetic field of a wave is negligible. It is not: it is \( E/c \), small in number and essential to the phenomenon.
    • Thinking that free-space loss is absorption. It is geometry: the same power spread over a larger sphere.
    • Assuming that an antenna amplifies. Gain is directivity: it concentrates what there is, it does not add power.
    • Measuring a radiated field in the near field. There \( 1/r \) does not hold and the measurement does not mean what it seems to.
    • Forgetting that the Friis equation gets worse with frequency. At equal gain, 5 GHz reaches less far than 2.4.
    • Confusing transmitter power with EIRP. The latter already includes the gain of the antenna.

    06Self-assessment

    What is the relation between \( E \) and \( B \) in a wave?

    \( E = cB \), and the two oscillate in phase, perpendicular to each other and to the propagation.

    What radiates and what does not?

    An accelerated charge radiates. A stationary one, or one at constant velocity, does not.

    What is the impedance of vacuum?

    377 ohms. It relates the electric field to the magnetic one in a plane wave.

    Why does a 50 Hz installation not radiate?

    Because with a wavelength of 6000 km everything is in the near field, where the energy goes back and forth without escaping.

    What energy does a 620 nm photon have?

    Two electronvolts: \( 1240/620 \).

    Why does a silicon photodiode not see beyond 1100 nm?

    Because there the photon no longer has the energy of its 1.12 eV band gap and does not generate an electron-hole pair.
