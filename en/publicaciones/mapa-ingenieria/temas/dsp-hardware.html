    Digital signal processing and hardware

    A filter with a Blackman window attenuates 75 dB in floating point. With 12-bit coefficients, barely 57: the precision of the hardware decides which filter can be implemented.

    Multiply-accumulate Harvard architecture Q15 fixed point DMA and blocks FFT

    01An operation repeated millions of times

    Almost all digital signal processing boils down to sums of products. An \( N \)-coefficient FIR filter computes each output sample with \( N \) multiplications and \( N \) additions; so does an FFT, a correlation or an equalizer. The basic operation is multiply-accumulate (MAC):

    \[ y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} h[k]\, x[n-k] \] \[ \text{MAC/s} = N\, f_s \] A 51-coefficient FIR at 48 kHz needs 2.45 million MACs per second per channel; a channel filter in a software-defined radio, hundreds of millions. The cycle budget per sample is \( f_{clk}/f_s \).

    A general-purpose processor does a MAC in several instructions: two memory reads, a multiplication, an addition and the pointer update. A digital signal processor (DSP) is designed to do it in one cycle.

    02The hardware of a DSP

    ResourceWhat it is for
    Modified Harvard architectureSeparate buses for instructions and data, and sometimes two for data: the coefficient and the sample are read in the same cycle
    Hardware multiplier and accumulatorOne MAC per cycle, with a wide accumulator
    Circular addressingThe filter’s delay line without moving data: the pointer wraps back to the start on its own
    Bit-reversed addressingThe reordering of the FFT with no computation
    Zero-overhead loopsRepeating a block of instructions without branch instructions
    DMAMoving the samples from the converter to memory without using the core

    Today these ideas are also in microcontrollers: the Cortex-M4 and M7 have single-cycle MAC instructions and SIMD operations that process two 16-bit samples at once. For higher bandwidths, FPGAs are used, with hundreds of multipliers in parallel, and GPUs. The general organization of processors was covered in processor architecture.

    03Fixed point: Q15, saturation and guard bits

    Many DSPs and almost all microcontrollers work in fixed point: the numbers are integers that are interpreted as fractions. In the Q15 format, a 16-bit integer represents values between −1 and 1 − 2⁻¹⁵:

    \[ x = \frac{X}{2^{15}}, \quad X \in [-32768,\ 32767] \] The product of two Q15 numbers is a 32-bit Q30. Adding many products can overflow, which is why accumulators have guard bits, 40 bits in many 16-bit DSPs. When going back to 16 bits the result is rounded and saturated: an overflow that wraps around from positive to negative produces a click; saturation produces only clipping.

    The coefficients are also rounded. A filter designed in floating point, such as those in filter design, changes its response when quantized: the error in each coefficient is noise that fills the stopband, and it limits the attenuation to about 6 dB per bit.

    04Real time: sample by sample or in blocks

    A real-time system has to finish processing each sample before the next one arrives. There are two ways to organize it:

    • Sample by sample: one interrupt for each sample from the converter. Latency is minimal, but the cost of entering and leaving the interrupt is paid on every sample.
    • In blocks: the DMA fills one buffer while the core processes the other (ping-pong). A whole block is processed with efficient loops, in exchange for a latency of one or two blocks. It is the usual approach in audio and communications.

    The \( N \)-point FFT needs \( (N/2) \log_2 N \) butterflies, each with one complex multiplication: 5120 for 1024 points, against more than a million products for the direct DFT. DSPs accelerate it with bit-reversed addressing and, in some cases, with dedicated accelerators.

    06Common mistakes

    • Designing the filter in floating point and quantizing without checking. The attenuation can drop by tens of dB.
    • Accumulating in 16 bits. The products are summed in a wide accumulator and rounded only once at the end.
    • Letting the overflow wrap around. You have to saturate, or scale the input so that it does not occur.
    • Forgetting the latency of block processing. In a control loop it can make the system unstable.

    07Self-assessment

    How many MACs per second does a 128-coefficient FIR at 96 kHz in stereo need?

    \( 128 \cdot 96\,000 \cdot 2 \approx 24.6 \) million.

    What value does the integer 16384 represent in Q15?

    0.5.

    How many butterflies does a 4096-point FFT have?

    \( 2048 \cdot 12 = 24\,576 \).

    Why a 40-bit accumulator in a 16-bit DSP?

    The products are 32 bits; the 8 guard bits make it possible to add up to 256 full-scale products without overflow.
