    Oscillatory motion

    The oscillating spring and the RLC circuit obey the same differential equation. Whoever understands one understands the other, and mechanical resonance is literally the same as that of a radio tuner.

    Simple harmonic Pendulum Damping Resonance Q factor

    01Simple harmonic motion

    Whenever a restoring force is proportional to the displacement and points in the opposite direction, the resulting motion is simple harmonic. Hooke’s law, \(F = -k \cdot x\), combined with the second law gives:

    \[ m \frac{d^2 x}{d t^2} + k x = 0 \quad \implies \quad x (t) = A \cos (\omega_0 t + \varphi) , \; \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \] The natural frequency does not depend on the amplitude: that is why a pendulum works as a clock.
    SystemNatural angular frequencyCondition
    Mass and spring\( \omega _{0} = \sqrt{k/m} \)Ideal spring
    Simple pendulum\( \omega _{0} = \sqrt{g/L} \)Small angles
    Physical pendulum\( \omega _{0} = \sqrt{m\cdot g\cdot d/I} \)Small angles
    LC circuit\( \omega _{0} = 1/\sqrt{L\cdot C} \)No resistance

    The energy is exchanged between kinetic and elastic potential, and their sum stays constant: \(E = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot k \cdot A^2\). In the LC circuit exactly the same thing happens between the capacitor and the inductor.

    02Damped oscillations

    With a viscous friction force proportional to the velocity, \(F = -b \cdot v\), the equation is completed:

    \[ m \ddot{x} + b \dot{x} + k x = 0 \] It is the same equation as the series RLC circuit, with L in place of m, R in place of b and 1/C in place of k.
    RegimeConditionBehavior
    Underdamped\( b < 2\sqrt{m\cdot k} \)Oscillates with an amplitude that decays exponentially
    Critically damped\( b = 2\sqrt{m\cdot k} \)Returns to rest in the shortest possible time, without oscillating
    Overdamped\( b > 2\sqrt{m\cdot k} \)Returns slowly, without oscillating
    The quality factor Q

    \(Q = \sqrt{m \cdot k}/b\) measures how many oscillations survive before the amplitude drops appreciably. It is the same Q as in a resonant circuit: a high Q means little loss, a sharp resonance and selectivity. A quartz crystal reaches a Q of 105, and that is why it sets the frequency of a clock or of a microcontroller.

    Critical damping is what is sought in a car’s shock absorber, in the motion of the needle of an analog instrument and in the response of a switching power supply: reaching the final value quickly without overshooting.

    03Forced oscillations and resonance

    If a periodic force \(F_0 \cdot \cos(\omega t)\) is also applied, once the transient has died out the system oscillates at the frequency of the excitation, not its own, and with an amplitude that depends on how close one is to the other:

    \[ A (\omega) = \frac{F_0 / m}{\sqrt{{\left(\omega_0^2 -\omega^2 \right)}^2 + {\left(b \omega / m \right)}^2}} \]

    When \(\omega\) approaches \(\omega_0\) the denominator becomes small and the amplitude grows: that is resonance. With little damping the amplitude can become destructive —it is what shatters glasses with sound and what forces engineers to study the wind on a bridge—, but put to good use it is what lets you tune in a station among all those that reach the antenna.

    05Where it shows up in electronics

    • Quartz crystals and MEMS resonators. A mechanical oscillator of very high Q that sets the timebase of almost everything digital.
    • Filters and tuning. The resonance curve in this lab is the frequency response of a bandpass filter.
    • Piezoelectric transducers. Ultrasonic emitters work at their mechanical resonance frequency, where the efficiency is highest.
    • Vibration-isolation mounting. Onboard equipment is mounted on elastic elements chosen so that its natural frequency stays far from that of the excitation.
    • Response of a switching power supply. The feedback loop is compensated aiming for a behavior close to critical: fast but without overshoot.

    06In the lab

    Activity 1 · Measuring g with a pendulum

    Time twenty oscillations of a pendulum of known length and solve for \(g\) from \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\). Repeat with amplitudes of 5° and 30° to see where the approximation stops holding.

    Activity 2 · Spring constant and Q

    Measure k statically with weights, then let the mass oscillate and film it. From the decay of the amplitude you get \(b\), and with it \(Q\). Verify that the measured \(\omega_0\) matches \(\sqrt{k/m}\).

    Activity 3 · Resonance with a speaker and an accelerometer

    Drive a cantilevered strip with a speaker sweeping through frequencies and record the vibration with an accelerometer. Plot the amplitude against frequency and measure the −3 dB bandwidth: \(Q = f_0/\Delta f\), the same definition as in a resonant circuit.

    07Common mistakes

    • Believing the period of a pendulum depends on the mass. It does not; it depends only on the length.
    • Using the pendulum formula with large amplitudes. It holds as long as \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\).
    • Confusing frequency \(f\) with angular frequency \(\omega\). \(\omega = 2\pi \cdot f\).
    • Assuming that in the forced regime the system oscillates at its natural frequency. It oscillates at that of the excitation; the natural one appears only in the transient.
    • Believing that more damping is always better. Beyond critical, the response becomes slower.
    • Thinking resonance is always a problem. Without resonance there would be no radio.

    08Self-assessment

    A 0.5 kg mass on a 200 N/m spring. What is its natural frequency?

    \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{200/0.5} = 20\text{ rad/s}\), that is \(f_0 = 20/2\pi \approx\) \(3.18\text{ Hz}\).

    What must b be for critical damping in that system?

    \(b = 2\sqrt{m \cdot k} = 2\sqrt{100} =\) \(20\,\mathrm{N \cdot s/m}\).

    How long is a pendulum that takes 2 s for each complete oscillation?

    \(L = g \cdot T^2/(4\pi^2) = 9.8 \cdot 4/39.48 \approx\) \(0.99\text{ m}\). It is the classic seconds pendulum.

    What is the electrical equivalent of mass?

    Inductance: both oppose abrupt changes in their variable —velocity and current— and store energy in the same form, \(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot m \cdot v^2\) and \(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot L \cdot i^2\).

    A system has \(Q = 50\) and \(f_0 = 1\text{ kHz}\). What is its bandwidth?

    \(\Delta f = f_0/Q =\) \(20\text{ Hz}\). The same calculation as for a resonant circuit.

    Why does a quartz crystal define a frequency so well?

    Because its extremely high Q makes the resonance curve very sharp: only an extremely narrow band of frequencies produces appreciable oscillation.
