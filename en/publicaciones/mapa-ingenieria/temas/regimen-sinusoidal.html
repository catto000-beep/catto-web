    Sinusoidal steady state and the s-plane

    The gain of a filter at any frequency can be measured with a ruler on a drawing: it is the distance between a point on the imaginary axis and the pole. The s-plane turns the frequency response into geometry.

    s-plane Poles and zeros Bode Decibels Power factor

    01The complex frequency \( s \)

    The previous topic used \( j\omega \) to turn derivatives into products. That \( j\omega \) is a particular case of something more general: the complex frequency \( s = \sigma + j\omega \). A signal of the form \( e^{st} \) covers, in a single stroke, all the elementary signals of the signals unit:

    Value of \( s \)Signal \( e^{st} \)
    \( s = 0 \)A constant: pure DC
    \( s = \sigma \) real and negativeA decaying exponential: a transient
    \( s = j\omega \), purely imaginaryA sinusoid of constant amplitude: steady state
    \( s = \sigma + j\omega \) with \( \sigma<0 \)A damped sinusoid: an oscillatory transient
    \( \sigma>0 \)Something that grows without bound: an unstable circuit

    Sinusoidal steady state is, then, what happens along a single line of the plane: the imaginary axis. The rest of the \( s \)-plane describes transients, and is studied in the transient response unit.

    02The transfer function, its poles and its zeros

    With impedances written in \( s \) —\( Z_L = sL \), \( Z_C = 1/sC \)— the relationship between the output and the input of a linear circuit is a ratio of polynomials in \( s \): the transfer function \( H(s) \). The roots of the numerator are the zeros; those of the denominator, the poles. They are enough to know the entire circuit, up to a constant.

    \[ H(s) = \frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)} = \frac{1}{1 + sRC} \qquad \text{pole at } \; p = -\frac{1}{RC} \] The RC low-pass filter: a single real pole, on the negative half-axis. Its frequency response is \( H(j\omega) \): evaluating \( H(s) \) by walking along the imaginary axis.

    That evaluation has a geometric reading that is worth internalizing. The magnitude of \( H(j\omega) \) is inversely proportional to the distance between the point \( j\omega \) and the pole: when \( \omega \) is small, the point is close to the origin and the distance to the pole hardly changes; when \( \omega \) exceeds \( |p| \), the distance grows with \( \omega \) and the gain falls. The phase angle is, with its sign changed, the angle of that same vector.

    \[ |H(j\omega)| = \frac{|p|}{|j\omega - p|} \qquad \angle H(j\omega) = -\arg(j\omega - p) \] At \( \omega = |p| \) the vector makes 45° with the real axis: the gain drops to \( 1/\sqrt2 \) (−3 dB) and the phase is −45°. That is the cutoff frequency, \( f_c = 1/(2\pi RC) \).
    Lab · from the s-plane to the Bode plot

    On the left, the \( s \)-plane with the RC filter’s pole and the point \( j\omega \) that runs along the imaginary axis. On the right, the gain in decibels that comes from measuring that vector. You move the frequency and the two views move together.

    03Decibels and Bode plots

    The gain of a circuit varies over many orders of magnitude, and so does the frequency. That is why the frequency response is plotted with both axes logarithmic: the frequency in decades and the gain in decibels, \( 20\log_{10}|H| \). On that scale, each pole contributes a straight line with a slope of −20 dB per decade starting at its cutoff frequency, and each zero, +20 dB per decade.

    \( f/f_c \)\( |H| \)GainPhase
    0.10.995−0.04 dB−5.7°
    10.707−3.01 dB−45°
    100.0995−20.04 dB−84.3°
    1000.0100−40.00 dB−89.4°

    One decade above the cutoff, the filter already attenuates ten times; two decades above, a hundred. That rule of −20 dB per decade per pole is what makes it possible to sketch by hand, in a minute, the response of a circuit with several poles: you add up straight lines.

    04Power in sinusoidal steady state

    With sinusoidal voltage and current out of phase by an angle \( \varphi \), the instantaneous power oscillates, and only part of it is turned into useful work. Three powers organize the matter:

    \[ \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{V}\,\mathbf{I}^* = P + jQ \qquad P = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\varphi \qquad Q = V_{rms}I_{rms}\sin\varphi \qquad pf = \cos\varphi = \frac{P}{|S|} \] \( P \) is the active (real) power, in watts: the one that heats, moves and lights. \( Q \) is the reactive power, in volt-amperes reactive: the one that goes back and forth between the source and the fields of inductors and capacitors without doing net work. \( |S| \), the apparent power, in volt-amperes, is the one that sizes cables and transformers.
    A refrigerator motor

    At 230 V it draws 10 A with a power factor of 0.8 lagging. The apparent power is 2300 VA; the active power, 1840 W; the reactive power, 1380 var. The utility has to carry 10 A even though only 8 A “work”: that is why it charges surcharges to industrial users with a low power factor, and why power-factor-correction capacitors exist, which locally supply the reactive power the motor asks for.

    05Where it shows up in electronics

    CaseWhat is read from the s-plane or from the Bode plot
    Anti-aliasing filter before an A/D converterHow many poles are needed to attenuate enough above half the sampling frequency
    Stability of a feedback amplifierIf any closed-loop pole crosses into the right half-plane, the amplifier oscillates
    Op amp datasheetThe gain-bandwidth product is, literally, the location of its dominant pole
    Switched-mode power supply with power factor correctionThe PFC stage makes the mains current follow the voltage, with a \( pf \) close to 1

    07Common mistakes

    • Confusing \( s \) with \( j\omega \). Sinusoidal steady state is \( s=j\omega \); the rest of the plane describes transients.
    • Using \( 10\log \) for a voltage ratio. For voltages and currents it is \( 20\log \); \( 10\log \) is for powers.
    • Forgetting the sign of a pole’s phase. A pole lags: it contributes negative phase, down to −90°.
    • Adding apparent powers as if they were active. \( S \) is a complex number; its magnitudes do not add unless all the loads have the same power factor.
    • Believing that reactive power is “lost.” It is not dissipated; what it costs is the extra current it makes circulate through the conductors.

    08Self-assessment

    On which part of the \( s \)-plane is the frequency response evaluated?

    On the imaginary axis, \( s = j\omega \).

    Where is the pole of an RC low-pass filter?

    At \( s = -1/RC \), on the negative real half-axis.

    What is the gain at the cutoff frequency?

    \( 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707 \), that is, −3.01 dB, with −45° of phase.

    What slope does each pole contribute on a Bode plot?

    −20 dB per decade above its cutoff frequency.

    What does the power factor measure?

    The fraction of the apparent power that is active: \( \cos\varphi = P/|S| \).

    What does a pole in the right half-plane indicate?

    An unstable circuit: its response grows without bound.
