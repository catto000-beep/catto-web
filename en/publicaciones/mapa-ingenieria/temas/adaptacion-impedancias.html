    Impedance matching

    Matching 5 Ω to 50 Ω with an L network forces a Q of 3, and the match lasts about 23 MHz around 100. The transformation ratio decides the bandwidth.

    Conjugate load L network Quality factor π and T networks Return loss

    01Why match

    An RF power transistor presents a few ohms at its output; an antenna, 50 Ω; a low-noise amplifier delivers its best noise figure with a particular source impedance. Connecting them directly wastes power and, on lines, produces the standing waves of transmission lines. A lossless matching network transforms one impedance into another at one frequency:

    \[ P_{L,\max} = \frac{|V_s|^2}{8 R_s} \quad \text{if} \quad Z_L = Z_s^* \] Maximum power transfer is obtained with the conjugate of the source as the load. In a power amplifier, on the other hand, the load chosen is the one that gives the required power and efficiency, which is not the conjugate: the network transforms the antenna into that load.

    02The L network

    The simplest network has two reactances: one in series on the side of the lower resistance and another in parallel on the side of the higher one. The ratio between the resistances sets its quality factor, and the reactances follow from it:

    \[ Q = \sqrt{\frac{R_{high}}{R_{low}} - 1} \] \[ X_{series} = Q\, R_{low}, \quad X_{parallel} = \frac{R_{high}}{Q} \] With a series inductor and a parallel capacitor, the network is low-pass and also attenuates harmonics; with the components swapped, high-pass. To take 5 Ω to 50 Ω at 100 MHz, Q = 3: 23.9 nH in series and 95.5 pF in parallel.

    The Q is not free: it is set by the transformation ratio, and with it the bandwidth of the match, which is approximately inversely proportional to Q. A large transformation in a single step is narrowband.

    03π and T networks, multiple sections and transformers

    With three elements, the π and T networks make it possible to choose the Q independently of the transformation ratio: the path goes through a virtual intermediate resistance. They serve to narrow the band, for example to filter harmonics at the output of a transmitter. To widen the band the opposite is done: several L networks in cascade with intermediate resistances in geometric progression, each with a lower Q. At higher frequencies, the networks are built with line sections and stubs, and designed on the Smith chart. Broadband transformers, with a ferrite core or a transmission-line type, give fixed ratios over several octaves.

    05Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the parasitics of the components. At hundreds of megahertz a capacitor has inductance and an inductor, capacitance.
    • Expecting broadband from a large transformation in one step. The high Q prevents it; several sections are needed.
    • Matching a power amplifier to the conjugate. It is matched to the load that gives the required power and efficiency.
    • Ignoring inductor losses. With a high network Q, the circulating current dissipates in the winding resistance.

    06Self-assessment

    What Q does an L network that matches 10 Ω to 50 Ω have?

    \( \sqrt{5 - 1} = 2 \).

    What reactances does it need in series and in parallel?

    20 Ω in series on the 10 Ω side and 25 Ω in parallel on the 50 Ω side.

    What advantage does a low-pass L network have at the output of a transmitter?

    Besides matching, it attenuates harmonics.

    How is the bandwidth of a large-ratio match widened?

    With several sections in cascade and intermediate resistances in geometric progression.
