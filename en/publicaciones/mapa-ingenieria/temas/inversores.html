    Inverters

    With the same fundamental, unipolar modulation moves the harmonics to twice the carrier frequency and lowers the distortion from 146 % to 77 %: an inductive load filters them much better.

    H-bridge Sinusoidal PWM Modulation index Overmodulation Multilevel

    01The bridge inverter

    An inverter generates AC voltage from a DC source. The single-phase H-bridge has two legs of two switches; with the load connected diagonally, between the midpoints, it can apply \( +V_{dc} \), \( -V_{dc} \) or zero. The three-phase one has three legs, one per phase. Each switch carries an antiparallel diode, because with an inductive load the current keeps flowing when the switch opens. Between the turn-off of one switch and the turn-on of the other in the same leg, a dead time of a fraction of a microsecond is left so as not to short-circuit the supply.

    Switching once per half-cycle gives a square wave, with the maximum fundamental, \( \frac{4}{\pi} V_{dc} \), but with all the odd harmonics, of amplitude \( 1/h \), and with no way to vary the voltage.

    02Sinusoidal pulse-width modulation

    In sinusoidal PWM a sinusoidal reference at the desired frequency is compared with a much faster triangular carrier. The amplitude modulation index \( m_a \) is the ratio of their amplitudes and the frequency modulation index \( m_f \), the ratio of their frequencies. In the linear region, the fundamental of the output is proportional to \( m_a \):

    \[ \hat{V}_1 = m_a V_{dc}, \quad m_a \le 1 \] In bipolar modulation, the output jumps between \( +V_{dc} \) and \( -V_{dc} \) and the harmonics appear around \( m_f \). In unipolar modulation, each leg is compared separately with the reference and with its opposite; the output passes through zero and the harmonics are grouped around \( 2 m_f \). With \( m_a > 1 \), overmodulation, the fundamental grows less than proportionally and low-order harmonics reappear.

    An H-bridge fed with 400 V that generates 50 Hz. Top, the output voltage over one cycle with the scaled reference (yellow); bottom, the odd harmonics as a percentage of the fundamental, up to a little beyond \( 2 m_f \).

    The voltage distortion looks enormous, but what matters is the current: an inductive load, such as a motor, attenuates each harmonic in proportion to its order, and high-order ones hardly produce any current. That is why a fast carrier is desirable, up to the limit set by the switching losses seen in power devices.

    03Three-phase inverter and other techniques

    In the three-phase inverter, three references shifted by 120° are used. The harmonics that are multiples of three cancel in the line voltages, and with \( m_f \) a multiple of three the ones around the carrier also disappear. Space-vector modulation (SVPWM) chooses in each period the bridge states that synthesize the desired voltage vector, and makes use of 15 % more of the DC bus than sinusoidal modulation. For high voltages, multilevel inverters are used —neutral-point-clamped or cascaded H-bridge— whose output has more steps, fewer harmonics and switches that withstand only a fraction of the voltage.

    05Common mistakes

    • Judging the inverter by the voltage distortion. What heats the motor is the harmonic current.
    • Forgetting the dead time. Without it, the two switches of a leg overlap; with too much, the current is distorted.
    • Expecting linear gain in overmodulation. The fundamental saturates toward that of the square wave.
    • Choosing an even mf. Even harmonics appear and the waveform loses its symmetry.

    06Self-assessment

    What fundamental does a 400 V bridge give with ma = 0.8?

    320 V peak, about 226 V RMS.

    And with a square wave?

    \( \frac{4}{\pi} \cdot 400 \approx 509 \) V peak.

    With mf = 21, where are the first important harmonics in unipolar modulation?

    Around 2 mf: at 41 and 43.

    What are the antiparallel diodes across the switches for?

    To give a path to the inductive load current when the switch opens.
