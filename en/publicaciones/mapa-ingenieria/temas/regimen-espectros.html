    Steady state and spectra

    A filter does not deform a signal in some mysterious way: it changes the proportion among its harmonics. With that single idea, everything learned for sinusoids is useful for any periodic signal.

    Fourier series Spectrum Harmonics Parseval THD

    01Any periodic signal is a sum of sinusoids

    Everything learned about sinusoidal steady state would seem to be useful only for pure sinusoidal signals, which in practice hardly exist. Fourier solved the problem in 1807: every reasonable periodic signal is a sum of sinusoids whose frequencies are integer multiples of the fundamental. Since linear circuits obey superposition, it is enough to solve the circuit once for each of those sinusoids and add the results.

    \[ x(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \cos(n\omega_0 t + \theta_n) \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T} \] \( a_0 \) is the average value; each term \( n \) is a harmonic, with amplitude \( c_n \) and phase \( \theta_n \). The one with \( n=1 \) is the fundamental.

    02The line spectrum

    Plotting the amplitudes \( c_n \) against frequency gives the spectrum of the signal: a series of lines at \( f_0, 2f_0, 3f_0, \ldots \). Each waveform has its signature.

    Waveform, amplitude 1Harmonics presentAmplitude of harmonic \( n \)How they decay
    Symmetric square waveOdd only\( 4/(\pi n) \)Like \( 1/n \): slowly
    Symmetric triangle waveOdd only\( 8/(\pi^2 n^2) \)Like \( 1/n^2 \): quickly
    SawtoothAll\( 2/(\pi n) \)Like \( 1/n \)

    The rule behind this is general: the more abrupt a signal is, the more slowly its harmonics decay. A sudden jump, like that of the square wave, needs very high frequencies to be built; a continuous ramp, like the triangle wave, is put together almost entirely from the first few terms.

    03The response, harmonic by harmonic

    If the input is a sum of sinusoids, the output of a linear circuit is the sum of the responses to each one. Each harmonic comes out multiplied by the magnitude of \( H \) at its own frequency and shifted by its phase.

    \[ y(t) = H(0)\,a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n' \cos(n\omega_0 t + \theta_n') \] \[ c_n' = |H(jn\omega_0)|\,c_n \qquad \theta_n' = \theta_n + \angle H(jn\omega_0) \] A filter does not “deform” the signal in some mysterious way: it changes the proportion among its harmonics. What looks different on the oscilloscope is the sum of those new proportions.

    04RMS value and harmonic distortion

    Since harmonics of different frequencies do not exchange average power with one another, the square of the RMS value of a signal is the sum of the squares of the RMS values of its harmonics: the Parseval identity.

    \[ X_{rms}^2 = a_0^2 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_n^2}{2} \] \[ THD = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_{n\geq2} c_n^2}}{c_1} \] The total harmonic distortion compares the energy of all the harmonics with that of the fundamental. A square wave has a THD of 48.3 %; a triangle wave, 12.1 %; a pure sinusoid, zero.

    05From the line spectrum to the continuous spectrum

    If the period grows without bound, the lines of the spectrum move closer to one another until they form a continuum: the Fourier series becomes the Fourier transform, which describes the frequency content of a signal that does not repeat, such as a single pulse. The power signals of the signals unit have a line spectrum; the energy signals, a continuous spectrum. It is the same idea with two tools, and the Signals and Systems course develops them in detail.

    08Common mistakes

    • Applying harmonic superposition to a nonlinear circuit. A diode or a saturated transistor generates new harmonics; the recipe in this unit holds only for linear circuits.
    • Forgetting that \( H \) changes from harmonic to harmonic. Each one is multiplied by \( |H(jn\omega_0)| \), not by a single value.
    • Adding harmonic amplitudes to obtain the RMS value. What gets added are the squares of the RMS values, not the amplitudes.
    • Expecting even harmonics in a wave with half-wave symmetry. The symmetric square and triangle waves have only odd ones.
    • Confusing a line spectrum with a continuous spectrum. The first belongs to periodic signals; the second, to signals that do not repeat.

    09Self-assessment

    Which harmonics does a symmetric square wave have?

    Only the odd ones, with amplitude \( 4/(\pi n) \).

    Why do the harmonics of the triangle wave decay faster than those of the square wave?

    Because the triangle wave has no jumps: it is continuous, and only its slope changes abruptly. The smoother the signal, the faster its spectrum decays.

    How is the output of a linear circuit calculated for a periodic wave?

    The input is decomposed into harmonics, each one is multiplied by \( H(jn\omega_0) \) and the responses are added.

    What does the Parseval identity say?

    That the square of the RMS value is the sum of the squares of the RMS values of each harmonic component, plus that of the average value.

    What is the THD of a square wave?

    48.3 %.
