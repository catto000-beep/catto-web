    Multistage amplifiers

    Three identical 1 MHz stages do not make a 1 MHz amplifier: the cascade cuts off at 510 kHz. Each stage adds gain and eats up bandwidth.

    Cascade Loading effect Cascode Miller effect Bandwidth

    01Why more than one stage

    A single stage rarely meets everything that is asked of an amplifier: high gain, high input resistance so as not to load the source, and low output resistance to drive the load. Each configuration is good at one thing, so they are combined in cascade, each doing its own job:

    StageFunctionTypical configuration
    InputNot loading the source, rejecting the common mode, low noiseDifferential pair, follower, FET
    GainProviding almost all the voltage amplificationCommon emitter or common source with an active load
    OutputDelivering current to a low-resistance loadEmitter follower, complementary pair

    This is exactly the structure of an integrated operational amplifier: a differential pair, a gain stage and an output stage.

    02The gain of a cascade

    The total gain is the product of the gains of each stage, but measured with the real load that the next one places on it. Between two stages a divider is formed between the output resistance of one and the input resistance of the other:

    \[ A_v = A_1 \frac{R_{in,2}}{R_{out,1} + R_{in,2}}\; A_2 \frac{R_{in,3}}{R_{out,2} + R_{in,3}}\; A_3 \cdots \] In decibels, the gains add and the loading losses subtract.

    The typical mistake is to calculate each stage unloaded and multiply: a stage with a gain of 100 and a 10 kΩ output, loaded by a 10 kΩ input, delivers only 50. A follower inserted between the two solves the problem, at the cost of one more stage.

    03How the stages are connected

    CouplingAdvantageProblem
    CapacitiveEach stage is biased separatelyDC does not pass; each capacitor adds a low-frequency cutoff
    DirectAmplifies down to DC; no large capacitors: it is the only option in an integrated circuitThe DC levels of one stage set those of the next, and drift is amplified
    TransformerMatches impedances and isolatesHeavy, expensive and band-limited: today only in radio frequency and isolation

    04Classic combinations

    • Darlington: two transistors where the emitter of the first feeds the base of the second. They behave like a single one with a β of around \( \beta_1 \beta_2 \), about 10,000, and twice the base-emitter voltage. It is used in output stages and in power switches.
    • Cascode: a common emitter followed by a common base. The common base keeps the collector voltage of the first fixed, so the first has almost no voltage gain and therefore barely suffers from the Miller effect: the collector-base capacitance, which in a common emitter with a gain of 100 is seen multiplied by 101 from the input, is multiplied by barely 2 in the cascode. Result: the same \( g_m R_C \) with much more bandwidth.
    • Common emitter followed by common collector: the gain of the first and the low output resistance of the second.

    05The bandwidth shrinks

    Each stage has its own high-frequency cutoff. When they are cascaded, the cutoffs multiply and the overall response falls off sooner than that of each one. For \( n \) identical stages with a pole at \( f_H \):

    \[ f_{H,\text{total}} = f_H \sqrt{2^{1/n} - 1} \qquad f_{L,\text{total}} = \frac{f_L}{\sqrt{2^{1/n} - 1}} \] With two stages, the bandwidth drops to 64 %; with three, to 51 %; with four, to 43.5 %. The low-frequency cutoff rises in the same proportion.

    That is why it is not a good idea to build an enormous gain with many identical stages: each added stage gives gain but eats up bandwidth. The design balances the number of stages, the gain of each one and its own bandwidth.

    06In the lab

    Activity 1 · Two stages and a follower

    Build two common emitters coupled by a capacitor and measure the total gain. Insert an emitter follower between them and compare with the product of the unloaded gains.

    Activity 2 · Measuring the Miller effect

    Measure the high-frequency cutoff of a common emitter with a 10 kΩ source resistance, and then that of a cascode with the same input transistor and the same load.

    Activity 3 · Bandwidth shrinkage

    With two identical stages, measure the bandwidth of each one and that of the cascade, and compare it with the 64 % predicted by the formula.

    07Common mistakes

    • Multiplying unloaded gains. The loading of each stage on the previous one must be included.
    • Assuming the total bandwidth is that of one stage. With several stages it is smaller.
    • Direct coupling without checking the DC levels. One stage can end up saturated by the voltage of the previous one.
    • Forgetting the Miller effect. A high-gain stage multiplies its collector-base capacitance as seen from the input.

    08Self-assessment

    A stage with a gain of 50 and a 5 kΩ output drives a 20 kΩ input. What does it deliver?

    \( 50 \times 20/25 = 40 \).

    What bandwidth do three identical stages of 1 MHz each have?

    \( 1 \text{ MHz} \times \sqrt{2^{1/3} - 1} \approx 510 \) kHz.

    Why does the cascode have more bandwidth than the common emitter?

    Because the input transistor has almost no voltage gain and its collector-base capacitance is not multiplied by the Miller effect.

    What is the approximate β of a Darlington made of two transistors with β = 80?

    About 6400.
