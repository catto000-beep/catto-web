    Multijunction devices: thyristors and IGBTs

    A microsecond pulse on the gate and the thyristor keeps conducting by itself until the next zero crossing. It is the same mechanism that, when no one wants it, burns out a CMOS circuit in latch-up.

    Thyristor TRIAC Phase control IGBT Latching

    01Four layers, three junctions

    A thyristor (or SCR, silicon-controlled rectifier) is a PNPN structure: a P anode, a wide, lightly doped N layer that withstands the voltage, a P layer that carries the gate terminal, and an N cathode. It has three junctions in series, and with the anode positive the middle one is reverse biased and blocks. So far it looks like a diode that does not conduct in either direction. The trick is that, once it starts to conduct, it keeps conducting by itself, even if the gate signal is removed.

    It is a power device: there are ones of a few amperes for controlling a lamp and ones of several kilovolts and thousands of amperes, made on an entire silicon wafer, for high-voltage direct-current transmission stations.

    02The two-transistor model

    The PNPN structure can be cut into two interlocked transistors: a PNP formed by the first three layers and an NPN by the last three. The collector of each one feeds the base of the other. With common-base gains \( \alpha_1 \) and \( \alpha_2 \), the anode current becomes

    \[ I_A = \frac{\alpha_2\, I_G + I_{CBO1} + I_{CBO2}}{1 - (\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)} \] The \( \alpha \) of the bipolar transistors grow with the current. When their sum reaches 1, the denominator vanishes and the current is limited only by the external circuit.

    That is positive feedback: a gate pulse makes the NPN conduct, its collector feeds the base of the PNP, the PNP returns more base current to the NPN, and within microseconds both are saturated. From that moment the gate no longer has control. The thyristor turns off by itself only when the anode current drops below the holding current \( I_H \), which in alternating current happens by itself at every zero crossing.

    03Phase control

    On an AC mains, firing the thyristor with a delay \( \alpha \) relative to the zero crossing lets through only the final part of each half-cycle. By varying the angle, the power delivered to the load is regulated while dissipating almost nothing in the control. With a resistive load and full-wave control:

    \[ V_{rms} = V_m \sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi} \left( \pi - \alpha + \frac{\sin 2\alpha}{2} \right)} \] \[ \frac{P}{P_{\max}} = \frac{1}{\pi} \left( \pi - \alpha + \frac{\sin 2\alpha}{2} \right) \] With a single thyristor, which conducts in one half-cycle, the power is halved and a DC component appears, \( \dfrac{V_m}{2\pi} (1 + \cos\alpha) \).

    The same four layers appear unintentionally in CMOS circuits: the latch-up of the CMOS inverter is a parasitic thyristor that triggers and short-circuits the supply, and the two-transistor model explains why it is avoided by placing substrate contacts close to every transistor: they lower the parasitic gains.

    05The IGBT

    The insulated-gate bipolar transistor (IGBT) combines a MOSFET at the input with a PNP bipolar at the output: the gate is driven by voltage, with almost no current, like a MOS, and the main current is conducted by a bipolar structure. It also has four layers, but it is designed so that the parasitic thyristor never triggers.

    Its advantage is conductivity modulation: when conducting, the P collector layer injects holes into the lightly doped N region that withstands the voltage and fills it with carriers, greatly lowering its resistance. A 1200 V MOSFET has to have that lightly doped region thick and it conducts poorly; the IGBT, with the same region flooded with carriers, drops only about 1.5 to 2 V. The cost is the tail current at turn-off, while those carriers recombine, which limits the frequency to tens of kilohertz.

    06In the lab

    Mains voltage

    Activities with 220 V are carried out with an isolation transformer, with the circuit mounted in an enclosure and under the supervision of the instructor. The oscilloscope is not connected directly to the mains: differential probes are used.

    Activity 1 · Latching

    With a thyristor, a 12 V DC source and a 12 V lamp, trigger it with a pushbutton on the gate and verify that the lamp stays on when the button is released. Lower the voltage until it goes off and estimate the holding current.

    Activity 2 · The two-transistor model

    Build a thyristor with a BC548 and a BC558 connected according to the model, and verify that it latches just like the integrated device.

    Activity 3 · A dimmer

    With a commercial TRIAC and DIAC dimmer powered from a low-voltage isolation transformer, use the oscilloscope to observe the voltage across the load and measure the firing angle for three potentiometer positions.

    07Common mistakes

    • Expecting the thyristor to turn off when the gate is removed. It stays latched until the current drops below \( I_H \).
    • Using an ordinary thyristor in direct current without planning how to turn it off. In DC the current never passes through zero.
    • Forgetting the snubber network. Without an RC in parallel, the \( dv/dt \) of the mains or of the inductive load triggers the device unintentionally.
    • Measuring with the oscilloscope referenced to earth on the mains. The probe ground short-circuits the circuit.
    • Confusing the IGBT with a large MOSFET. It conducts with a nearly fixed drop, not with a resistance, and it has tail current at turn-off.

    08Self-assessment

    Why does the thyristor keep conducting without gate current?

    Because each of the two transistors of the model supplies base current to the other; with \( \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 1 \) the feedback sustains itself.

    How is a thyristor turned off in an AC circuit?

    By itself: at every zero crossing the current drops below the holding current.

    With a TRIAC and \( \alpha = 90° \), what fraction of the maximum power does a resistive load receive?

    Half: \( (\pi - \pi/2 + 0)/\pi = 0.5 \).

    What is conductivity modulation in the IGBT?

    The flooding of the drift region with carriers injected by the P collector, which lowers its resistance when conducting.

    What is the relationship between the thyristor and latch-up?

    Latch-up is the triggering of a parasitic thyristor formed by the P and N regions of a CMOS circuit.
