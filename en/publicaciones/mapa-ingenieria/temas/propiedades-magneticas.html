    Magnetic properties of matter

    The area of the hysteresis loop is energy lost on every cycle. Multiplied by the frequency, it gives the watts that heat the core, and from that number comes the choice of material for every transformer.

    Permeability Domains Hysteresis Saturation Curie

    01Three responses to the field

    Every material responds to a magnetic field, even though in almost all of them the response is imperceptible. The quantity that measures it is the relative permeability, and according to its value materials split into three very unequal groups.

    \[ \vec{B} = \mu_0\mu_r\vec{H} \qquad\qquad \mu_r = 1 + \chi_m \] \( H \) is the magnetizing field (the excitation), the one produced by the currents you control; \( B \) is the resulting flux density, which includes what the material contributes. In vacuum \( \mu_r = 1 \) exactly.
    Type\( \mu_r \)WhyExamples
    DiamagneticBarely less than 1 The field induces opposing moments: it repelsCopper, water, graphite, bismuth
    ParamagneticBarely greater than 1 Permanent moments that partly alignAluminum, platinum, liquid oxygen
    FerromagneticFrom 100 to 100,000 Coupled moments that align as a blockIron, nickel, cobalt, ferrites

    The difference between the first two rows and the third is five orders of magnitude, and that is why in practice there are only two categories: magnetic materials and all the others. An aluminum shield is useless against a static field for exactly that reason: its permeability is practically that of vacuum.

    Domains

    In a ferromagnet the moments are not disordered but grouped into domains: regions a few microns across where all of them point the same way. A demagnetized piece has its domains oriented at random and the net field is zero. When \( H \) is applied, the favorably oriented domains grow at the expense of the others by moving their walls, and that motion is not reversible: the walls get caught on impurities and grain boundaries. Everything in the next section comes from that irreversibility.

    02The hysteresis loop

    Since the motion of the domain walls costs energy and does not undo itself, \( B \) does not depend only on \( H \): it also depends on how you got there. Going through a complete cycle of excitation, the curve encloses a loop.

    ParameterWhat it isWhat it determines
    \( B_{sat} \)Saturation flux densityThe limit of the core: beyond it, it contributes nothing
    \( B_r \)Remanence: what is left at \( H = 0 \)Whether the material works as a permanent magnet
    \( H_c \)Coercivity: the \( H \) that demagnetizes itWhether it is soft or hard
    Loop areaEnergy per unit volume per cycleThe hysteresis losses

    The last row is the key for electronics: the area of the loop is energy lost in every cycle, and multiplied by the frequency it gives dissipated power. That is why transformer cores are made of narrow-loop materials —magnetically soft— and permanent magnets of wide-loop ones.

    03Soft and hard

    Magnetically softMagnetically hard
    \( H_c \)Less than 1000 A/mMore than 10,000 A/m
    LoopNarrow, low lossesWide: that is what is wanted
    What forCores of transformers and inductorsPermanent magnets, motors, loudspeakers
    ExamplesSilicon steel, ferrite, mu-metalAlnico, hard ferrite, neodymium

    The Curie temperature marks the end of all this: above it, thermal agitation overcomes the coupling between moments, the domains disappear and the material becomes a plain paramagnet. It is 770 °C for iron, 358 for nickel and barely 310 for a common neodymium magnet, which is why a permanent-magnet motor has a strict thermal limit: past a certain point the magnets degrade and the motor is never the same again.

    Why switching power supplies use ferrite

    At 50 Hz the hysteresis losses of silicon steel are tolerable and its saturation of 2 T makes it compact. But those losses grow with frequency, and at 100 kHz they would be two thousand times greater. Ferrite has a much narrower loop and, above all, is a ceramic that barely conducts, so it has no eddy currents either. It pays for this with a saturation of only 0.4 T —five times lower—, but at that frequency the core needs much less flux and it does not matter.

    04In the lab

    Activity 1 · Tracing the loop on the oscilloscope

    With a transformer whose core is accessible, send the drop across a series resistor to the X axis —proportional to \( H \)— and the output of an RC integrator on the secondary to the Y axis —proportional to \( B \)—. View the loop in XY mode and measure \( B_r \) and \( H_c \).

    Activity 2 · Saturating a core

    Increase the voltage applied to the primary and observe the current with the oscilloscope. Identify the moment when it stops being sinusoidal and spikes appear: that is saturation. Explain why the current shoots up.

    Activity 3 · Curie with a magnet

    Heat a nail held by a magnet with a torch until it drops off. Estimate the temperature from the color of the metal and compare it with the 770 °C of iron. Verify that when it cools it is attracted again.

    05Common mistakes

    • Confusing \( B \) with \( H \). One is the resulting flux density; the other, the excitation that you apply.
    • Believing that \( \mu_r \) is a constant. It depends on \( H \), on history and on temperature.
    • Shielding a static field with aluminum or copper. They are diamagnetic: \( \mu_r \approx 1 \) and they do not divert anything.
    • Ignoring saturation when designing an inductor. Beyond \( B_{sat} \) the inductance collapses and the current shoots up.
    • Using silicon steel at high frequency. The losses grow with frequency; ferrite goes there.
    • Forgetting the Curie temperature of a magnet. Neodymium degrades well before iron does.

    06Self-assessment

    What does the area of the hysteresis loop represent?

    The energy lost per unit volume in each cycle. Multiplied by the frequency it gives the dissipated power.

    What distinguishes a magnetically soft material?

    Its low coercivity and narrow loop: it demagnetizes easily and loses little energy per cycle.

    Why is aluminum no use as a magnetic shield?

    Because it is paramagnetic with \( \mu_r \) almost 1: it does not divert the field lines.

    What happens above the Curie temperature?

    Thermal agitation overcomes the coupling, the domains disappear and the material becomes paramagnetic.

    Why do switching power supplies use ferrite and not sheet steel?

    Because of the losses: ferrite has a narrow loop and barely conducts, so it has no eddy currents either.

    What happens to an inductor that saturates?

    Its inductance collapses and the current shoots up, because the core can no longer contribute more flux.
