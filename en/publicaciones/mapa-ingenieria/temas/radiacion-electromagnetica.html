    Electromagnetic radiation

    A car's one-meter antenna has, in the AM band, a radiation resistance of thousandths of an ohm. A half-wave dipole has 73 Ω and connects straight to a coaxial cable.

    Elementary dipole Radiation resistance Directivity Radiation pattern Near field

    01Why a conductor radiates

    A charge at rest creates an electric field; a charge in uniform motion also creates a magnetic one. But only an accelerated charge sheds fields that move away on their own and carry energy off to infinity: that is radiation. In a conductor carrying alternating current the electrons accelerate and brake all the time, so any conductor with a varying current radiates something.

    A transmission line hardly radiates because its two conductors carry opposite currents very close to each other, and their far fields cancel. If the ends of a two-wire line are opened out into a T shape, the two currents end up in the same direction, no longer cancel and the structure radiates: it is a dipole, the most elementary antenna.

    02The elementary dipole

    The starting point of the calculation is a segment of current \( I \) of length \( \ell \) much smaller than the wavelength. Solving Maxwell's equations with retarded potentials, far from the dipole we get:

    \[ E_\theta = j\,\frac{\eta\, k\, I \ell}{4\pi r}\, \sin\theta \; e^{-jkr} \] \[ H_\phi = \frac{E_\theta}{\eta} \] \( \theta \) is the angle measured from the axis of the dipole and \( k = 2\pi/\lambda \). The far field decreases as \( 1/r \), is transverse and behaves locally like a plane wave, with \( E/H = \eta = 377\ \Omega \).

    The fact that the field falls as \( 1/r \) is what makes communication possible: the power density falls as \( 1/r^2 \), exactly what is needed for the total power that crosses a sphere of any radius to be the same. The factor \( \sin\theta \) says that the dipole does not radiate along its axis and that it radiates more in the perpendicular plane.

    03Radiation resistance

    The radiated power leaves the source just as if it were dissipated in a resistor. That equivalent resistance, the radiation resistance, is defined by \( P = \tfrac12 I^2 R_r \):

    \[ R_r = 80\pi^2 \left(\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2 \] \[ R_r = 20\pi^2 \left(\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2 \] The first is that of the elementary dipole, with uniform current. The second is that of a real short dipole: the current goes to zero at the tips and falls off linearly, and the resistance is four times smaller.

    A short dipole has a tiny radiation resistance: one of \( \lambda/10 \) has 2 Ω, and the one-meter antenna of a car in the AM band, a few thousandths of an ohm. Against that, the ohmic resistance of the conductor is no longer negligible, and the efficiency \( R_r / (R_r + R_p) \) drops. The half-wave dipole has a radiation resistance of 73 Ω, and that is why it is the reference for practical antennas: it matches effortlessly to a 50 or 75 Ω cable. Its impedance also has a reactive part of about \( +j42 \) Ω, which is canceled by shortening it a little.

    04Radiation pattern and directivity

    The radiation pattern is the far field as a function of direction. The directivity compares the intensity in the direction of maximum radiation with what an isotropic source radiating the same power in all directions would have:

    \[ D = \frac{4\pi\, U_{max}}{P_{rad}} \] \( U \) is the radiation intensity, in watts per steradian. The short dipole has \( D = 1.5 \) (1.76 dBi); the half-wave one, 1.64 (2.15 dBi). Decibels referred to the isotropic source are written dBi.

    A longer dipole concentrates the radiation more in the perpendicular plane, up to 1.25 λ, where the directivity reaches 5.2 dBi. Longer still, the current reverses direction in parts of the conductor and the pattern opens up into several lobes.

    05Near field and far field

    Close to the antenna, besides the field that moves away, there are fields that store energy and return it on each cycle, as in a capacitor or an inductor. They decrease as \( 1/r^2 \) and \( 1/r^3 \), and dominate up to a distance of about \( \lambda / 2\pi \): 48 m for a 1 MHz AM station, 5 cm at 1 GHz. In that region neither the relation \( E/H = 377\ \Omega \) nor the radiation pattern holds.

    For antennas that are large compared with the wavelength, the pattern only forms at the Fraunhofer distance, \( 2D^2/\lambda \), with \( D \) the largest dimension of the antenna: a 1 m parabolic dish at 10 GHz needs 67 m. That is why antennas are measured on long test ranges or in anechoic chambers with near-field techniques.

    Some systems work in the near field on purpose: contactless cards and NFC, at 13.56 MHz (wavelength of 22 m), communicate through inductive coupling over a few centimeters, radiating almost nothing.

    08Common mistakes

    • Measuring the pattern in the near field. Within a few wavelengths, the field does not have the shape of the pattern.
    • Confusing radiation resistance with losses. The power in \( R_r \) is the power that is radiated: it is the useful one.
    • Ignoring the efficiency of a short antenna. With 0.02 Ω of radiation resistance, a few hundredths of an ohm of loss in the conductor or in the loading coil take most of the power.
    • Believing that a short cable does not radiate. At the harmonics of a clock of tens of MHz, a cable one meter long is an efficient antenna.

    09Self-assessment

    What radiation resistance does a short dipole of \( \lambda/10 \) have?

    \( 20\pi^2 \cdot 0.01 \approx 2 \) Ω.

    In what direction does a dipole not radiate?

    Along its axis, where \( \sin\theta = 0 \).

    Up to what distance does the near field of a 100 MHz antenna dominate?

    Up to about \( \lambda/2\pi = 3 / 2\pi \approx 0.48 \) m.

    How much more directive is a half-wave dipole than a short one?

    1.64 versus 1.5: barely 0.39 dB.
