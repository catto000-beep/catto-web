    Wave equations and plane waves

    Seawater reflects almost all of a long-wave radio signal and swallows within a few meters whatever gets in. That is why a submerged submarine can only be reached on very low frequencies.

    Propagation constant Intrinsic impedance Attenuation Reflection Standing wave

    01From Maxwell to the wave equation

    Combining the two curl equations of the previous unit in a source-free region, the electric field is governed by a single equation, the Helmholtz equation:

    \[ \nabla^2 \mathbf{E} - \gamma^2 \mathbf{E} = 0 \] \[ \gamma = \alpha + j\beta = \sqrt{j\omega\mu\,(\sigma + j\omega\varepsilon)} \] \( \gamma \) is the propagation constant: its real part \( \alpha \) is the attenuation, in nepers per meter, and its imaginary part \( \beta \), the phase constant, in radians per meter.

    The simplest solution is the plane wave: a uniform field in every plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation, advancing as \( e^{-\gamma z} = e^{-\alpha z} e^{-j\beta z} \). It is the model for the wave from a distant antenna: many wavelengths away from the source, any spherical wave looks locally flat.

    02The wave in a lossless medium

    With \( \sigma = 0 \), the wave is not attenuated and its parameters come directly from \( \mu \) and \( \varepsilon \):

    \[ \beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon} \] \[ v_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}} \] \[ \eta = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon}} = \frac{377\ \Omega}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}} \] \( \eta \) is the intrinsic impedance of the medium: the ratio between the electric and the magnetic field of the wave. In vacuum it is 376.73 Ω.

    E and H are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation, and are in phase. The power density is \( |E|^2 / 2\eta \): with an amplitude of 1 V/m in air, 1.33 mW per square meter. The direction in which the electric field oscillates is the polarization of the wave.

    03The wave in a lossy medium

    With conductivity, \( \gamma \) and \( \eta \) become complex. The two limiting cases are the useful ones:

    Low-loss dielectric, \( \sigma \ll \omega\varepsilon \)Good conductor, \( \sigma \gg \omega\varepsilon \)
    Attenuation\( \alpha \approx \dfrac{\sigma}{2}\sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon} \)\( \alpha \approx \beta \approx 1/\delta \)
    ImpedanceAlmost real, \( \approx \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon} \)\( (1 + j)/\sigma\delta \), very small, at 45°
    WavelengthThat of the lossless dielectric\( 2\pi\delta \): the wave vanishes within one wavelength

    Attenuation is expressed in decibels by multiplying by 8.686: one neper is 8.686 dB. In seawater at 1 kHz, \( \alpha = 0.126 \) Np/m, about 1.1 dB per meter; at 1 GHz, 673 dB per meter.

    04Reflection at normal incidence

    When a wave arrives perpendicular to the boundary between two media, part of it is reflected and part is transmitted. The boundary conditions give the coefficients for the electric field:

    \[ \Gamma = \frac{\eta_2 - \eta_1}{\eta_2 + \eta_1} \] \[ \tau = 1 + \Gamma \] The fraction of power reflected is \( |\Gamma|^2 \). From air to glass (\( \varepsilon_r = 2.25 \)), \( \Gamma = -0.2 \) and 4% is reflected; from air to water, 64%; from air to a metal, practically all of it.

    The incident and the reflected waves superpose and form a standing wave: fixed maxima and minima separated by half a wavelength. It is exactly the phenomenon that appears in badly terminated transmission lines, with the load impedance in the role of \( \eta_2 \).

    Lab · a plane wave entering a medium

    A plane wave travels through air and strikes a medium perpendicularly. The attenuation, wavelength, impedance and reflection are calculated with the exact complex expressions, and the electric field inside the medium is plotted as a function of depth.

    05Where it appears in electronics

    CaseThe phenomenon
    Radome of an antennaA thickness of half a wavelength in the material so that the reflection cancels
    Ground-penetrating radarThe attenuation of the ground limits the depth; low frequencies are used to see deeper
    Microwave ovenThe water in food absorbs at 2.45 GHz and heats up
    Radio reception inside a buildingReflection and attenuation by walls and glass
    Absorbing materials of an anechoic chamberAn impedance that grows gradually up to \( \eta_0 \) so as not to reflect

    06In the lab

    Activity 1 · Standing waves in air

    With a 10 GHz transmitter aimed at a metal plate and a detector that moves between them, locate the minima of the standing wave and measure the wavelength.

    Activity 2 · Attenuation by water

    Measure the signal of a 2.4 GHz link with water containers of different thickness between the antennas and estimate the attenuation per centimeter.

    Activity 3 · The phone in the cage

    Wrap a phone in aluminum foil and check that it loses signal. Then try with a mesh with large holes and relate the result to the wavelength.

    07Common mistakes

    • Using \( c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r} \) in a medium with large losses. The velocity and the wavelength change with the conductivity.
    • Confusing nepers with decibels. \( 1\ \text{Np} = 8.686\ \text{dB} \).
    • Forgetting that \( \Gamma \) is for the field. The reflected power is \( |\Gamma|^2 \).
    • Assuming that a good conductor absorbs the wave. Almost all of it is reflected; the little that enters is attenuated within a few \( \delta \).

    08Self-assessment

    What intrinsic impedance does a lossless dielectric with \( \varepsilon_r = 4 \) have?

    \( 377/2 \approx 188 \) Ω.

    What fraction of the power is reflected from air to a 188 Ω medium?

    \( \Gamma = (188 - 377)/(188 + 377) \approx -0.33 \); 11% is reflected.

    How many decibels is 0.5 Np?

    \( 0.5 \times 8.686 \approx 4.3 \) dB.

    Why does ground-penetrating radar use low frequencies?

    Because the attenuation of the ground grows with frequency, and at low frequencies the wave reaches deeper.
