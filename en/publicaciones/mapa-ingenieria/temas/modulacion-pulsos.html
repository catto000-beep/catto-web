    Pulse modulation and PCM

    With 8 bits and μ-law, a voice 40 dB below the maximum sounds as good as with a 12-bit uniform converter. Digital telephony saves four bits per sample with a logarithmic curve.

    PAM, PWM and PPM PCM Quantization noise μ-law and A-law TDM and E1

    01Modulating a pulse train

    If the message is sampled at a frequency greater than twice its bandwidth, it is enough to transmit the samples. The carrier becomes a pulse train, and the message can change its amplitude, its duration or its position:

    ModulationWhat the sample carriesUse
    PAM (amplitude)The height of the pulseIntermediate step in every converter; basis of PCM
    PWM (width)The width of the pulseMotor control, class D amplifiers, switching power supplies
    PPM (position)The delay of the pulse relative to a clockRadio control, low-power optical communications
    PCM (code)A binary number with the quantized valueDigital telephony, audio, almost all present-day transmission

    PAM, PWM and PPM remain analog: the noise added to the height, width or position of the pulse reaches the message. In PCM, on the other hand, each sample is rounded to one of \( 2^n \) levels and transmitted as \( n \) bits. The receiver only has to decide whether a 1 or a 0 arrived, and a repeater can regenerate the signal without accumulating noise. The introduction to the equipment is in sampling theorem, in the technician program.

    02PCM: bit rate and quantization noise

    A PCM system samples at \( f_s \), quantizes with \( n \) bits and transmits the bit sequence. The bit rate and the minimum bandwidth, with Nyquist pulses, are:

    \[ R_b = n\, f_s \] \[ B_{\min} = \frac{R_b}{2} \] Telephony samples voice at 8 kHz with 8 bits: 64 kbit/s per channel. CD audio, at 44.1 kHz with 16 bits and two channels, needs 1.41 Mbit/s.

    Rounding adds an error that, with a uniform quantizer of step \( \Delta \), is spread between \( \pm\Delta/2 \) and has power \( \Delta^2/12 \). With a sine wave that fills the whole range, the quantization signal-to-noise ratio is:

    \[ \mathrm{SNR_q} = 6.02\, n + 1.76 \ \mathrm{dB} \] Each bit adds about 6 dB. The formula holds only at full scale: with a signal 20 dB smaller, the quantization error is the same and the ratio drops by 20 dB. The details of the quantizer and the converter are in data converters.

    03Companding: \( \mu \)-law and A-law

    Voice has a dynamic range of about 40 dB: a person speaking softly produces signals a hundred times smaller than one who shouts. With a uniform quantizer, weak signals end up with very few levels. The solution is to compress the signal before quantizing, with a logarithmic curve that expands small values, and to expand it at the receiver. The G.711 standard uses \( \mu \)-law in North America and Japan:

    \[ c(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x)\, \frac{\ln(1 + \mu |x|)}{\ln(1 + \mu)} \] The input is normalized to \( |x| \le 1 \) and the standard uses \( \mu = 255 \). In Europe and Argentina, A-law is used, with \( A = 87.6 \), which is very similar. In both cases the quantization step grows with amplitude and the error is approximately proportional to the signal.

    The result is a signal-to-noise ratio that is nearly constant over a wide range of levels, in exchange for losing about 12 dB at full scale. With 8 bits, \( \mu \)-law gives a −40 dB signal the same quality as a 12-bit uniform quantizer.

    04Time-division multiplexing

    Between two samples of one channel there is free time, which is used to transmit the samples of other channels: this is time-division multiplexing (TDM). A frame contains one sample of each channel and repeats at the sampling frequency. The European hierarchy, which Argentina uses, begins with the E1 system:

    \[ R_{E1} = 32 \times 8\ \mathrm{bit} \times 8\ \mathrm{kHz} = 2.048\ \mathrm{Mbit/s} \] Of the 32 time slots, 30 carry voice; one carries frame synchronization and another signaling. The North American T1 system groups 24 channels with one framing bit: 1.544 Mbit/s.

    Other variants reduce the bit rate at the cost of complexity: differential PCM (DPCM) quantizes the difference between the sample and a prediction, and delta modulation uses a single bit per sample, at a much higher rate. Present-day speech coders transmit parameters of a vocal-tract model and get down to a few kbit/s.

    05In the lab

    Activity 1 · Quantization noise

    Digitize a sine wave with a microcontroller, reducing the number of bits in software, and measure the signal-to-noise ratio with the FFT for each resolution. Verify the 6 dB per bit.

    Activity 2 · Companding

    Implement \( \mu \)-law on the microcontroller and repeat the measurement for different input levels. Compare with the uniform quantizer of the same number of bits.

    Activity 3 · PWM

    Generate a PWM modulated by a tone and recover it with an RC lowpass filter. Observe the residue of the switching frequency.

    06Common mistakes

    • Applying \( 6.02n + 1.76\text{ dB} \) to any signal. It holds for a full-scale sine wave; with smaller signals the ratio drops dB for dB.
    • Forgetting the anti-aliasing filter. Whatever is above \( f_s/2 \) folds over the useful band and cannot be separated.
    • Believing that companding improves everything. It evens out the quality across levels, but at full scale it is worse than uniform.
    • Confusing bit rate with bandwidth. The bandwidth depends on the pulse shape: at minimum, half the bit rate.

    07Self-assessment

    What quantization signal-to-noise ratio does a 12-bit converter give with a full-scale sine wave?

    \( 6.02 \cdot 12 + 1.76 \approx 74 \) dB.

    What bit rate does an audio channel sampled at 48 kHz with 24 bits need?

    1.152 Mbit/s.

    How many voice channels does an E1 carry and what is its bit rate?

    30 voice channels at 2.048 Mbit/s.

    With an 8-bit uniform quantizer, what signal-to-noise ratio does a sine wave 30 dB below full scale have?

    About \( 50 - 30 = 20\text{ dB} \).
