    Attenuators and compensators

    A 10 dB attenuator for 50 Ω needs 25.97 and 35.14 Ω. With the nearest commercial resistors it gives 10.07 dB: the rounding hardly matters, but you have to calculate it.

    01Lowering the level without causing a mismatch

    An attenuator reduces the amplitude of a signal by a fixed number of decibels, and also presents the correct impedance on both sides. A simple resistive divider attenuates, but it changes the impedance seen by the generator and the load: in a 50 Ω system that produces reflections, as seen in transmission lines, and the real attenuation is no longer the calculated one. They are used to protect the input of an instrument, to lower the level of a generator below its minimum, to improve the match between two pieces of equipment and to measure gains by comparison.

    02T and π attenuators

    A symmetric attenuator designed for an impedance \( Z_0 \) has an image impedance of \( Z_0 \) on both sides: terminated in \( Z_0 \), it presents \( Z_0 \) at the input. With \( K = 10^{A/20} \), the voltage ratio corresponding to an attenuation of \( A \) decibels:

    \[ R_{serie} = Z_0\,\frac{K - 1}{K + 1} \] \[ R_{der} = Z_0\,\frac{2K}{K^2 - 1} \] For the T attenuator: two equal series resistors and one shunt resistor in the middle.
    \[ R_{der} = Z_0\,\frac{K + 1}{K - 1} \] \[ R_{serie} = Z_0\,\frac{K^2 - 1}{2K} \] For the π attenuator: two equal shunt resistors at the ends and one series resistor between them.

    The T is convenient for small attenuations, where the shunt resistor is large; the π, for large ones. Both are symmetric two-ports and, as seen in two-port networks, in cascade they add their decibels only if each one is properly terminated. The exact values almost never exist as commercial resistors, and rounding them changes the attenuation and the match slightly.

    You choose the topology, the impedance and the attenuation. The exact values are calculated, rounded to the nearest E24 (5 %) or E96 (1 %) series value and the real attenuator is recalculated between a generator and a load of \( Z_0 \). The schematic shows the rounded values.

    04Compensators

    A compensator is a network that corrects the frequency response or the phase of another block. The simplest case was already seen in the oscilloscope: the compensated attenuator, with a capacitor in parallel with each resistor so that the divider is the same at all frequencies. In control, phase lead and lag networks are used:

    \[ H(s) = \alpha\,\frac{1 + s R_1 C_1}{1 + s\,\alpha R_1 C_1} \] \[ \sin\varphi_m = \frac{1 - \alpha}{1 + \alpha} \] The lead network: \( R_1 \) in parallel with \( C_1 \), in series, and \( R_2 \) to ground, with \( \alpha = R_2/(R_1 + R_2) \). Between the zero and the pole it advances the phase, with a maximum \( \varphi_m \) at the geometric mean of the two frequencies. With \( \alpha = 0.1 \), the maximum phase lead is 55°.

    Cable equalizers are compensators of the same kind: a long cable attenuates high frequencies more, and a network with the inverse response restores them. Every high-speed data receiver has one, fixed or adaptive.

    06Common mistakes

    • Using a simple divider where an attenuator is needed. It changes the impedance and reflections appear.
    • Forgetting the power rating of the resistors. The first resistor of an attenuator connected to a transmitter dissipates almost all the power.
    • Terminating the attenuator in a different impedance. The attenuation and the match are no longer the design values.
    • Ignoring parasitic inductances. At hundreds of megahertz, surface-mount resistors and short traces are essential.

    07Self-assessment

    What resistors does a 6 dB T for 50 Ω have?

    With \( K = 2 \): series \( 50 \cdot 1/3 \approx 16.7\ \Omega \) and shunt \( 50 \cdot 4/3 \approx 66.7\ \Omega \).

    How much power does a 10 dB attenuator receiving 10 W dissipate?

    1 W comes out: it dissipates 9 W.

    What maximum phase lead does a network with \( \alpha = 0.25 \) give?

    \( \arcsin(0.75/1.25) = \arcsin 0.6 \approx 37° \).

    Why do two badly terminated 3 dB attenuators not give 6 dB?

    Because the impedance each one sees is no longer the design one, and their attenuation and match change.
