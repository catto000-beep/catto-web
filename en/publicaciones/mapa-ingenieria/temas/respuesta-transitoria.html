    Transient response and residues

    Before doing a single calculation, the location of the poles already says whether a circuit will oscillate, how long it will take to settle and whether it will overshoot. The residues only supply the numbers.

    Laplace Residues Poles Damping Overshoot

    01The whole circuit, transformed into \( s \)

    Steady state was solved with phasors. The transient —what happens from the moment a switch closes until the circuit settles— is solved with the Laplace transform, which does for derivatives the same thing that phasors do, but without requiring the signal to be sinusoidal or a long time to have gone by. It also incorporates, in a natural way, the initial conditions: the charge a capacitor already had or the current that was already flowing through an inductor.

    ElementIn timeIn \( s \), with initial condition
    Resistor\( v = Ri \)\( V = RI \)
    Inductor\( v = L\,di/dt \)Impedance \( sL \) in series with a source \( L\,i(0) \)
    Capacitor\( i = C\,dv/dt \)Impedance \( 1/sC \) in series with a source \( v(0)/s \)

    With that substitution, the circuit is solved with the same rules as always —mesh analysis, nodal analysis, dividers— and the response comes out as a ratio of polynomials in \( s \).

    02Residues: from \( Y(s) \) back to time

    To go back to time, the ratio is decomposed into partial fractions, one per pole. The coefficient of each fraction is the residue of that pole, and it is calculated without solving any system of equations:

    \[ Y(s) = \sum_k \frac{K_k}{s - p_k} \qquad y(t) = \sum_k K_k\, e^{p_k t} \] \[ K_k = \lim_{s\to p_k} (s-p_k)\,Y(s) \] Each pole contributes an exponential \( e^{p_k t} \) and its residue says with what weight. A pair of complex conjugate poles gives conjugate residues, and together they build a real damped sinusoid.

    The consequence is powerful: the shape of the transient is decided by the location of the poles, before doing any calculation. The residues only adjust the amplitudes.

    PolesContribute
    Negative realDecaying exponentials, without oscillating
    Complex conjugates with negative real partAn oscillation that dies out: the “ringing”
    On the imaginary axisAn oscillation that never dies out
    With positive real partSomething that grows without bound: instability

    03The series RLC with a step input

    The case that sums everything up: a series RLC connected to a DC source \( V \). The capacitor voltage in \( s \) has three poles: one at the origin, which gives the final value, and two that depend on the components.

    \[ V_C(s) = \frac{V\,\omega_n^2}{s\,(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n s + \omega_n^2)} \] \[ \omega_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \qquad \zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} \] \( \omega_n \) is the natural frequency and \( \zeta \) the damping ratio. With \( \zeta<1 \) the poles are complex and the voltage oscillates before settling; with \( \zeta=1 \) they merge into a double pole, the fastest response without overshoot; with \( \zeta>1 \) they separate along the real axis.
    Lab · the poles decide the shape

    Series RLC with \( L = 1 \) mH and \( C = 100 \) nF, connected to 5 V. You move \( R \): on the left, the two poles in the \( s \)-plane; on the right, the capacitor voltage that results from adding up their residues.

    \[ M_p = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \] The overshoot, as a fraction of the final value, for \( \zeta<1 \). With \( \zeta = 0.5 \) it is 16.3 %; with \( \zeta = 0.707 \), 4.3 %, a widely used compromise between speed and overshoot.

    04Where it shows up in electronics

    CaseThe second-order transient
    Poorly compensated oscilloscope probeThe “ringing” on the edges of a square wave is the inductance of the ground lead resonating with the input capacitance
    Fast data lines on a circuit boardThe oscillation after each edge can cross the logic threshold and produce a false bit
    Input LC filter of a switched-mode power supplyWhen it is plugged in, the capacitor can overshoot the input voltage if the filter is underdamped
    Feedback amplifierIts step response reveals, through the overshoot, how close it is to oscillating

    05In the lab

    Activity 1 · Measuring the overshoot

    Build the lab’s RLC with R = 100 Ω and drive it with a low-frequency square wave. Measure the overshoot of the capacitor voltage and compare it with the 16.3 % predicted by \( \zeta = 0.5 \).

    Activity 2 · Finding critical damping

    Using this page’s lab, locate the value of \( R \) at which the two poles merge. Verify that it matches \( R = 2\sqrt{L/C} \).

    Activity 3 · Residues by hand

    Calculate the three residues of \( V_C(s) \) for \( R = 300 \) Ω and write \( v_C(t) \) as a sum of exponentials. Compare with the lab’s data panel.

    06Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the initial conditions. A charged capacitor or an inductor carrying current are sources in the transformed circuit.
    • Looking for the shape of the transient in the residues. The shape is decided by the poles; the residues only set the amplitudes.
    • Treating a double pole as if it were two simple ones. A double pole contributes \( t\,e^{pt} \), not two equal exponentials.
    • Confusing \( \omega_n \) with the frequency at which it oscillates. The damped oscillation runs at \( \omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2} \), somewhat lower.
    • Believing that more resistance always gives a faster response. Past critical damping, the response becomes slower.

    07Self-assessment

    What does a charged capacitor represent in the transformed circuit?

    An impedance \( 1/sC \) in series with a source \( v(0)/s \).

    How is the residue of a simple pole calculated?

    \( K_k = \lim_{s\to p_k}(s-p_k)Y(s) \).

    What transient shape do two complex poles with negative real part give?

    An oscillation that dies out exponentially.

    What is the overshoot with \( \zeta = 0.5 \)?

    16.3 % of the final value.

    At what resistance is the damping of a series RLC of 1 mH and 100 nF critical?

    \( R = 2\sqrt{L/C} = 200 \) Ω.
