    Signals and signal sources

    A 600 Ω microphone connected to a 600 Ω input loses half of its signal before the amplifier does anything. Choosing an amplifier starts with looking at the source.

    Thévenin and Norton Decibels Loading effect Input impedance Amplifier types

    01The signal and its source

    An amplifier never works alone: it takes the signal from a source —a microphone, a sensor, an antenna, the output of another circuit— and delivers it to a load. How well or badly it performs depends as much on those two ends as on the amplifier itself. That is why the study of amplifiers starts by modeling the source. The description of signals in time and in frequency is in signals in circuits; here the source matters as a circuit.

    Every linear source can be replaced by a Thévenin equivalent —a voltage source \( v_s \) in series with a resistance \( R_s \)— or a Norton equivalent —a current source \( i_s = v_s / R_s \) in parallel with the same \( R_s \)—. The two models are equivalent; you choose the one that better describes what the source does: a microphone behaves like a voltage source, a photodiode like a current source.

    The source resistance also sets a noise floor: every resistance generates thermal noise \( v_n = \sqrt{4 k T R B} \). A 1 kΩ source, over the 20 kHz of audio and at room temperature, already brings about 0.58 µV rms that no amplifier can remove.

    03Decibels

    The signals in a system span many orders of magnitude, from the microvolts of the antenna to the volts of the loudspeaker. That is why they are measured in decibels, a logarithmic scale of ratios:

    \[ G_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{P_2}{P_1} \] \[ G_{\text{dB}} = 20 \log_{10} \frac{V_2}{V_1} \quad \text{(with the same resistance)} \] Doubling the voltage is 6 dB; multiplying it by ten, 20 dB. In a chain of stages the gains in decibels add.

    With a fixed reference, the decibel becomes an absolute unit:

    UnitReferenceUse
    dBm1 mWRadio frequency and telecommunications
    dBu0.775 V rms, the voltage that gives 1 mW in 600 ΩProfessional audio
    dBV1 V rmsConsumer audio (−10 dBV = 0.316 V)
    dBµV1 µVSignal levels at antennas and in electromagnetic compatibility

    04The loaded source

    When the source is connected to the input of an amplifier, of resistance \( R_{in} \), a voltage divider is formed. The voltage that actually arrives is

    \[ v_{in} = v_s \frac{R_{in}}{R_s + R_{in}} \] With \( R_{in} = R_s \) half arrives: 6 dB lost. With \( R_{in} = 10\,R_s \), 91 %: 0.8 dB.

    This gives three criteria, depending on what you want to transfer:

    • Voltage: \( R_{in} \gg R_s \). This is the case for almost all audio and instrumentation: an input ten or more times larger than the source.
    • Current: \( R_{in} \ll R_s \). A photodiode is connected to a transimpedance amplifier, whose input is almost a short circuit.
    • Power: \( R_{in} = R_s \). This is the matching used in radio frequency, where everything is 50 Ω so that there are no reflections and the maximum power is transferred.

    Besides its resistance, every input and every cable has capacitance. Together with the source resistance it forms a low-pass filter, with cutoff frequency \( f_c = 1 / (2\pi\, (R_s \parallel R_{in})\, C_{in}) \): a high-impedance source loses its high frequencies in a long cable.

    05The four types of amplifier

    Depending on which quantity it takes from the source and which quantity it delivers to the load, an ideal amplifier belongs to one of four types. Their ideal input and output resistances follow from the same loading argument:

    TypeInput → outputGainIdeal \( R_{in} \)Ideal \( R_{out} \)
    Voltagevoltage → voltageV/VInfiniteZero
    Currentcurrent → currentA/AZeroInfinite
    Transconductancevoltage → currentA/V (siemens)InfiniteInfinite
    Transresistancecurrent → voltageV/A (ohms)ZeroZero

    A transistor on its own is, by nature, a transconductance amplifier: its input is a voltage and its output a current. The circuits in the following units surround it with resistors and other transistors to turn it into whichever type is needed.

    07Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the source resistance. The open-circuit voltage is not the one that arrives.
    • Matching impedances in audio. Half the voltage is lost for no reason: in audio the aim is \( R_{in} \gg R_s \).
    • Adding power decibels and voltage decibels as if they were the same. 20 log for voltages, 10 log for powers.
    • Ignoring the cable capacitance with high-impedance sources. It cuts the high frequencies.

    08Self-assessment

    How many decibels are lost if \( R_{in} = R_s \)?

    6 dB: half the voltage arrives.

    How many volts is +4 dBu?

    \( 0.775 \times 10^{4/20} \approx 1.23 \) V rms.

    Which type of amplifier suits a photodiode?

    A transresistance amplifier: current input, with a very low resistance, and voltage output.

    A 50 kΩ source with 200 pF of cable and a very high input resistance: where does it cut off?

    \( 1/(2\pi \times 50\,000 \times 200 \times 10^{-12}) \approx 15.9 \) kHz.
