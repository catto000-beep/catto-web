    Hypothesis tests

    Moving the decision threshold splits the risk between the two possible errors, but does not reduce it. The only thing that lowers both at once is measuring more, and which of the two hurts more is an engineering decision.

    Null hypothesis Type I and II error Power p-value Significance

    01How a test is set up

    A hypothesis test decides between two statements using data. The structure is always the same and is deliberately asymmetric: you start by assuming that nothing is going on and you only abandon that assumption if the data make it very hard to believe.

    What it claimsIts role
    \( H_0 \), nullThere is no effect, no difference Upheld unless proven otherwise
    \( H_1 \), alternativeThere is an effect It is what has to be demonstrated

    The asymmetry is intentional and resembles that of a trial: innocence is not proven, it is presumed. That is why the outcome of a test is never “\( H_0 \) is true,” but “there is not enough evidence to reject it”, which is quite a bit less.

    \[ z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \qquad\qquad t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \] The test statistic measures how many standard errors the observation is from what \( H_0 \) predicts. If that number is large, the data are unusual under \( H_0 \); how large it has to be is set by the significance level.

    02The two errors

    Any decision made with limited data can go wrong in two ways, and each has its own name because they are not interchangeable.

    \( H_0 \) is true\( H_0 \) is false
    RejectType I error, probability \( \alpha \)Correct: power \( 1-\beta \)
    Do not rejectCorrectType II error, probability \( \beta \)

    In quality control they have names of their own: the producer’s risk is rejecting a batch that was good, and the consumer’s risk is accepting one that was bad. Which of the two hurts more depends on what is being manufactured, and that decision is one of engineering, not of statistics.

    Lab · the two errors and the threshold

    The two distributions —that of \( H_0 \) and that of \( H_1 \)— with the decision threshold in between. Move it and watch how one error grows when the other drops. Then increase the sample size and see what happens to both at once.

    03The p-value, and what it is not

    The p-value is the probability of observing a result at least as extreme as the one obtained assuming that \( H_0 \) is true. If it is smaller than \( \alpha \), you reject.

    Three things the p-value is not

    It is not the probability that \( H_0 \) is true: it is calculated assuming that it is. It is not the probability of having made a mistake in rejecting. And it does not measure the size of the effect: with a large enough sample, a negligible difference gives a tiny p. A result can be statistically significant and practically irrelevant, and that distinction is the most important one in the whole unit.

    SituationDecisionWhat it really means
    \( p = 0.001 \), effect of 0.01 %\( H_0 \) is rejected The difference exists and does not matter
    \( p = 0.20 \), effect of 30 %Not rejected There may be a large effect and data are lacking
    \( p = 0.049 \) versus \( p = 0.051 \)Reject / do not reject They are practically the same result

    The third row is against the magic threshold. The 0.05 is a convention of Fisher’s, not a natural law, and treating 0.049 and 0.051 as opposite conclusions is an artifact. The reasonable approach is to report the p-value together with the effect size and its confidence interval, and let the reader judge.

    04In the lab

    Activity 1 · Comparing two batches

    Measure twenty resistors from each of two different batches of the same nominal value. State the hypothesis that the means are equal, calculate the t statistic and the p-value, and decide with \( \alpha = 0.05 \).

    Activity 2 · The power of the test

    With the measured standard deviation, calculate what difference between batches could be detected with 80 % power using twenty measurements per batch. Recalculate for fifty and compare.

    Activity 3 · Significant is not important

    Simulate two sets of a thousand data points that differ by 0.1 % of the mean. Verify that the p-value comes out very small and discuss whether that difference would justify rejecting a batch in production.

    05Common mistakes

    • Saying that \( H_0 \) was “accepted.” It was not rejected, which is different: the evidence may be lacking.
    • Interpreting the p-value as the probability that \( H_0 \) is true. It is calculated assuming that it is.
    • Confusing significant with important. With a large sample, any difference comes out significant.
    • Lowering \( \alpha \) without looking at \( \beta \). Reducing one error enlarges the other.
    • Choosing the hypothesis after seeing the data. That invalidates the whole test.
    • Testing many things and reporting the one that worked. With twenty tests at 5 %, one comes out significant by pure chance.

    06Self-assessment

    What is a Type I error?

    Rejecting \( H_0 \) when it is true. Its probability is \( \alpha \), the significance level.

    What is the power of a test?

    \( 1-\beta \): the probability of detecting an effect that really exists.

    How do you reduce both errors at once?

    By increasing the sample size. Moving the threshold only trades one for the other.

    What does \( p = 0.03 \) mean?

    That if \( H_0 \) were true, a result this extreme would occur 3 % of the time.

    Can a result be significant and not matter?

    Yes, and it is common. With large samples, negligible differences give very small p-values.

    What is the consumer’s risk?

    Accepting a batch that was bad: a Type II error in the context of quality control.
