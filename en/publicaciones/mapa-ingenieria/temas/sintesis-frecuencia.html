    Frequency synthesis

    With 1 kΩ in the filter, a PLL takes 645 μs to change channel; with 3.3 kΩ, 205 μs. A single resistor decides whether the loop oscillates before locking.

    Direct synthesis Charge-pump PLL Loop filter Damping Lock time

    01How a frequency is synthesized

    A transmitter or a receiver needs local oscillators that change channel with the accuracy of a crystal. There are three families of synthesizers, which are combined in real equipment:

    TechniqueIdeaAdvantageLimit
    Analog direct synthesisMix, multiply and divide reference frequenciesVery fast switchingBulky and with spurs
    Direct digital synthesis (DDS)A phase accumulator and a converterVery fine resolution, instantaneous changeFrequency below half the clock
    Phase-locked loop (PLL)A VCO locked to a reference through a dividerGHz frequencies with low noiseThe loop takes time to lock

    Direct digital synthesis was covered in signal sources, and the architecture of the integer-N and fractional-N PLL, with its phase noise, in synthesized generators. Here the dynamics of the loop are studied: how long it takes to change channel and how the filter is chosen.

    02The linear model of the charge-pump PLL

    Once locked, the PLL is a phase control system. The charge-pump phase detector delivers a current proportional to the phase error, \( K_\phi = I_{cp}/2\pi \); the loop filter, a resistor in series with a capacitor, converts it to a voltage; the VCO converts it to frequency, and since phase is the integral of frequency, the VCO behaves as an integrator \( K_v/s \). With the divider \( N \), the loop gain has two poles at the origin and a zero:

    \[ G(s) = \frac{K_\phi K_v (1 + sRC)}{N C\, s^2} \] \[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{K_\phi K_v}{N C}} \] \[ \zeta = \frac{R C\,\omega_n}{2} \] It is a type 2 loop: it follows a frequency change with no phase error, as seen in steady state. \( \omega_n \) and \( \zeta \) set the loop bandwidth and the shape of the lock. In practice a second capacitor is added in parallel to filter the charge-pump pulses, which adds a high pole.

    03Lock time and trade-offs

    When the channel changes, the divider changes its N and the VCO frequency goes from the old value to the new one with the step response of the closed loop. The lock time is of the order of a few time constants \( 1/(\zeta\omega_n) \): a wide loop locks quickly. But the bandwidth has limits. It has to be much smaller than the comparison frequency, so that the loop is stable and attenuates the reference spurs. And it decides the phase noise: inside the band the reference's noise multiplied by N dominates, outside, that of the VCO.

    05Common mistakes

    • Seeking the widest possible loop. It lets through the reference noise and the comparison spurs.
    • Forgetting that ωn depends on N. When changing channel over a wide range, the bandwidth and the damping change.
    • Using a damping ratio of 0.707 as a rule. With the filter zero, the overshoot is greater than in a pure second-order system.
    • Ignoring the VCO gain over its whole range. Kv varies, and with it the dynamics.

    06Self-assessment

    What Kφ does a 2 mA charge pump have?

    \( 2\cdot10^{-3}/2\pi \approx 318 \) μA/rad.

    If C is quadrupled while keeping R, how do ωn and ζ change?

    ωn is halved; ζ is doubled, because it is proportional to C·ωn.

    Why does the charge-pump PLL follow a frequency change with no phase error?

    Because its loop gain has two integrators: that of the VCO and that of the filter capacitor.

    What limits the loop bandwidth from above?

    The comparison frequency: the loop has to be much slower, for stability and to filter the spurs.
