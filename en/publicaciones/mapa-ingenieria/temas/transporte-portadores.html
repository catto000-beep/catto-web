    Basic semiconductor theory: carrier transport

    A silicon wafer with one phosphorus atom per ten million conducts more than a hundred thousand times better than pure silicon. Every device is born from controlling how many carriers there are and how they move.

    Drift Mobility Diffusion Einstein relation Recombination

    01From material to device

    The page on semiconductor physics established how many carriers there are: the intrinsic concentration \( n_i \), on the order of \( 10^{10} \) cm⁻³ in silicon at room temperature, the law of mass action \( n\,p = n_i^2 \), and how doping fixes one of the two concentrations. A device, on the other hand, is defined by how those carriers move: what current flows, in which direction, and how long it takes to appear or disappear.

    There are only two mechanisms that produce current in a semiconductor, drift and diffusion, and two processes that change the number of carriers, generation and recombination. With those four pieces and one equation that ties them together you can explain the diode, the bipolar transistor and the solar cell.

    02Drift and mobility

    An electric field accelerates the carriers, but collisions with the crystal lattice and with impurities slow them down again and again. The net result is an average velocity proportional to the field, and the constant of proportionality is the mobility \( \mu \):

    \[ v_n = -\mu_n E \qquad v_p = \mu_p E \] \[ J_{\text{drift}} = q\,(n\,\mu_n + p\,\mu_p)\,E = \sigma E \qquad \rho = \frac{1}{\sigma} \] Electrons move against the field and holes with it, but since their charges have opposite signs the two currents add.

    In lightly doped silicon at 300 K, \( \mu_n \approx 1400 \) and \( \mu_p \approx 470 \) cm²/V·s: electrons are about three times more mobile than holes, and that asymmetry reappears in every device (a PMOS transistor has to be wider than an NMOS to conduct the same current). With 1 V applied across 10 µm, the field is 1000 V/cm and an electron drifts at about 14 km/s.

    Mobility drops as doping increases, because each ionized impurity is one more scattering center. A widely used empirical fit is the Caughey–Thomas one:

    \[ \mu(N) = \mu_{\min} + \frac{\mu_{\max} - \mu_{\min}}{1 + \left( N / N_{\text{ref}} \right)^{\alpha}} \] For electrons in silicon: \( \mu_{\min} = 68.5 \), \( \mu_{\max} = 1414 \) cm²/V·s, \( N_{\text{ref}} = 9.2 \times 10^{16} \) cm⁻³, \( \alpha = 0.711 \). For holes: 44.9, 470.5, \( 2.23 \times 10^{17} \) and 0.719.
    Proportionality has a ceiling

    \( v = \mu E \) holds for moderate fields. Above about \( 10^4 \) V/cm the velocity stops growing and saturates near \( 10^7 \) cm/s. In a modern MOS transistor, with channels tens of nanometers long, almost the whole channel works in velocity saturation: it is one of the short-channel effects covered in the unit on MOS technology.

    03Diffusion and the Einstein relation

    With no applied field there can still be current. If the carrier concentration is not uniform, their random thermal motion carries more carriers from the crowded region to the sparse one than the other way around: this is diffusion, the same process that spreads a drop of ink in a glass of water. The current is proportional to the concentration gradient:

    \[ J_{n,\text{diff}} = q\,D_n \frac{dn}{dx} \qquad J_{p,\text{diff}} = -q\,D_p \frac{dp}{dx} \] \( D \) is the diffusion coefficient, in cm²/s. The minus sign for holes: they diffuse toward where there are fewer of them, and they carry positive charge.

    Drift and diffusion are not independent: both are limited by the same collisions. Einstein showed that the ratio between the diffusion coefficient and the mobility is the thermal voltage:

    \[ \frac{D}{\mu} = \frac{k\,T}{q} = V_T \approx 25.85 \text{ mV at } 300 \text{ K} \] With \( \mu_n = 1414 \) cm²/V·s, \( D_n \approx 36.6 \) cm²/s; with \( \mu_p = 470.5 \), \( D_p \approx 12.2 \) cm²/s.

    The total current of each carrier is the sum of the two contributions. These two expressions are the starting point of all device theory:

    \[ J_n = q\,n\,\mu_n E + q\,D_n \frac{dn}{dx} \] \[ J_p = q\,p\,\mu_p E - q\,D_p \frac{dp}{dx} \]

    The thermal voltage \( V_T \) appears here for the first time and never goes away: it is in the diode equation, in the transconductance of the bipolar transistor and in the subthreshold slope of the MOSFET.

    04Generation, recombination and lifetime

    At equilibrium, electron-hole pairs are generated by thermal agitation at the same rate at which they recombine, and \( n\,p = n_i^2 \). If something injects extra carriers (light, a forward-biased junction), the excess recombines and the concentration returns to equilibrium. Under low injection —the excess is much smaller than the majority-carrier concentration— the minority carrier sets the rate, and recombination is proportional to the excess:

    \[ R = \frac{\Delta n}{\tau_n} \qquad L_n = \sqrt{D_n\,\tau_n} \] \( \tau \) is the minority-carrier lifetime; \( L \), the diffusion length: how far they travel on average before recombining.

    Silicon has an indirect band gap: an electron cannot fall directly into a hole emitting a photon, and it almost always recombines through defects with levels inside the band gap (the Shockley–Read–Hall mechanism). That is why the lifetime depends more on purity than on the material: it ranges from milliseconds in very pure silicon to nanoseconds when it is deliberately contaminated with gold or platinum to make fast diodes. With \( D_n = 36.6 \) cm²/s and \( \tau_n = 1 \) µs, the diffusion length is about 60 µm.

    05The continuity equation

    The previous pieces come together in a balance: in a small piece of semiconductor, the number of electrons changes by what comes in minus what goes out, plus what is generated, minus what recombines:

    \[ \frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{q} \frac{\partial J_n}{\partial x} + G_n - R_n \] Together with the current expressions and Poisson's equation for the field, this is the system that device simulators solve.

    The most useful case is the steady state, with no field, with minority carriers injected at one end. The equation becomes \( D\,\dfrac{d^2 \Delta p}{dx^2} = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\tau} \), and its solution is an excess that decays exponentially with the diffusion length:

    \[ \Delta p(x) = \Delta p(0)\; e^{-x/L_p} \] At one diffusion length from the edge, 37 % of the excess remains; at three, 5 %.

    That profile is exactly what happens on each side of a forward-biased junction, and from its slope at the edge comes the diode current, as seen in the next unit.

    08Common mistakes

    • Thinking that more doping always gives proportionally more conduction. The conductivity grows less than the doping, because the mobility drops.
    • Using the same mobility for electrons and holes. In silicon they differ by a factor of three.
    • Forgetting diffusion where there is no field. In the base of a bipolar transistor almost all of the current is diffusion current.
    • Applying \( v = \mu E \) at high fields. The velocity saturates near \( 10^7 \) cm/s.
    • Confusing lifetime with transit time. The first is how long a minority carrier lives; the second, how long it takes to cross a region.

    09Self-assessment

    What are the two current mechanisms in a semiconductor and what drives them?

    Drift, driven by the electric field, and diffusion, driven by the concentration gradient.

    Why does mobility decrease with doping?

    Because each ionized impurity is an additional scattering center for the carriers.

    If \( \mu_p = 470 \) cm²/V·s, what is \( D_p \) at 300 K?

    \( D_p = \mu_p V_T = 470 \times 0.02585 \approx 12.2 \) cm²/s.

    With \( D = 12 \) cm²/s and \( \tau = 10 \) µs, what is the diffusion length?

    \( L = \sqrt{12 \times 10^{-5}} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-2} \) cm, about 110 µm.

    Why is the silicon of a fast diode contaminated with gold?

    Gold introduces levels in the middle of the band gap that speed up recombination and shorten the lifetime, so the stored charge disappears sooner.
