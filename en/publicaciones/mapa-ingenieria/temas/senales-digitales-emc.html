    Digital signals, logic analyzers and electromagnetic compatibility

    Two identical 25 MHz clocks emit very differently at 300 MHz if one has 1 ns edges and the other 5 ns. What decides the high emissions is the rise time, not the frequency.

    Logic analyzer Electromagnetic compatibility CISPR 32 Trapezoidal spectrum Rise time

    01The logic analyzer

    A digital system has dozens of lines changing at once. The oscilloscope shows a few with analog detail; the logic analyzer shows many, 16, 32 or more, but only as zeros and ones, comparing each input with a threshold. It works in two modes:

    ModeSampling clockWhat it is for
    TimingInternal, asynchronous, several times faster than the signalSeeing timing relationships, spurious pulses, setup-time violations
    StateThe system’s own clockSeeing what values the buses held in each cycle, as the circuit sees it

    In timing mode, the resolution is the sampling period: to see an edge with an error of 10% of the signal’s period you have to sample at least ten times faster. Mixed-signal analyzers and oscilloscopes decode protocols (UART, SPI, I²C, CAN, USB) and trigger on contents, as seen in serial protocols. For fast links, the tool is the eye diagram from digital modulation.

    02Electromagnetic compatibility

    A device is electromagnetically compatible if it works well in its environment without disturbing others. Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) has two faces: emissions, what the device produces, and immunity, what it withstands. Each is divided into conducted, through the cables, and radiated, through the air:

    TestTypical rangeHow it is measured
    Conducted emissions150 kHz to 30 MHzWith a line impedance stabilization network (LISN) between the mains and the device
    Radiated emissions30 MHz to 1 GHz and beyondWith antennas at 3 or 10 m, in a semi-anechoic chamber or open-area test site
    ImmunityElectrostatic discharges, fast transients, RF fields, surgesApplying the disturbance and verifying operation

    The emission limits for multimedia equipment are in the CISPR 32 standard. For residential use, class B, the radiated emissions measured at 10 m must not exceed 30 dBμV/m between 30 and 230 MHz, nor 37 dBμV/m between 230 MHz and 1 GHz, with a quasi-peak detector. Preliminary measurements in the development laboratory, with a spectrum analyzer and near-field probes, save repeating costly formal tests.

    03The spectrum of a clock

    The most common source of emissions in a digital circuit is its clock. An ideal square wave has odd harmonics that decrease 20 dB per decade; a real one, with edges of finite duration, is a trapezoid. Its spectral envelope has two breakpoints:

    \[ c_n = 2A\,\frac{\tau}{T}\left|\frac{\sin(n\pi\tau/T)}{n\pi\tau/T}\right|\left|\frac{\sin(n\pi t_r/T)}{n\pi t_r/T}\right| \] \[ f_1 = \frac{1}{\pi\tau}, \quad f_2 = \frac{1}{\pi t_r} \] \( A \) is the amplitude, \( T \) the period, \( \tau \) the pulse width and \( t_r \) the rise time. Up to \( f_1 \) the envelope is flat; between \( f_1 \) and \( f_2 \) it falls 20 dB per decade, and above \( f_2 \), 40 dB per decade. The rise time, not the clock frequency, decides the high-frequency emissions.

    The harmonics of a clock with a 50% duty cycle, in dBμV, and its envelope. The shaded zone is the radiated emissions band from 30 MHz to 1 GHz.

    The measures that follow from this result are those of design for EMC: edges no faster than necessary, series resistors on clock outputs, continuous ground planes that shorten the current loops, and decoupling capacitors close to each integrated circuit.

    04In the laboratory

    Activity 1 · State and timing

    Capture an SPI bus with a logic analyzer in both modes and compare what is seen in each.

    Activity 2 · Near-field probe

    Scan a microcontroller board with a near-field probe connected to a spectrum analyzer and find the source of the clock harmonics.

    Activity 3 · Series resistor

    Add a 33 Ω resistor in series with the clock output and measure the reduction of the high harmonics.

    05Common mistakes

    • Sampling in timing mode only slightly faster than the signal. Pulses are missed and the position of the edges is imprecise.
    • Thinking that lowering the clock frequency reduces all the emissions. The high ones are set by the rise time.
    • Leaving EMC for the final test. Fixing a finished design is expensive; it is prevented in the layout.
    • Confusing the near-field probe measurement with the standards test. It is for comparing, not for certifying.

    06Self-assessment

    Where is the second breakpoint of the envelope of a clock with 2 ns edges?

    \( 1/(\pi \cdot 2\cdot10^{-9}) \approx 159 \) MHz.

    How much does the envelope fall between 300 MHz and 1 GHz if the second breakpoint is at 100 MHz?

    40 dB per decade: about 21 dB.

    What radiated emission limit does CISPR 32 class B set at 100 MHz, measured at 10 m?

    30 dBμV/m quasi-peak.

    What is the difference between state mode and timing mode?

    In state mode, sampling is done with the system clock; in timing mode, with a faster internal clock.
