    The circuit in the transformed domain

    In the Laplace domain, a charged capacitor is an impedance 1/sC in series with a source v(0)/s. The initial conditions stop being a separate problem and become part of the circuit.

    Laplace transform Initial conditions Transfer function Poles Damping

    01Why transform

    A circuit with inductors and capacitors is described by differential equations, and solving them in the time domain requires setting up the homogeneous and particular solutions and fitting constants to the initial conditions. The Laplace transform turns derivatives into products by \( s \) and differential equations into algebraic ones. The entire circuit can be transformed element by element and solved with the same tools as a resistive circuit: meshes, nodes, Thévenin. In Circuit Theory I the s-plane was used in transient response; here the method is completed.

    The foundation is that the circuit be linear and time-invariant: its components do not change value and the response to a sum of excitations is the sum of the responses. That is what the theory of LTI systems requires.

    02The transformed elements

    ElementIn timeIn the s domain
    Resistor\( v = R\,i \)\( V = R\,I \)
    Inductor\( v = L\, di/dt \)\( V = sL\,I - L\,i(0) \): impedance \( sL \) in series with a source \( L\,i(0) \)
    Capacitor\( i = C\, dv/dt \)\( V = I/(sC) + v(0)/s \): impedance \( 1/sC \) in series with a source \( v(0)/s \)

    The energy stored at the start appears as additional sources, and the transformed circuit is solved in one go with all the sources. The complete response breaks down into two parts that can be calculated separately and added:

    \[ Y(s) = H(s)\,X(s) + \frac{P(s)}{D(s)} \] The first term is the zero-state response, to the excitation with the circuit discharged; the second, the zero-input response, which is produced only by the initial conditions. \( H(s) \) is the transfer function, and its denominator, the same in both terms, gives the poles.

    03The poles decide the shape

    Each pole \( p \) of the response contributes a term \( K e^{pt} \). A negative real pole gives a decaying exponential; a complex conjugate pair \( -\alpha \pm j\omega_d \), a damped oscillation; a pole in the right half-plane, a growing response: an unstable circuit. In the series RLC:

    \[ s^2 + \frac{R}{L}\,s + \frac{1}{LC} = 0 \] \[ \zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} \] With \( \zeta < 1 \) the poles are complex and the response oscillates (underdamped); with \( \zeta = 1 \), double and real (critical damping, the fastest rise without overshoot); with \( \zeta > 1 \), real and distinct (overdamped).
    Lab · a series RLC with initial conditions

    A 5 V step at \( t = 0 \) on a series RLC with \( L = 10 \) mH and \( C = 1\ \mu \)F (resonance at 1.59 kHz; critical damping with 200 Ω). The capacitor can start charged and the inductor with current. At the top, the capacitor voltage; on the right, the poles in the s-plane.

    04Transfer function and frequency response

    With zero initial conditions, the ratio of the transformed output to the transformed input is the transfer function \( H(s) \). Evaluated on the imaginary axis, \( s = j\omega \), it gives the frequency response; its inverse transform, the impulse response. A single function then describes both the transient and the steady-state behavior: the same poles that make the step response oscillate produce the resonance peak in the Bode plot.

    06In the lab

    Activity 1 · Three damping cases

    Build a series RLC with 10 mH and 1 µF, drive it with a low-frequency square wave and observe the voltage across the capacitor with 40 Ω, 200 Ω and 600 Ω. Measure the overshoot and the oscillation frequency.

    Activity 2 · Initial condition

    Charge the capacitor to 3 V with a source and a switch, switch over to the circuit and compare the response with that of the lab.

    Activity 3 · From the Bode plot to the step

    Measure the frequency response of the same circuit and relate the resonance peak to the overshoot of the step response.

    07Common mistakes

    • Forgetting the initial-condition sources. The transform of the derivative brings in the initial value.
    • Getting the sign of the inductor source wrong. The direction of the initial current has to be respected.
    • Looking for poles in the numerator. The poles are the roots of the denominator; those of the numerator are zeros.
    • Applying the final value theorem to an unstable system. It only holds if all the poles, except perhaps one at the origin, are in the left half-plane.

    08Self-assessment

    What resistance gives critical damping with 10 mH and 1 µF?

    \( 2\sqrt{L/C} = 2\sqrt{10^4} = 200\ \Omega \).

    How is a capacitor charged to 3 V represented in s?

    An impedance \( 1/sC \) in series with a source of \( 3/s \).

    What overshoot does the step response have with ζ = 0.2?

    \( e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} \approx 0.53 \): 53 %.

    What shape does the response have if the poles are −1000 and −4000 s⁻¹?

    The sum of two decaying exponentials, with no oscillation: overdamped.
