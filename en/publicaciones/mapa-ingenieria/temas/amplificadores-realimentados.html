    Feedback amplifiers

    An amplifier that distorts 7.7 % drops to 1.2 % with a loop gain of 10. Feedback trades surplus gain for linearity.

    Loop gain Desensitivity Topologies Impedances Distortion

    01The loop gain

    A feedback amplifier takes a sample of its output, multiplies it by a factor \( \beta \) and subtracts it from the input. If the amplifier without feedback has gain \( A \), the closed-loop gain is:

    \[ A_f = \frac{A}{1 + A\beta} = \frac{A}{1 + T} \] \( T = A\beta \) is the loop gain: how much a signal is amplified as it travels around the complete loop. If \( T \gg 1 \), \( A_f \approx 1/\beta \) and the gain is set by the feedback network, which is built with precise, stable resistors.

    Feedback trades gain for other qualities. The most direct trade is desensitivity: a relative variation of \( A \) reaches the feedback gain divided by \( 1 + T \):

    \[ \frac{dA_f}{A_f} = \frac{1}{1 + T}\, \frac{dA}{A} \] With A = 10⁵ and β = 0.01 (T = 1000), a 50 % change in the amplifier gain, due to temperature or spread, moves the feedback gain by barely 0.1 %.

    The multistage amplifiers of Applied Electronics I have large but imprecise gains; feedback turns them into small, exact gains.

    02The four topologies

    The sample of the output can be a voltage (shunt connection across the output) or a current (series connection), and the feedback signal can be subtracted as a voltage (in series with the input) or as a current (in parallel). Each combination stabilizes a different transfer function and modifies the impedances by the factor \( 1 + T \):

    Topology (input–output)StabilizesInputOutput
    Series–shuntVoltage gain\( R_i(1+T) \)\( R_o/(1+T) \)
    Shunt–seriesCurrent gain\( R_i/(1+T) \)\( R_o(1+T) \)
    Series–seriesTransconductance\( R_i(1+T) \)\( R_o(1+T) \)
    Shunt–shuntTransresistance\( R_i/(1+T) \)\( R_o/(1+T) \)

    The rule is simple: a series connection multiplies the impedance by \( 1 + T \) and a shunt connection divides it. The noninverting amplifier with an op amp is series–shunt: very high input impedance and almost zero output impedance. The common-emitter stage with an unbypassed emitter resistor is series–series, and with a collector-to-base resistor, shunt–shunt.

    In real circuits the feedback network loads the amplifier. The rigorous analysis first separates the amplifier loaded by the network, calculates \( A \) and \( \beta \) of that version, and only then applies the formulas. Another, more direct way is to calculate \( T \) by breaking the loop at a point and to apply Blackman's theorem for the impedances.

    03Distortion, noise and bandwidth

    Feedback continuously compares the output with the input: if the amplifier distorts, the error appears at the amplifier input and is corrected. The distortion components generated inside the loop end up divided, approximately, by \( 1 + T \), when compared at equal output amplitude. The same happens with bandwidth: with a dominant pole at \( f_H \),

    \[ f_{Hf} = f_H\,(1 + T_0) \] \[ A_f\, f_{Hf} = A_0\, f_H \] The gain–bandwidth product is conserved: the gain that is given up is converted into bandwidth. This is the basis of the frequency analysis in the following units.
    Lab · feedback reduces distortion

    An amplifier of gain \( A \) with an asymmetric hyperbolic-tangent transfer function, which saturates at +7 V and −13 V. The feedback output is calculated by solving the loop at each instant.

    There is something that feedback does not improve: the noise that enters along with the signal, or that the first stage generates. That noise is amplified just like the signal. The signal-to-noise ratio is decided at the input stage, before the loop.

    04In the lab

    Activity 1 · Desensitivity

    Build a two-stage transistor amplifier, and measure its gain without feedback and with a series-voltage feedback network. Swap the transistor for another with a different β and compare the variations.

    Activity 2 · Impedances

    Measure the input and output impedances with and without feedback and verify the factor \( 1 + T \).

    Activity 3 · Distortion

    With a spectrum analyzer or the FFT of an oscilloscope, measure the harmonic distortion with and without feedback, adjusting the input for the same output.

    05Common mistakes

    • Comparing distortion at equal input. With feedback the output is much smaller; the valid comparison is at equal output.
    • Forgetting the loading of the feedback network. Without including it, \( A \) and \( \beta \) come out wrong and so do the impedances.
    • Expecting feedback to lower the input noise. It only reduces what is generated inside the loop, after the input.
    • Confusing negative feedback with positive. If the sign is reversed, \( 1 + T \) can vanish and the circuit oscillates.

    06Self-assessment

    An amplifier with gain 2000 is fed back with β = 0.05. What gain does it have?

    \( 2000/(1 + 100) \approx 19.8 \).

    In the same circuit, if the amplifier gain drops to 1000, how much does the feedback gain change?

    It becomes \( 1000/51 = 19.6 \): a 1 % change for a 50 % one.

    An amplifier with a 1 kΩ output resistance is fed back in series–shunt with T = 99. What output resistance does it have?

    10 Ω.

    An amplifier with gain 10⁴ has a pole at 100 Hz. What bandwidth does it have with gain 100?

    About 10 kHz: the gain–bandwidth product, 1 MHz, is conserved.
