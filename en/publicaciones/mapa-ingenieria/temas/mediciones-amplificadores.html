    Amplifier measurements

    An amplifier with a 1.5 dB noise figure and 10 dB of gain, measured with an ordinary analyzer, reads as if it had 10.5 dB. Without correcting for the second instrument, the measurement says nothing about the amplifier.

    01What is measured in an amplifier

    The specifications of an amplifier fall into four families, and each one has its instrument and its method:

    FamilyQuantitiesInstruments
    Small signalGain, frequency response, input and output impedances, return lossGenerator and oscilloscope or voltmeter; network analyzer
    Large signalOutput power, 1 dB compression, harmonic distortionLow-distortion generator and spectrum or audio analyzer
    Linearity with several signalsIntermodulation, third-order intercept pointTwo combined generators and spectrum analyzer
    NoiseNoise figure, noise temperature, input-referred noiseCalibrated noise source and analyzer

    The theory of each quantity was covered in Applied Electronics II, for example in feedback amplifiers and frequency response. Here the interest is in how to measure them well.

    02Compression and intermodulation

    As the input is raised, the gain starts to fall: the 1 dB compression point is the output power at which the gain has dropped 1 dB relative to the small-signal gain. With two close tones of frequencies \( f_1 \) and \( f_2 \), the third-order nonlinearity produces products at \( 2f_1 - f_2 \) and \( 2f_2 - f_1 \), which fall inside the band and cannot be filtered. Their level grows 3 dB for each dB of input, and the line that represents them crosses that of the fundamental at the third-order intercept point:

    \[ OIP3 = P_{out} + \frac{\Delta}{2} \] \( P_{out} \) is the power of each tone at the output and \( \Delta \) the difference in dB between a tone and the intermodulation product. With 0 dBm tones and intermodulation at −60 dBc, \( \mathrm{OIP3} = +30\ \text{dBm} \). The measurement is valid far from compression, where the slope is 3:1.

    03Noise figure: the Y-factor method

    The noise figure indicates how much an amplifier degrades the signal-to-noise ratio. To measure it, a calibrated noise source is connected to the input, an avalanche diode that is switched on and off. Its excess noise when on is expressed with the excess noise ratio, ENR. The analyzer measures the noise power at the output with the source on and off, and their ratio is the Y factor:

    \[ Y = \frac{N_{on}}{N_{off}} \] \[ F_{sys} = \frac{ENR}{Y - 1} \] \[ F_1 = F_{sys} - \frac{F_2 - 1}{G_1} \] All quantities as ratios, not in dB. The last one is the Friis formula solved for the amplifier’s noise figure: the analyzer has its own noise figure \( F_2 \), and what is measured first is that of the system. To obtain that of the amplifier, the analyzer has to be calibrated and the gain \( G_1 \) measured.

    For very low-noise amplifiers a low-ENR source is better, which gives a smaller Y factor and one less affected by the linearity of the detector; for high noise, a high-ENR one. In all cases, the uncertainty of the source’s ENR is transferred almost entirely to the result.

    04In the laboratory

    Activity 1 · Compression

    Sweep the input power of an RF amplifier and plot the output to find the 1 dB compression point.

    Activity 2 · IP3

    Combine two generators with a power combiner and measure the third-order intermodulation of an amplifier at several levels. Verify the 3:1 slope and calculate OIP3.

    Activity 3 · Noise figure

    Measure the noise figure of a low-noise amplifier with a noise source and an analyzer with the noise figure option, with and without calibration of the second instrument.

    05Common mistakes

    • Measuring IP3 near compression. The slope stops being 3:1 and the result is not valid.
    • Forgetting the analyzer’s contribution. With little gain in the amplifier, the analyzer’s noise figure dominates.
    • Doing the Y-factor calculation in dB. The formulas apply to linear ratios.
    • Measuring gain without matching impedances. Mismatch losses are confused with gain.

    06Self-assessment

    With −10 dBm tones and intermodulation 50 dB down, what is the OIP3?

    \( -10 + 25 = +15 \) dBm.

    With an ENR of 15 dB, \( Y = 10\ \text{dB} \) is measured. What is the noise figure of the system?

    \( 31.6/(10 - 1) = 3.51 \): 5.5 dB.

    If the amplifier has 20 dB of gain and the analyzer a 20 dB noise figure, how much is subtracted?

    \( (100 - 1)/100 = 0.99 \) as a ratio.

    Why are third-order products the ones that cause the most concern?

    Because they fall close to the signals, inside the band, and cannot be filtered.
