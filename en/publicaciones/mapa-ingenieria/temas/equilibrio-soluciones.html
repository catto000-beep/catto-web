    Equilibrium in solutions

    Hydrofluoric acid is a weak acid and the most dangerous reagent in an electronics laboratory. The two facts do not contradict each other: the strength of an acid measures its dissociation equilibrium, not its toxicity.

    pH Ka and pKa Titration Buffers Solubility product

    01Water ionizes itself

    Two water molecules exchange a proton, and everything else follows from that equilibrium. Its constant is small but not zero, and that is enough to fix the scale on which acidity is measured:

    \[ \mathrm{2H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + OH^-} \qquad K_w = [\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] = 10^{-14} \] At 25 °C. From this it follows that in pure water \( [\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-7} \) and the pH is 7, and that \( \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14 \) in any aqueous solution.

    And from this also follows the resistivity limit of ultrapure water that appeared in the unit on solutions: those \(10^{-7}\) moles per liter of each ion are what set the floor of 18.2 MΩ·cm. No purification can remove them, because the water itself makes them.

    7 is not neutral at every temperature

    \( K_w \) grows with temperature: at 60 °C it is \( 9.6\times10^{-14} \) and pure water has a pH of 6.5. It is still neutral —there are as many protons as hydroxide ions— but the number is no longer 7. Neutral means \( [\mathrm{H^+}] = [\mathrm{OH^-}] \), not “pH equal to seven.”

    02Strong, weak and the constant that separates them

    A strong acid dissociates completely; a weak one reaches an equilibrium, and how much it dissociates is given by its \( K_a \). The scale is handled better in logarithms:

    \[ \mathrm{HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-} \qquad K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a \]

    Hydrochloric, HClStrong—Cleaning of metal oxides, pickling
    Hydrofluoric, HF\( 6.8\times10^{-4} \)3.17 The only one that attacks SiO₂; weak, and yet extremely dangerous
    Acetic\( 1.8\times10^{-5} \)4.74Aluminum etchant mixtures
    Ammonium ion, NH₄⁺\( 5.6\times10^{-10} \)9.25The ammonia pair in BOE
    Weak does not mean harmless

    Hydrofluoric acid is a weak acid and the most dangerous reagent in an electronics laboratory. Precisely because it is weak it circulates undissociated, passes through the skin without warning —at first it hardly hurts— and once inside the fluoride binds to the calcium of the bones. A splash on a small area can be fatal. The strength of an acid measures its dissociation equilibrium, not its toxicity.

    03Titration and buffer solutions

    Adding base to an acid a little at a time and following the pH draws a curve with three regions: a plateau at the beginning —the buffer region, where the pH hardly moves—, a sharp jump at the equivalence point and another plateau at the end. In a buffer solution the pH is set by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:

    \[ \mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} \] When the two forms are present in equal amounts the logarithm vanishes and the pH is exactly \( \mathrm{p}K_a \). That point is the center of the buffer region and the point of maximum buffering capacity.
    Lab · titration curve

    25 mL of 0.1 M acid titrated with 0.1 M sodium hydroxide. The pH does not come from an approximate formula: the exact charge balance is solved for each point. Compare the jump of hydrochloric acid with that of ammonium.

    BOE is a true buffer solution

    The buffered oxide etch —hydrofluoric acid with ammonium fluoride— is the standard etch of silicon oxide. Ammonium fluoride is the conjugate base of HF, so the mixture is exactly a buffer pair: it keeps constant the concentration of the etching species as it is consumed, and that is why the etch rate does not keep falling during the process. Unbuffered HF etches more and more slowly and the thickness removed stops being predictable.

    04Solubility product

    A sparingly soluble salt in contact with its saturated solution is also in equilibrium, and its constant is called the solubility product:

    \[ \mathrm{AgCl(s) \rightleftharpoons Ag^+ + Cl^-} \qquad K_{sp} = [\mathrm{Ag^+}][\mathrm{Cl^-}] = 1.8\times10^{-10} \]

    Comparing the product of the concentrations with \( K_{sp} \) tells you whether it will precipitate or not, just like \( Q \) against \( K \). And from this follows the common-ion effect: adding one of the two ions reduces the solubility of the other, which is the principle by which salts are purified by recrystallization.

    Salt\( K_{sp} \)Where it appears
    AgCl\( 1.8\times10^{-10} \)Ag/AgCl reference electrodes
    CaF₂\( 3.9\times10^{-11} \)What forms when HF waste is neutralized with lime
    Cu(OH)₂\( 2.2\times10^{-20} \)Treatment of electroplating effluents
    PbSO₄\( 1.8\times10^{-8} \)What is deposited on the plates of a discharged battery

    The second row is the safety procedure for a hydrofluoric acid spill: it is neutralized with calcium carbonate or hydroxide, and the fluoride precipitates as calcium fluoride, which is insoluble and harmless. The last one explains why a lead battery that was left discharged for a long time does not recover: the precipitated sulfate crystallizes and stops responding.

    05In the lab

    Activity 1 · Recording a titration curve

    Titrate 25 mL of 0.1 M acetic acid with 0.1 M sodium hydroxide, measuring the pH every milliliter. Plot it, locate the equivalence point by the derivative and read the \( \mathrm{p}K_a \) at the half-equivalence point. Compare with the tabulated value.

    Activity 2 · Testing a buffer solution

    Prepare an acetate/acetic acid buffer and measure how much its pH changes when a drop of strong acid is added. Repeat on distilled water and compare the two shifts.

    Activity 3 · Precipitating and the common ion

    Form silver chloride by mixing dilute silver nitrate with sodium chloride. Then add excess chloride and observe. Calculate the solubility before and after with \( K_{sp} \), and compare with what you see.

    06Common mistakes

    • Believing that pH 7 is always neutral. It is at 25 °C; neutral means \( [\mathrm{H^+}] = [\mathrm{OH^-}] \).
    • Confusing a weak acid with a dilute acid. Strength is the constant; concentration is how much there is.
    • Assuming that the pH is 7 at the equivalence point. Only if the acid and the base are both strong; with a weak acid the salt hydrolyzes and the solution ends up basic.
    • Believing that a weak acid is less dangerous. Hydrofluoric acid disproves that in the worst way.
    • Using Henderson-Hasselbalch outside the buffer region. It is an approximation valid near \( \mathrm{p}K_a \).
    • Comparing \( K_{sp} \) between salts of different stoichiometry. The exponents are not the same and the numbers are not directly comparable.

    07Self-assessment

    What is the pH at the half-equivalence point?

    Exactly \( \mathrm{p}K_a \): there is as much acid form as basic form and the Henderson-Hasselbalch logarithm vanishes.

    Why does the equivalence point of acetic acid with sodium hydroxide give a basic pH?

    Because what is left is sodium acetate, and acetate is a weak base that hydrolyzes.

    What is a buffer solution?

    A mixture of a weak acid with its conjugate base, which resists changes in pH when acid or base is added.

    What is ammonium fluoride for in BOE?

    It is the conjugate base of HF: it buffers the pH and keeps the oxide etch rate constant as the acid is consumed.

    How is a hydrofluoric acid spill neutralized?

    With a calcium compound: the fluoride precipitates as CaF₂, which is insoluble —\( K_{sp} = 3.9\times10^{-11} \)— and harmless.

    What is the common-ion effect?

    That adding one of the ions of a sparingly soluble salt shifts the equilibrium and reduces its solubility.
