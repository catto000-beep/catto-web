    Transmission lines

    A quarter-wave length of cable turns 100 Ω into 25 Ω without a single component. At those frequencies, the length of the cable is part of the circuit.

    Characteristic impedance Reflection coefficient SWR Quarter wave Reflectometry

    01When a cable stops being a cable

    In circuit theory, a cable is a node: the voltage is the same at both of its ends at the same instant. That holds as long as the signal takes much less time to travel along it than it takes to change. When the length of the cable approaches an appreciable fraction of the wavelength —the rule of thumb is one tenth— or when the rise time of a pulse is comparable to the travel time, the cable becomes a transmission line: the signal travels along it as a wave, and what happens at one end depends on what is at the other. The page on lumped models in Circuit Theory I marked that limit.

    02The distributed model

    A short section of line is modeled with four parameters per unit length: the resistance \( R \) and the inductance \( L \) of the conductors, and the conductance \( G \) and the capacitance \( C \) between them. From these come the telegrapher's equations, which describe a wave traveling in both directions, with two parameters that characterize the line:

    \[ Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{R + j\omega L}{G + j\omega C}} \] \[ \gamma = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)} \] \( Z_0 \) is the characteristic impedance: the ratio between voltage and current of a wave traveling in a single direction. In a lossless line, \( Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C} \) is real and the velocity is \( 1/\sqrt{LC} \).

    In a coaxial cable, \( Z_0 = \dfrac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}} \ln \dfrac{b}{a} \), with \( a \) the radius of the inner conductor and \( b \) that of the shield: the impedance depends on the geometry and the dielectric, not on the length.

    03Reflection and standing waves

    If the line ends in a load \( Z_L \) different from \( Z_0 \), part of the wave is reflected, just like the plane wave at the boundary between two media:

    \[ \Gamma_L = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} \] \[ SWR = \frac{1 + |\Gamma_L|}{1 - |\Gamma_L|} \] The standing wave ratio (SWR; ROE in Spanish) is the ratio between the maximum and the minimum of the voltage along the line. With a matched load it is 1; with a short circuit or an open circuit, infinite.

    The incident and the reflected waves add with phases that depend on position, and the voltage along the line has fixed maxima and minima separated by half a wavelength. For the same reason, the impedance seen from the input depends on the length:

    \[ Z_{in} = Z_0\, \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan \beta \ell}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan \beta \ell} \] A half-wave section repeats the load; a quarter-wave section inverts it, \( Z_{in} = Z_0^2 / Z_L \): this is the quarter-wave transformer. A shorted or open section behaves as a pure reactance.

    Solving this expression by hand is tedious, and that is why the Smith chart exists, which turns it into a rotation on a graph.

    04Pulses and reflectometry

    In the time domain, a step entering the line travels to the load and comes back reflected with an amplitude \( \Gamma_L \) times the incident one, after a time equal to twice the delay of the section. If the source is not matched either, the reflection bounces back again, and the voltage settles in a staircase of echoes. Time-domain reflectometry (TDR) uses this principle to locate faults: the time of the echo gives the distance and its shape, the type of fault. This is how a break in a buried cable or a discontinuity in a board trace is found.

    06In the lab

    Activity 1 · Echoes in a coaxial cable

    With a fast pulse generator, 20 m of RG-58 and an oscilloscope at the input, observe the echo with the far end open, shorted and terminated in 50 Ω. Measure the delay and calculate the propagation velocity.

    Activity 2 · The quarter wave

    With a network analyzer, measure the input impedance of a section of coaxial cable terminated in 100 Ω and find the frequency at which it equals 25 Ω. Relate it to the length of the section.

    Activity 3 · The SWR of an antenna

    Measure the SWR of an amateur radio antenna with an SWR meter across its band and locate the frequency of best match.

    07Common mistakes

    • Treating as a node a cable that is long compared with the wavelength. The voltage changes along the cable.
    • Believing that \( Z_0 \) depends on the length. It depends only on the cross-section and the dielectric.
    • Measuring with a 1 MΩ probe at the end of an unterminated 50 Ω line. The pulse is reflected and the waveform lies.
    • Forgetting the velocity of the dielectric when calculating a quarter wave. In RG-58, the physical length is 66% of the electrical one.

    08Self-assessment

    What SWR does a 25 Ω load produce on a 50 Ω line?

    \( \Gamma = -1/3 \), SWR = 2.

    What impedance is seen a quarter wave away from a 100 Ω load on a 50 Ω line?

    \( 50^2 / 100 = 25 \) Ω.

    How long is a quarter wave of RG-58 at 100 MHz?

    \( 0.66 \times 3\text{ m} / 4 \approx 0.50 \) m.

    What is seen on a TDR if the cable is cut?

    An echo of the same polarity as the pulse (open circuit, \( \Gamma = +1 \)) at the time that corresponds to the distance of the break.
