    Microelectronic technology

    Quadrupling the area of a chip, from 100 to 400 mm², multiplies its cost by six: four times fewer fit on the wafer and, besides, a smaller proportion comes out good.

    Wafer Photolithography CMOS process Packaging Yield

    01From silicon to the wafer

    An integrated circuit is fabricated on a monocrystalline silicon wafer, sliced from an ingot grown from molten silicon by the Czochralski method. Current wafers are 200 or 300 mm in diameter and less than a millimeter thick, and are polished until the surface is flat on the atomic scale. The whole process takes place in clean rooms, because a particle the size of a transistor is enough to ruin a chip. Hundreds or thousands of identical chips are fabricated at once on each wafer.

    02The processes

    ProcessWhat it does
    PhotolithographyTransfers the pattern of a mask to a photosensitive resist, with 193 nm deep ultraviolet or 13.5 nm extreme ultraviolet light
    OxidationGrows silicon dioxide on the wafer, gate and isolation insulator
    Ion implantation and diffusionIntroduce dopants into defined areas to form n and p regions
    Deposition (CVD, PVD)Adds layers of polysilicon, dielectrics and metals
    EtchingRemoves material where the resist does not protect it; dry, with plasma, for vertical walls
    Chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP)Planarizes each layer so the next one can be stacked

    A CMOS process repeats those steps dozens of times: it forms the n and p wells, isolates the transistors with oxide trenches, grows the gate oxide and defines the gates, implants source and drain, and then builds on top eight to more than ten layers of copper interconnect separated by dielectrics. The first part, which makes the transistors, is the front end; the interconnect part, the back end. Besides CMOS there are bipolar processes, BiCMOS, silicon-on-insulator (SOI) and compound semiconductors such as gallium arsenide and gallium nitride. Circuit design for these processes was covered in analog integrated circuit design.

    03Testing, dicing and packaging

    Once the wafer is finished, each chip is tested with a probe card and the defective ones are marked. Then the wafer is diced and the good chips are packaged: they are bonded to a lead frame or substrate and connected with gold or copper wires (wire bonding) or flipped over onto solder balls (flip chip). Packages evolved from through-hole to surface mount and to maximum density:

    PackageCharacteristics
    DIPThrough-hole, leads every 2.54 mm
    SOIC, TSSOPSurface mount with leads on two sides
    QFPLeads on all four sides; many pins, but fragile
    QFNNo protruding leads, with a thermal pad underneath
    BGAArray of solder balls over the whole underside
    CSP and wafer-level packageThe size of the chip itself

    04Manufacturing yield

    Defects are spread at random over the wafer with a density \( D \) per unit area. A chip of area \( A \) comes out good if it has no defects at all; with a Poisson distribution, and with the Murphy model, which assumes a variable density and fits the data better:

    \[ Y_{Poisson} = e^{-DA} \] \[ Y_{Murphy} = \left(\frac{1 - e^{-DA}}{DA}\right)^2 \] The number of chips that fit on a wafer of diameter \( d \) is approximated by \( \pi (d/2)^2 / A - \pi d / \sqrt{2A} \): the second term discounts the incomplete chips at the edge. The cost of each good chip is that of the wafer divided by the number of good chips, and it grows much faster than the area.

    That is why large chips are divided into several smaller ones, chiplets, which are tested separately and mounted together in a single package.

    06Common mistakes

    • Thinking that the chip cost grows with the area. It grows faster, because the yield falls.
    • Forgetting the edge of the wafer. Large chips waste more surface at the perimeter.
    • Confusing the technology node with a physical measurement. Today the node name no longer matches any dimension of the transistor.
    • Choosing the package by size alone. It decides the heat dissipation, the lead inductance and whether the joints can be inspected.

    07Self-assessment

    How many 100 mm² chips fit on a 300 mm wafer?

    About 640.

    With 0.1 defects per cm², what yield does the Poisson model give for that chip?

    \( e^{-0.1} \approx 0.905 \).

    What is chemical-mechanical polishing for?

    To planarize each layer so the next one can be stacked with lithographic precision.

    What advantage does a BGA have over a QFP?

    Many more leads in less area, short connections and better heat dissipation; in exchange, the joints can only be inspected with X-rays.
