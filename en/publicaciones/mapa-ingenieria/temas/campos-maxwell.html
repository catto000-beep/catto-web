    Electromagnetic fields for links: Maxwell in practice

    At 1 GHz the current in a copper wire flows in the first 2 microns of its surface, and the energy does not travel through the copper but through the insulator around it.

    Phasor form Boundary conditions Skin effect Surface resistance Poynting vector

    01From physics to the link

    Maxwell's equations already appeared in Physics II, on the page on Maxwell's equations, as the synthesis of all electricity and magnetism. In Transmission Media they are used for a concrete purpose: carrying a signal from one point to another, through the air, along a cable, through a waveguide or along a fiber. Every medium in this course is a solution of the same four equations with different boundary conditions.

    Since communication signals are almost always sinusoidal, or sums of sinusoids, it is convenient to write the equations in phasor form, where the time derivative is replaced by \( j\omega \):

    \[ \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -j\omega\mu\, \mathbf{H} \] \[ \nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + j\omega\varepsilon\, \mathbf{E} \] \[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho \] \[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0 \] With \( \mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E} \) in a conducting medium, the second equation becomes \( \nabla \times \mathbf{H} = (\sigma + j\omega\varepsilon)\,\mathbf{E} \): the conduction current and the displacement current compete.

    02Conductor or dielectric: it depends on the frequency

    The ratio between the conduction current and the displacement current, \( \sigma/\omega\varepsilon \), decides how a material behaves. If it is much greater than 1, the material is a good conductor; if it is much less, a good dielectric. The same material can be both, depending on the frequency:

    Seawater is the classic example: at low frequencies it is a conductor that does not let waves through —which is why submerged submarines communicate on very low frequencies and with enormous antennas—, and above a gigahertz it behaves as a lossy dielectric.

    03Boundary conditions

    What distinguishes a waveguide from a line or an antenna is the surfaces that bound it. At the boundary between two media, Maxwell's equations impose:

    ComponentBetween two dielectricsOn a perfect conductor
    Tangential \( \mathbf{E} \)ContinuousZero
    Tangential \( \mathbf{H} \)Continuous (no surface current)Equal to the surface current
    Normal \( \mathbf{D} \)Continuous (no surface charge)Equal to the surface charge
    Normal \( \mathbf{B} \)ContinuousZero

    The condition \( E_{\text{tan}} = 0 \) on the metal is the one that fixes the modes of a waveguide: only the fields that vanish at the walls survive.

    04The skin effect

    Inside a good conductor, an alternating field decays exponentially from the surface. The current concentrates in a layer of characteristic thickness \( \delta \), the penetration depth (skin depth):

    \[ \delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f \mu \sigma}} \] \[ R_s = \frac{1}{\sigma \delta} \] In copper, \( \delta \) is 9.3 mm at 50 Hz, 66 µm at 1 MHz and 2.1 µm at 1 GHz. \( R_s \) is the surface resistance, in ohms per square.

    The consequences are everywhere: the resistance of a wire grows with frequency, Litz wire —many thin insulated strands twisted together— is used in radio-frequency coils and in switching power supplies, high-frequency conductors are silver-plated because only the surface matters, and a shield a few \( \delta \) thick attenuates almost the entire field.

    05Where the energy travels

    The power density carried by an electromagnetic field is the Poynting vector:

    \[ \mathbf{S} = \tfrac{1}{2}\, \mathrm{Re}\{ \mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}^* \} \] In watts per square meter, time-averaged for sinusoidal fields.

    Applied to a coaxial cable, it gives a surprising result: the electric field runs from the center conductor to the shield and the magnetic field encircles the center conductor, so the power travels through the dielectric between the two, not through the copper. The conductors guide the energy and take the losses, but the energy is carried by the field. This is the idea that connects transmission lines with waveguides and antennas.

    07In the lab

    Activity 1 · Resistance versus frequency

    With an LCR meter, measure the series resistance of a piece of 1 mm copper wire at 1 kHz, 10 kHz and 100 kHz, and compare it with the lab value. Repeat with Litz wire of the same cross-section.

    Activity 2 · A shield that works or does not

    With a coil driven by a generator and another one as a pickup, measure the signal with a 0.1 mm aluminum foil between the two at 1 kHz, 100 kHz and 1 MHz. Explain the results with the penetration depth.

    Activity 3 · Dielectric constant of a board

    Measure the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor formed by the two copper faces of an FR-4 board and deduce its relative permittivity.

    08Common mistakes

    • Using the DC resistance at high frequency. The skin effect multiplies it.
    • Classifying a material as a conductor or a dielectric without looking at the frequency. It is decided by \( \sigma/\omega\varepsilon \).
    • Thinking that the power travels through the copper. It travels through the field, between the conductors.
    • Shielding against low-frequency magnetic fields with a thin foil. A thickness of several \( \delta \) is needed, or a magnetic material.

    09Self-assessment

    What is the penetration depth of copper at 100 kHz?

    \( 66 \text{ µm} \times \sqrt{10} \approx 209 \) µm.

    At what frequency does seawater stop behaving as a conductor?

    When \( \sigma = \omega\varepsilon \): \( f = 4/(2\pi \times 81 \varepsilon_0) \approx 890 \) MHz.

    What condition does a perfect conductor impose on the electric field?

    That its tangential component be zero at the surface.

    Why are radio-frequency conductors silver-plated?

    Because the current flows in a surface layer of thickness δ, and it is enough for that layer to be a good conductor.
