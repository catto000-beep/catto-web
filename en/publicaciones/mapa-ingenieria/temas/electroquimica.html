    Electrochemistry

    That aluminum wire is not spliced to copper is no electrician’s whim: it is two volts of difference in the table of potentials, and with moisture that is a cell that eats the aluminum.

    Redox Anode and cathode Nernst Galvanic corrosion Batteries

    01Electrons that change owners

    All of electrochemistry is an oxidation-reduction reaction in which the electrons are forced to pass through a wire instead of being handed over directly. That is the only difference between a nail that rusts and a battery: the path.

    Occurs inWhat happensElectrodeSign in a galvanic cell
    OxidationThe species loses electronsAnodeNegative
    ReductionThe species gains electronsCathodePositive

    The rule for not getting lost: oxidation always occurs at the anode, in a galvanic cell and in an electrolytic cell. What does get reversed between the two is the sign of the terminal, and that is where the most common confusion in the subject comes from.

    Galvanic cell and electrolytic cell

    In a galvanic cell the reaction is spontaneous and generates current: the anode is the negative terminal. In an electrolytic cell an external source forces a reaction that would not occur on its own —electroplating, anodizing, electrolysis— and there the anode is the positive terminal. Oxidation is still at the anode in both cases.

    02Electrode potentials and the Nernst equation

    Each half-reaction is assigned a standard potential measured against the hydrogen electrode, which by convention was assigned zero. The EMF of the cell is the difference, and it is always built by subtraction: cathode minus anode.

    \[ E^{\circ}_{\text{cell}} = E^{\circ}_{\text{cathode}} - E^{\circ}_{\text{anode}} \qquad E = E^{\circ} - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log Q \] The Nernst equation at 25 °C, with \( n \) the electrons exchanged and \( Q \) the reaction quotient. When the cell runs down, \( Q \) reaches \( K \) and the EMF falls to zero: that is a discharged battery.
    Lab · build a cell

    Choose the two metals and the cell solves itself: which one is oxidized, which one is reduced and how much voltage it delivers. The concentration control applies Nernst. Try the zinc–copper pair, which is the Daniell cell.

    03Galvanic corrosion: the cell nobody meant to build

    Two different metals in electrical contact with moisture between them form a cell, whether you want it or not. The less noble one becomes the anode and corrodes, and the greater the potential difference, the faster it happens.

    Pair in contact\( \Delta E^{\circ} \)What happens
    Aluminum with copper2.00 V Very bad: the aluminum gets eaten away. That is why aluminum wire is not spliced to copper without a bimetallic connector
    Steel with copper0.78 VBad: the steel pits at the contact
    Tin with copper0.48 VTolerable, and it is the pair of every solder joint on a trace
    Gold over nickelProtected That is why a gold-plated contact has a nickel barrier underneath

    The same mechanism is used to advantage: a sacrificial anode of zinc or magnesium connected to a steel structure corrodes in its place. It is galvanic corrosion directed on purpose against a metal that does not matter.

    Electroplating, which is the same chemistry in reverse

    The copper traces of a multilayer board and the columns that connect its layers are made by electroplating copper from a sulfate solution. The amount deposited is set by Faraday's law: \( m = \frac{QM}{nF} \), with \( F = 96485 \) coulombs per mole of electrons. The thickness is controlled by the current and the time, and by nothing else.

    04Batteries

    A battery is a cell with the reaction chosen to give the voltage and the energy that are needed. The voltage comes from the difference of potentials; the capacity, from how much mass of reactant there is.

    SystemVoltageWhy
    Alkaline Zn/MnO₂1.5 VNot rechargeable; it is the reaction from the stoichiometry unit
    Lead-acid2.0 V per cellRechargeable and cheap; six cells give the 12 V of a car
    Nickel–metal hydride1.2 VRechargeable, robust, with less energy per kilogram
    Lithium-ion3.6 VLithium is the most electropositive metal and the lightest: both at once

    The last row explains why lithium prevailed. With \( E^{\circ} = -3.04 \) V it is the end of the table of potentials, so each cell delivers more than twice the voltage of an alkaline one; and since it is also the lightest solid metal, the energy per kilogram has no competition. It also explains its danger: that same reactivity is what makes a damaged cell catch fire.

    05In the lab

    Activity 1 · The Daniell cell

    Build the zinc–copper cell with a salt bridge and measure the open-circuit EMF. Compare with the theoretical 1.10 V and explain the difference. Then measure with a load and discuss the drop due to internal resistance.

    Activity 2 · Nernst in action

    Repeat the measurement, diluting the copper sulfate to one-tenth and one-hundredth. Plot the EMF against the logarithm of the concentration and verify the slope of 0.0592/n volts per decade.

    Activity 3 · Copper-plating a part

    Electroplate copper onto a clean steel part with controlled current. Weigh before and after, calculate the expected mass with Faraday's law and obtain the current efficiency.

    06Common mistakes

    • Believing that the anode is always the positive one. It is in the electrolytic cell; in the galvanic cell it is the negative one. Oxidation, however, is always at the anode.
    • Adding the potentials of the two half-reactions. They are subtracted: cathode minus anode.
    • Multiplying \( E^{\circ} \) when balancing. The potential is intensive; the coefficients do not change it.
    • Confusing voltage with capacity. One comes from the potentials, the other from the mass of reactant.
    • Putting metals that are far apart in the table in contact. That is a cell, and the less noble one corrodes.
    • Forgetting the salt bridge. Without it the solutions become charged and the current stops right away.

    07Self-assessment

    Where does oxidation occur?

    At the anode, always: in a galvanic cell and in an electrolytic cell. What changes between the two is the sign of the terminal.

    What is the EMF of the Daniell cell?

    1.10 V: \( +0.34 \) of copper minus \( -0.76 \) of zinc.

    What happens to the EMF when the cell runs down?

    \( Q \) grows until it equals \( K \) and the EMF falls to zero. That is a discharged battery.

    Why is aluminum not spliced to copper?

    Because 2.00 V separates them in the table: they form a cell and the aluminum, which is the anode, corrodes.

    What does Faraday's law say?

    That the mass deposited is \( m = QM/nF \): it depends only on the charge that passed, with \( F = 96485 \) C/mol.

    Why does lithium give so much energy per kilogram?

    Because it is the most electropositive metal in the table and also the lightest solid: a lot of voltage and little mass.
