    Semiconductor physics

    Every topic in this subject left a piece ready for this one: the photon, tunneling, Fermi-Dirac statistics, the Pauli principle. Here they all come together in the band gap and the Fermi level, which is where a diode comes from.

    Band gap Effective mass Law of mass action Fermi level Silicon

    01From atomic levels to bands

    The unit on atomic models ended with a pending idea: when \( 10^{22} \) atoms come together in a crystal, Pauli forces the single atomic level of each one to split into as many levels as there are atoms, so close together that they form a continuous band of allowed energies. Between two allowed bands there can be a band gap, of width \( E_g \), where no possible state exists. All the electricity of a material depends on how this scheme is arranged.

    MaterialBands\( E_g \)Example
    ConductorThe valence band is partially filled, or overlaps the conduction band—Copper, aluminum
    SemiconductorFull valence band, narrow band gap: thermal agitation is enough to cross it0.5 to 1.5 eVSilicon 1.12 eV · germanium 0.66 eV · gallium arsenide 1.42 eV
    InsulatorFull valence band, wide band gap: almost nobody crosses it> 4 eVDiamond, 5.5 eV

    The difference between a semiconductor and an insulator is not one of nature, it is one of the size of \( E_g \) compared with \( kT \): at room temperature, \( kT \approx 26 \) meV, an \( E_g \) of 1.1 eV still lets a fraction —small, but not zero— of electrons cross on their own.

    02The effective mass

    Inside a crystal, an electron feels not only the external force: it also feels the periodic potential of all the nuclei. The net result can be summarized, for most calculations, in a single number: the effective mass \( m^* \), which replaces \( m_e \) in \( F=m^*a \) and makes the electron in a crystal behave like a free particle, but lighter or heavier depending on the curvature of its energy band.

    Material\( m^*_e / m_e \) (electrons)\( m^*_h / m_e \) (holes)
    Silicon0.260.39
    Germanium0.120.30
    Gallium arsenide0.0670.45

    The electron in gallium arsenide weighs, for practical purposes, fifteen times less than a free one. It responds faster to an electric field and reaches a higher velocity before colliding with the lattice: that is why GaAs is used in the highest-frequency transistors, even though it is more expensive and harder to work with than silicon.

    03Intrinsic carriers and the law of mass action

    In a pure crystal —with no added impurity—, thermal agitation moves some electrons from the valence band to the conduction band. Each electron that crosses leaves behind a hole: an empty state that behaves, for almost all purposes, like a mobile positive charge. The concentration of each one, in that pure material, is the intrinsic carrier concentration \( n_i \).

    \[ n_i \propto T^{3/2} e^{-E_g/2kT} \qquad\qquad n\,p = n_i^{\,2} \] The second equality, the law of mass action, always holds in thermal equilibrium, doped or not: if something makes \( n \) go up, \( p \) goes down to keep the product constant. It is the most widely used equation in all of device physics.

    04Doping and the Fermi level

    Adding impurities —phosphorus to donate electrons, boron to accept them, in silicon— does not change \( n_i \), which depends only on the material and the temperature. What changes is how far \( n \) departs from \( p \), and with that, where the Fermi level falls relative to the midpoint of the band gap, \( E_i \).

    \[ E_F - E_i = kT \ln\!\left(\frac{n}{n_i}\right) \] n-type doping raises \( E_F \) toward the conduction band; p-type doping lowers it toward the valence band. A typical extrinsic semiconductor, with \( 10^{16} \) donors per cm³ in silicon, raises \( E_F \) about 0.35 eV above \( E_i \): much closer to the conduction band than to the middle.

    The same calculation explains why a semiconductor device stops working if it is heated too much: \( n_i \) grows exponentially with \( T \), and a point is reached —the intrinsic temperature— at which it exceeds the doping concentration. From then on the material forgets that it was doped and behaves as intrinsic: germanium, with its narrow band gap, reaches that temperature well before silicon, and that is one of the reasons germanium practically disappeared from modern electronics.

    05The end of the bridge

    This unit closes the subject where its curriculum description promised: in silicon. Each earlier topic contributed a piece that this last one uses without explaining it again. The photon from the photoelectric effect unit is the light that a photodiode converts into current by crossing this very band gap. The tunneling of the barrier is the leakage through the gate oxide of a MOSFET. The Fermi-Dirac statistics of the previous unit is, quite literally, the formula \( n = N_c e^{-(E_c-E_F)/kT} \) that was just used in the lab on this page. And the Pauli exclusion principle, two units back, is the ultimate reason bands exist instead of isolated levels.

    From here on, everything rests on this

    A diode is a junction between an n-doped region and a p-doped one, with their Fermi levels forced to equalize at equilibrium. A bipolar transistor is two such junctions, back to back. A MOSFET controls, with an electric field, how many carriers are available in a region of the crystal. None of those three sentences makes sense without the band gap, the Fermi level and the law of mass action on this page.

    06In the lab

    Activity 1 · Verifying the law of mass action

    With the lab on this page, for n-type doped silicon at \( 10^{17} \) cm⁻³, note down \( n \) and \( p \). Verify by hand that \( n\,p = n_i^2 \) and calculate how many orders of magnitude separate the majority carriers from the minority ones.

    Activity 2 · The intrinsic temperature

    Raise the temperature until \( n_i \) of germanium approaches \( 10^{16} \) cm⁻³. Repeat for silicon. Compare the two temperatures and explain why germanium diodes fail sooner with heat.

    Activity 3 · Where the Fermi level falls

    Calculate \( E_F - E_i \) for p-type doped silicon at \( 10^{15} \), \( 10^{17} \) and \( 10^{19} \) cm⁻³. Plot it against the logarithm of the concentration and verify that the relationship is linear.

    07Common mistakes

    • Thinking that doping changes \( n_i \). \( n_i \) depends only on the material and the temperature; doping changes \( n \) and \( p \) separately, not their product.
    • Forgetting the law of mass action when calculating the minority carrier. Once the majority carrier is known, the minority one follows directly from \( p = n_i^2/n \).
    • Confusing effective mass with the actual mass of the electron. It is a parameter that summarizes the effect of the crystal, and it can be larger, smaller or —in some bands— even negative.
    • Believing that the Fermi level is an occupied energy level. In a semiconductor it typically falls right in the band gap, where there is no state at all.
    • Ignoring the temperature dependence of \( n_i \) when designing a circuit. It is exponential, and it is the cause of a good share of thermal failures in power electronics.
    • Thinking that germanium is inferior to silicon in everything. Its higher \( n_i \) is a thermal disadvantage, but its narrow band gap also gives a lower forward voltage drop, useful in some signal diodes.

    08Self-assessment

    What distinguishes a semiconductor from an insulator?

    The size of the band gap \( E_g \): narrow in the semiconductor (thermal agitation crosses it), wide in the insulator.

    What does the effective mass represent?

    The net effect of the periodic potential of the crystal on the electron, summarized in a single number that replaces the actual mass in \( F=m^*a \).

    What does the law of mass action say?

    That the product \( n\,p = n_i^2 \) is constant in thermal equilibrium, for a given material and temperature, doped or not.

    What happens to the Fermi level with n-type doping?

    It rises from the midpoint of the band gap toward the conduction band.

    Why can a semiconductor device stop working if it is heated too much?

    Because \( n_i \) grows exponentially with temperature and, past the intrinsic temperature, exceeds the doping concentration: the material stops behaving as extrinsic.

    Why is gallium arsenide used in high-frequency transistors?

    Because its electron effective mass is much lower than that of silicon, so its electrons respond faster to an electric field.
