    Classical and quantum statistical mechanics

    The classical theory of electrons in a metal was off by almost two orders of magnitude, and it took twenty-seven years to learn why: Pauli forbids almost all of them from moving. The Fermi level that comes out of that is the central idea of the unit that closes this subject.

    Fermi-Dirac Fermi level Bose-Einstein Maxwell-Boltzmann Thermionic emission

    01Why a statistical physics is needed

    A cubic centimeter of copper has on the order of \( 10^{23} \) free electrons. Neither Newtonian mechanics nor the Schrödinger equation is designed to keep track of each one: what is needed is a description that says, instead, what fraction of those electrons occupies each available energy level. That is statistical mechanics, and the previous unit left open the question that makes it different for electrons than for anything else: the Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons from sharing the same state.

    02The classical distribution and why it fails for electrons

    Before Pauli, Drude (1900) treated the electrons of a metal as a classical gas: distinguishable particles, with no limit on how many share a state, distributed according to Maxwell-Boltzmann.

    \[ f_{MB}(E) = A\,e^{-E/kT} \] The same distribution that describes the velocities of the molecules of an ideal gas. Applied to the electrons of a metal, it predicts that each one should contribute \( \tfrac32 k \) to the heat capacity, just like a gas molecule.

    The prediction fails by a factor close to one hundred: the electrons of a real metal contribute much less heat capacity than the classical model demands. The error was not one of detail, it was conceptual, and it took twenty-seven years to resolve.

    03Fermi-Dirac: what Pauli imposes

    Sommerfeld solved the problem in 1927 by applying Pauli to the letter. If two electrons cannot share a state, most of them are “trapped” far below the top of the energy range: they cannot absorb a little thermal energy because the neighboring state, just slightly higher, is already occupied. Only the electrons near the top have empty states available nearby to jump into.

    \[ f_{FD}(E) = \frac{1}{e^{(E-E_F)/kT} + 1} \] \( E_F \), the Fermi level, is the energy where the occupation probability is exactly 1/2, at any temperature. At \( T=0 \) this function is a perfect step: everything occupied up to \( E_F \), everything empty above. At \( T>0 \) it smooths out over a band about \( 4kT \) wide.
    Lab · Fermi-Dirac versus Maxwell-Boltzmann

    You choose the metal —it changes \( E_F \)— and the temperature. The Fermi-Dirac curve looks almost vertical because \( kT \) is tiny compared with \( E_F \): that is, in a single image, the reason for the error of the classical model.

    04Bose-Einstein: when sharing a state is not forbidden

    Photons —and in general integer-spin particles, the bosons— do not obey Pauli: any number of them can occupy exactly the same state, and in fact they tend to do so. That is Bose-Einstein statistics, and it has already appeared twice in this subject without being named: Planck's blackbody law is the Bose-Einstein distribution applied to the photons of a cavity, and a Bose-Einstein condensate —the ultracold helium atom from the de Broglie topic— is the extreme case, with a macroscopic fraction of atoms in the same quantum state.

    Three statistics, one origin

    Maxwell-Boltzmann, Fermi-Dirac and Bose-Einstein share the same exponential form in the high-energy limit —far from \( E_F \), Fermi-Dirac reduces to the classical one— and differ only in how they treat the possibility of sharing a state: forbidden for fermions, free for bosons, and the question does not even arise if the particles are distinguishable. Which one to use depends exclusively on the spin of the particle being counted.

    05Where it appears in electronics

    Everything a metal or a semiconductor does —conduct, or insulate— depends on where \( E_F \) falls relative to the allowed bands, and on how many electrons the Fermi-Dirac tail has above that band. The next and last unit of this subject rests entirely on this.

    \[ J = A T^2 e^{-\varphi/kT} \] The Richardson-Dushman equation: the thermionic emission current of a hot cathode, with \( A \approx 1.2\times10^6 \) A/(m²K²). It is the high-energy Fermi-Dirac tail —the few electrons with \( E \gg E_F \)— that manages to escape from the metal. It is the mechanism that makes a vacuum tube, or the valve of an old amplifier, conduct.

    The same reasoning, applied to a semiconductor instead of a metal, is the next unit: how many electrons cross the band gap into the conduction band depends on where \( E_F \) is and on the Fermi-Dirac tail at the working temperature.

    07Common mistakes

    • Applying Maxwell-Boltzmann to the electrons of a metal. It is the correct statistics for a dilute classical gas, not for degenerate fermions.
    • Believing that \( E_F \) is the maximum energy an electron can have. At \( T>0 \) there is always a fraction, a small one, above \( E_F \).
    • Thinking that \( f_{FD}(E_F) \) depends on temperature. It is always 1/2, by definition of the Fermi level.
    • Confusing the Fermi level with an actual occupied energy level. It can fall right in a band gap, where there is no state at all —which is exactly what happens in an intrinsic semiconductor.
    • Using Bose-Einstein statistics for electrons, or Fermi-Dirac for photons. The spin of the particle decides which one applies, without exceptions.
    • Forgetting that Fermi-Dirac and Maxwell-Boltzmann coincide far from \( E_F \). Classical statistics is not “wrong”: it is the limit of the quantum one when the occupation is low.

    08Self-assessment

    Why does Drude's classical theory predict the heat capacity of the electrons in a metal poorly?

    Because it assumes that any electron can absorb thermal energy freely, when Pauli prevents almost all of them from doing so: only those near \( E_F \) have empty states available.

    What does the Fermi level \( E_F \) represent?

    The energy where the Fermi-Dirac occupation probability is exactly 1/2, at any temperature.

    What does \( f_{FD}(E) \) look like at \( T=0 \)?

    Like a perfect step: occupation 1 for all \( EE_F \).

    What distinguishes Bose-Einstein from Fermi-Dirac?

    That bosons do not obey the exclusion principle: any number can occupy the same state, whereas fermions are forbidden to.

    What becomes of the Planck blackbody distribution, seen from this unit?

    It is Bose-Einstein statistics applied to the photons of a thermal cavity.

    What part of the Fermi-Dirac distribution explains thermionic emission?

    The high-energy tail, far above \( E_F \), where \( f_{FD} \) already resembles the classical exponential: the few electrons there have enough energy to escape from the metal.
