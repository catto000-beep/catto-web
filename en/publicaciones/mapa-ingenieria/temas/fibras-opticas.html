    Optical fibers and fiber transmission

    At 10 Gb/s, a multimode fiber has power to spare for 2 km, but dispersion cuts the link at 300 m. On a single-mode fiber, the same transmitter reaches tens of kilometers.

    Numerical aperture Single-mode and multimode Windows Dispersion Link budget

    01Light through a thread of glass

    An optical fiber is a dielectric waveguide that works at frequencies of about 200 THz. It has a silica glass core surrounded by a cladding of slightly lower refractive index, and the light is trapped in the core. Compared with a copper cable it has three decisive advantages: it loses 0.2 dB per kilometer instead of tens of dB, it supports terahertz bandwidths, and it is not affected by electromagnetic interference or by ground potential differences between its ends.

    That is why almost all of the world's data traffic travels over fiber: the submarine cables between continents, the backbones between cities, the connection of cellular base stations and, more and more, the last stretch to every home.

    02Total internal reflection and numerical aperture

    A ray that travels along the core and reaches the cladding at an angle greater than the critical angle is entirely reflected, without loss, as seen in geometric optics. This limits the angle at which light can enter through the face of the fiber and be guided:

    \[ NA = \sin\theta_a = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2} \] The numerical aperture NA sets the acceptance cone of the fiber. With \( n_1 = 1.48 \) in the core and \( n_2 = 1.46 \) in the cladding, NA = 0.24 and the acceptance half-angle is 14°.

    A large aperture makes it easier to couple the light from a cheap source but, as will be seen shortly, it allows more distinct paths inside the fiber.

    03Multimode and single-mode

    As in a metal waveguide, only certain field configurations propagate in the fiber, the modes. How many fit is given by the normalized frequency, which combines the core radius \( a \), the aperture and the wavelength:

    \[ V = \frac{2\pi a}{\lambda}\, NA \] With \( V < 2.405 \) a single mode propagates: the fiber is single-mode. With a large V, a step-index fiber supports about \( V^2/2 \) modes.

    With an 8.2 µm core and NA = 0.12, V is 2.36 at 1310 nm: the fiber is single-mode above about 1285 nm, its cutoff wavelength. The 50 µm multimode fiber at 850 nm has V ≈ 37 and hundreds of modes.

    04Attenuation and windows

    Light is lost through absorption by the glass and its impurities, and above all through Rayleigh scattering: microscopic fluctuations in the density of the glass deflect the light with an intensity proportional to \( 1/\lambda^4 \). Between 850 and 1550 nm this loss drops by a factor of 11. Toward the far infrared the absorption of the silica itself grows, and between the two lies the minimum, near 1550 nm.

    WindowTypical lossSource and fiber

    1310 nmAbout 0.35 dB/kmLaser on single-mode; almost zero chromatic dispersion

    To the loss of the fiber are added that of each fusion splice (about 0.1 dB) and that of each connector (up to 0.5 dB), and bends that are too tight, which let light escape from the core.

    05Dispersion: the pulse broadens

    A light pulse arrives wider than it left, and if it broadens too much it overlaps the next one. There are two main causes:

    • Modal dispersion, in multimode fibers: each mode travels a different path and arrives at a different moment. In a step-index fiber with \( n_1 - n_2 = 0.02 \), the difference is about 67 ns per kilometer. Modern multimode fibers are graded-index: the index drops toward the edge of the core, the modes that travel along the outside go faster and the difference shrinks by orders of magnitude. It is specified with the bandwidth–distance product: 2000 MHz·km in an OM3 at 850 nm.
    • Chromatic dispersion, in all of them: the velocity depends on the wavelength, and no source emits just one.
    \[ \Delta t = D \cdot L \cdot \Delta\lambda \] \( D \) is the dispersion coefficient of the fiber, which in a G.652 is almost zero at 1310 nm and about 17 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm. With 50 km and a source 0.1 nm wide in spectrum, the pulse broadens by 85 ps.

    At 10 Gb/s each bit lasts 100 ps, so chromatic dispersion limits long links at 1550 nm. A 10 Gb/s transmitter without compensation tolerates an accumulated dispersion \( D \cdot L \) of the order of 1000 ps/nm, about 60 km of G.652. Beyond that, compensating fibers or coherent receivers that correct it by digital processing are used.

    06Emitters, receivers and the link budget

    The source is an LED, a VCSEL or a semiconductor laser, and the receiver, a PIN or avalanche photodiode, as seen in optoelectronics. The design of a link starts with a power budget in decibels, just like the one for power in lines: all the losses are subtracted from the transmitter power, and what arrives has to exceed the receiver sensitivity with a margin, typically 3 dB, for aging and future repairs.

    Lab · the budget of a fiber link

    The optical power in dBm along the link: two 0.5 dB connectors, a splice every 2 km (the length of a reel) and the slope of the fiber attenuation. The receiver is a PIN with the typical sensitivity for the chosen speed. The values are typical catalog values.

    08In the lab

    Activity 1 · Numerical aperture

    Illuminate a multimode fiber with a red laser, project the output cone onto a screen and measure its diameter at a known distance. Calculate the half-angle and the numerical aperture.

    Activity 2 · Loss budget

    With a source and an optical power meter, measure the loss of a reel of fiber, of a connector and of a splice. Build the budget of a link and compare it with the end-to-end measurement.

    Activity 3 · Bends

    Wind a single-mode fiber around mandrels of decreasing diameter and measure the increase in loss at 1310 and at 1550 nm. Explain why 1550 nm is more sensitive.

    09Common mistakes

    • Mixing multimode and single-mode. A single-mode transceiver on a multimode fiber, or the reverse, loses tens of dB or suffers modal dispersion.
    • Looking at the tip of a connected fiber. Radiation at 1310 and 1550 nm is invisible and can damage the retina.
    • Forgetting dispersion in the budget. At 10 Gb/s over multimode, the power would be enough for kilometers but modal dispersion limits it to a few hundred meters.
    • Dirty connectors. A dust particle on an 8 µm core adds decibels; they are cleaned and inspected before every connection.

    10Self-assessment

    What is the numerical aperture of a fiber with \( n_1 = 1.48 \) and \( n_2 = 1.46 \)?

    \( \sqrt{1.48^2 - 1.46^2} \approx 0.24 \), with an acceptance half-angle of 14°.

    Is a fiber with an 8.2 µm core and NA = 0.12 single-mode at 1310 nm?

    Yes: \( V = 2\pi \cdot 4.1 \cdot 0.12 / 1.31 \approx 2.36 < 2.405 \).

    How much arrives from a 0 dBm transmitter through 40 km of fiber at 0.25 dB/km with 1 dB of connectors?

    \( 0 - 10 - 1 = -11 \) dBm.

    Why do 10 Gb/s links over multimode reach only about 300 m?

    Because of modal dispersion: with 2000 MHz·km and about 6.7 GHz of required bandwidth, the maximum distance is 0.3 km, much less than the power would allow.
