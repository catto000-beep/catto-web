    Antennas and radio links

    For a 30 km link over flat terrain it is not enough for the antennas to see each other: at the midpoint, the Earth rises 13 m and the Fresnel zone has a radius of 14 m. The towers need about 22 m.

    Gain Effective area Friis equation Fresnel zone Fade margin

    01What defines an antenna

    An antenna is the transducer between a transmission line and space. The radiation unit covered radiation resistance, the pattern and directivity; to design a link you also need:

    ParameterWhat it tells you
    Gain \( G = e \cdot D \)The directivity after subtracting the losses of the antenna; given in dBi
    BeamwidthThe angle between the directions where the power falls to half (−3 dB)
    Impedance and bandwidthThe range of frequencies where the SWR is acceptable, typically less than 1.5 or 2
    PolarizationThe orientation of the electric field; the two antennas of the link have to match
    Front-to-back ratioHow much less it radiates backward than forward

    An antenna works the same for transmitting as for receiving: by reciprocity, the pattern and the gain are the same. In reception, the gain translates into an effective area:

    \[ A_e = \frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi}\, G \] The power delivered by the antenna is the incoming power density multiplied by its effective area. A half-wave dipole at 146 MHz has 0.55 m²; the same dipole at 2.4 GHz, 270 times less.

    02Antennas in common use

    AntennaTypical gainUse
    λ/4 monopole over a ground plane5.2 dBi, 36 ΩAM broadcasting (the tower is the monopole), mobile units, VHF radios
    Half-wave dipole2.15 dBi, 73 ΩReference; element of larger antennas
    Yagi-Uda8 to 15 dBiTelevision, VHF and UHF links
    Patch on a board6 to 9 dBiGPS, Wi-Fi, cellular telephony arrays
    Horn10 to 25 dBiFeed for parabolic dishes, measurement standard
    Parabolic dish of diameter \( D \)\( e\,(\pi D/\lambda)^2 \), 30 to 45 dBiMicrowave and satellite links

    The monopole takes advantage of the image formed by the ground plane: it behaves as half a dipole, with half the resistance and twice the directivity, because it radiates only over the upper half-space. The parabolic dish concentrates the energy in a beam of approximate width \( 70°\, \lambda / D \): a 0.6 m dish at 11 GHz has 34 dBi and a 3° beam, and it has to be aimed with that precision.

    03The Friis equation

    Between two antennas in free space, separated by a distance \( d \) much greater than their size, the received power is:

    \[ P_r = P_t\, G_t\, G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2 \] \[ L_{fs} = 32.45 + 20\log f_{MHz} + 20\log d_{km} \] The factor in parentheses is the free-space loss, which the second expression gives in decibels. It is not an absorption: it is the power spreading over an ever larger sphere, seen through the effective area of an isotropic antenna.

    In decibels, the link is built by adding and subtracting, just like the budget of a line or that of a fiber: transmitter power, minus cables, plus gains, minus path loss. What arrives is compared with the receiver threshold, which starts from thermal noise, −174 dBm per hertz at room temperature, and adds the bandwidth, the noise figure and the signal-to-noise ratio that the modulation requires. The difference is the fade margin, the reserve for rain and for changes in propagation.

    04Line of sight and Fresnel zones

    A microwave link needs more than just seeing the other antenna: the energy travels through a volume around the direct ray. The first Fresnel zone is the ellipsoid where the indirect path is up to half a wavelength longer than the direct one. Its radius at a point \( d_1 \) and \( d_2 \) from the ends is:

    \[ r_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda\, d_1 d_2}{d_1 + d_2}} \] The design criterion is to keep at least 60% of the first zone free of obstacles. Over 30 km at 11 GHz, the radius at the center is 14 m.

    The most common obstacle on a long link is the Earth itself. The refraction of the atmosphere bends the rays downward, and the effect is modeled with an effective Earth radius of 4/3 the real one. Even so, at the center of a 30 km link the ground rises 13 m above the straight line joining the bases of the towers: the antennas have to be about 22 m high to clear 60% of the zone over flat terrain.

    07In the lab

    Activity 1 · Measuring the gain

    With two identical antennas facing each other at a known distance in the far field, measure the attenuation between them with a generator and an analyzer. Solve for the gain of each from the Friis equation.

    Activity 2 · The pattern of a Yagi

    Rotate a UHF Yagi on an axis in 10° steps facing a fixed transmitter and plot the pattern. Measure the beamwidth and the front-to-back ratio.

    Activity 3 · Designing a real link

    With a terrain profile obtained from an elevation map, design a 5.8 GHz link between two points in the city: tower height, antennas and margin.

    08Common mistakes

    • Believing that free-space loss depends on frequency by itself. It grows with frequency because the effective area of an antenna of fixed gain shrinks; with antennas of fixed area, the link improves as the frequency goes up.
    • Designing with line of sight but without clearing the Fresnel zone. The link works in the test and drops out with changes in refraction.
    • Forgetting polarization. Two crossed antennas lose 20 dB or more.
    • Leaving no margin. An 18 GHz link with no margin drops out in the first storm.

    09Self-assessment

    What is the free-space loss over 30 km at 11 GHz?

    \( 32.45 + 20\log 11\,000 + 20\log 30 \approx 142.8 \) dB.

    What gain does a 0.6 m parabolic dish have at 11 GHz with 55% efficiency?

    \( 0.55\,(\pi \cdot 0.6 / 0.0273)^2 \approx 2630 \), that is, 34.2 dBi.

    How much does the received power change if the distance is doubled?

    It drops 6 dB, to a quarter.

    Why does a λ/4 monopole have 36 Ω and not 73 Ω?

    Because with its image in the ground plane it forms a half-wave dipole, but only the upper half radiates: the same current delivers half the power.
