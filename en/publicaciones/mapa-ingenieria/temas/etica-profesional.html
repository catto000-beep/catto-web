    Ethics in professional practice

    Almost all professional ethics violations are legal until someone gets hurt. Codes exist so that the decision is made beforehand.

    Deontology Code of ethics Conflict of interest Professional secrecy Ethics tribunal

    01Ethics, morality and deontology

    Morality is the set of convictions of a person or a community about what is right and what is wrong. Ethics is the reflection on those convictions: why something is right, how to decide when two duties collide. Professional deontology is ethics applied to a profession, written into concrete rules that a professional community gives itself and that a disciplinary tribunal can enforce.

    The Engineering and Society page on the profession and ethics introduced the concepts and the gender perspective. Here we work with the codes in force and with cases.

    02Codes of ethics

    In national jurisdiction, the code of ethics for surveying, architecture and engineering was approved by Decree 1099/84 and is applied by professional councils such as COPITEC. Each provincial association has its own, with very similar content. Internationally, the IEEE code is the reference for the field, and it begins with the same principle as all of them: putting the safety, health and welfare of the public first.

    Duties to…Examples
    SocietyNot signing work that puts people or the environment at risk; warning the authority when a danger is not corrected
    The professionNot lending your signature; not practicing outside your own competence; keeping up to date
    ColleaguesNot displacing another professional by offering lower fees; not replacing them on a job without notifying them; not criticizing them without grounds
    The client or employerLoyalty, professional secrecy, candidly reporting the risks and the limits of the work, avoiding conflicts of interest

    Breaches are judged before the ethics or disciplinary tribunal of the council or association, with penalties ranging from a warning to suspension or revocation of the license.

    03Conflict of interest

    A conflict of interest exists when a personal interest can influence, or appear to influence, the professional judgment that others expect to be impartial. The engineer who writes the specification for a tender and is a partner of one of the bidders; the one who recommends a supplier that pays them a commission; the expert witness who has a relationship with one of the parties. The general rule is simple: disclose it and, if it compromises impartiality, step aside. Hiding it turns an awkward situation into a breach.

    04Cases where the legal and the ethical do not coincide

    Most of the serious ethical failures in the history of engineering were not illegal at the time they were decided. Some cases studied around the world:

    CaseWhat happenedThe lesson
    Therac-25 (1985-1987)A radiotherapy machine with software errors delivered lethal overdoses; hardware safeguards had been removed in reliance on the softwareSafety software needs the same review as hardware, and failure reports must be investigated
    Challenger (1986)The engineers at the rocket manufacturer warned that the seals failed in cold weather; the decision to launch was made by managementThe engineer has to stand by their technical judgment under pressure from the organization
    Volkswagen emissions (2015)Software detected homologation tests and changed how the engine ranCheating on a test is a design decision, and someone writes it
    Dilemmas · what would you do?

    Three situations of the kind that come up in the professional life of an electronic engineer. You choose a response and the analysis appears according to the codes of ethics: there is not always a perfect option, but there are options that a disciplinary tribunal would judge.

    05Professional secrecy

    What a professional learns through their work —a client’s design, their processes, their failures— is confidential. Revealing it without just cause is an ethical breach and can be a crime (Article 156 of the Código Penal, the Criminal Code). Secrecy yields to a greater duty: if keeping it puts people’s lives or safety at risk, the duty to the public prevails.

    06Where it shows up in electronics

    SituationThe duty at stake
    A client asks you to skip an electromagnetic compatibility test to meet a deadlineTo the public and to the truth of what is being certified

    07Activities

    Activity 1 · Read your own code

    Obtain the code of ethics of the association or council of your jurisdiction and compare it with the IEEE’s: which duties do they share and which appear in only one.

    Activity 2 · Analyze a case

    Read a public report on the Therac-25 case and list the engineering decisions that, had they been made differently, would have prevented the accidents.

    Activity 3 · Write a dilemma

    Write a realistic ethical dilemma from the field of electronics with three courses of action and analyze them against the code of ethics.

    08Common mistakes

    • Believing that what is legal is sufficient. Many serious ethical breaches were legal.
    • Staying silent about a conflict of interest hoping it goes unnoticed. Disclosing it almost always solves it; hiding it makes it worse.
    • Lending your signature to an acquaintance. It is one of the breaches that disciplinary tribunals penalize most.
    • Thinking that responsibility lies with the company. The ethical duty is personal.

    09Self-assessment

    What is professional deontology?

    Ethics applied to a profession, written into concrete rules that a disciplinary tribunal can enforce.

    What principle heads the engineering codes of ethics?

    Putting the safety, health and welfare of the public first.

    What should you do in the face of a conflict of interest?

    Disclose it and, if it compromises impartiality, step aside.

    When does professional secrecy yield?

    When keeping it puts people’s lives or safety at risk.

    10Further reading

    • Nancy G. Leveson and Clark S. Turner. An Investigation of the Therac-25 Accidents. IEEE Computer, 1993. The classic analysis of the case, essential reading for anyone who designs equipment with software.
