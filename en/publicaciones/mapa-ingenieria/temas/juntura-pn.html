    PN junction and diodes

    A 1000 V diode and a 3 V Zener diode are the same PN junction with different doping. What decides how much voltage it withstands is the less doped side, not the more doped one.

    Depletion region Shockley equation Junction capacitance Breakdown Reverse recovery

    01The junction at equilibrium

    When a P region is joined to an N region, holes from the P side diffuse into the N side and electrons from the N side into the P side, following the concentration gradient described in the previous unit. Each carrier that crosses leaves behind an uncompensated fixed ion: negative acceptors on the P side, positive donors on the N side. That fixed charge creates an electric field that opposes diffusion, and the process stops when the drift current produced by the field exactly equals the diffusion current.

    What remains is a strip almost empty of carriers, the depletion region or space-charge region, and between its edges a potential difference, the built-in potential:

    \[ V_{bi} = V_T \ln \frac{N_A\, N_D}{n_i^2} \] With \( N_A = 10^{17} \), \( N_D = 10^{16} \) cm⁻³ and \( n_i = 10^{10} \) cm⁻³, \( V_{bi} \approx 0.774 \) V. It cannot be measured with a voltmeter: the contact potentials at the metal terminals exactly compensate it.

    The Electronics Technician program page on semiconductors and diodes tells the same story without equations; here it is quantified.

    02The width of the depletion region

    Solving Poisson's equation with uniform charge on each side (the depletion approximation), the field is triangular, with its maximum at the junction, and the potential grows as two parabolas. The total charge on each side has to be the same, so the region extends further into the less doped side:

    \[ W = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\varepsilon_s\,(V_{bi} - V)}{q} \left( \frac{1}{N_A} + \frac{1}{N_D} \right)} \] \[ N_A\, x_p = N_D\, x_n \qquad E_{\max} = \frac{2\,(V_{bi} - V)}{W} \] \( V \) is the applied voltage, positive in forward bias. \( \varepsilon_s = 11.7\,\varepsilon_0 \) in silicon.

    With the doping levels of the example and no applied voltage, \( W \approx 0.33 \) µm, of which 0.30 µm lie on the N side. With 10 V in reverse, \( W \) grows to 1.24 µm and the maximum field reaches \( 1.7 \times 10^5 \) V/cm. Almost all real junctions are asymmetric, with one side much more heavily doped than the other: the depletion region lies entirely in the lightly doped side, and its doping sets the breakdown voltage and the capacitance.

    03The diode equation

    A forward voltage lowers the barrier, and diffusion wins out over drift: minority carriers are injected on each side. At the edge of the depletion region the minority-carrier concentration rises by the factor \( e^{V/V_T} \), and from there it decays with the diffusion length, exactly the exponential profile of the continuity equation. The current is the diffusion of that excess, and adding the two sides gives the Shockley equation:

    \[ I = I_S \left( e^{V / n V_T} - 1 \right) \] \[ I_S = q\,A\,n_i^2 \left( \frac{D_p}{L_p\,N_D} + \frac{D_n}{L_n\,N_A} \right) \] \( n \) is the ideality factor: 1 when diffusion dominates, close to 2 when recombination inside the depletion region matters, at low currents.

    With the previous example, \( \tau = 1 \) µs on both sides and an area of 1 mm², \( I_S \approx 6 \times 10^{-14} \) A: the diode conducts 1 µA at 0.43 V, 1 mA at 0.61 V and 100 mA at 0.73 V. Each decade of current costs \( V_T \ln 10 \approx 60 \) mV, which is why the “0.6 V” of the Electronics Technician program is such a good approximation over such a wide range. 89 % of that current is carried by holes injected into the N side: the current is dominated by the less doped side, something the bipolar transistor exploits so that the emitter injects almost only electrons.

    Temperature

    \( I_S \) is proportional to \( n_i^2 \), which grows very fast with temperature: at constant current, the diode voltage drops about 2 mV per degree. This is the basis of integrated temperature sensors and the cause of thermal drift in amplifiers.

    04The two capacitances of the diode

    The junction stores charge in two different ways, and each one dominates in one bias condition:

    The exponent \( 1/2 \) is that of an abrupt junction; in a graded junction it is \( 1/3 \), and hyperabrupt varactors use special doping profiles so that the capacitance changes more with voltage.

    05Reverse breakdown

    In reverse bias only about \( I_S \) flows, until the maximum field reaches a critical value, on the order of \( 3 \) to \( 5 \times 10^5 \) V/cm in silicon. There are two mechanisms:

    • Avalanche: carriers gain enough energy from the field to knock loose new pairs when they collide, and the multiplication runs away. It dominates in lightly doped junctions, with breakdown above about 7 V, and its voltage rises with temperature.
    • Zener effect: with very high doping the depletion region is so thin that electrons cross it by tunneling. It dominates below about 5 V and its voltage falls with temperature.

    Between 5 and 6 V the two coefficients cancel: that is why the reference diodes with the best stability sit in that range. In an asymmetric junction, the avalanche breakdown voltage is set by the doping of the lightly doped side:

    \[ BV \approx \frac{\varepsilon_s\, E_c^2}{2\, q\, N} \] With \( N = 10^{16} \) cm⁻³ and \( E_c = 4 \times 10^5 \) V/cm, \( BV \approx 52 \) V. For a power diode to withstand 1000 V, its lightly doped region has to be doped close to \( 10^{14} \) cm⁻³ and be tens of microns thick.

    06Switching and reverse recovery

    A forward-biased diode has the charge of the injected minority carriers stored in it. If the voltage is suddenly reversed, the diode keeps conducting in reverse until that charge is removed: first with a reverse current limited by the circuit during the storage time, and then decaying as the depletion region re-forms:

    \[ t_s \approx \tau \ln \left( 1 + \frac{I_F}{I_R} \right) \] With \( \tau = 1 \) µs and \( I_R = I_F \), \( t_s \approx 0.69 \) µs. That is why ordinary rectifier diodes are not suitable at high frequency.

    The small-signal resistance is remarkable: at 1 mA it is 25.85 Ω, whatever the diode, because it depends only on the current and the temperature. The same idea gives the transconductance of the bipolar transistor in the next unit.

    09Common mistakes

    • Believing that the built-in potential can be measured. The metal contacts compensate it.
    • Assuming that the depletion region is split equally. It extends into the less doped side.
    • Treating the 0.7 V drop as a constant of silicon. It depends on the current, the area and the temperature.
    • Forgetting reverse recovery in a switching power supply. The slow diode conducts in reverse on every cycle and heats the transistor.
    • Confusing the two capacitances. The junction capacitance is the reverse-bias one; the diffusion capacitance, the forward-bias one.

    10Self-assessment

    What equilibrium is reached in a junction with no applied voltage?

    The diffusion current of each carrier equals the drift current produced by the field of the depletion region, and the net current is zero.

    In a P⁺N junction, on which side does almost the entire depletion region lie?

    On the N side, the less doped one, because the charge on each side has to be equal.

    How much does the voltage of a diode increase if the current is multiplied by one hundred?

    \( V_T \ln 100 \approx 119 \) mV, with \( n = 1 \).

    Why does a Schottky diode switch faster than a junction diode?

    Because it conducts with majority carriers and does not accumulate minority-carrier charge that has to be removed when the voltage is reversed.

    How does the capacitance of an abrupt junction change when \( V_{bi} - V \) is multiplied by four?

    It is halved, because the width \( W \) grows with the square root.
