    Regression analysis

    Four data sets can have the same line, the same R² and the same mean, and look nothing alike. That is the Anscombe quartet, and it is the reason a residual plot is not optional.

    Least squares Calibration R² Residuals Anscombe

    01The line of best fit

    Measuring a sensor’s output at several known temperatures gives a cloud of points, not a line. Regression looks for the line that best represents them, and “best” has a precise definition: the one that minimizes the sum of the squares of the vertical distances.

    \[ \hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x \qquad b_1 = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})^2} \qquad b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1\bar{x} \] The vertical distances are minimized because the error is assumed to be in \( y \) and \( x \) to be well known. That asymmetry matters: the line of \( y \) on \( x \) is not the same as that of \( x \) on \( y \).

    In electronics this is calibration: you apply known values, record what the instrument reads and fit the line that converts one into the other. The intercept is the offset and the slope is the gain, which are exactly the two parameters that a datasheet specifies.

    02How well it fits

    \[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i-\bar{y})^2} \qquad\qquad r = \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}} \] The coefficient of determination \( R^2 \) is the fraction of the variability of \( y \) that the line explains. The correlation coefficient \( r \) is its signed root, and measures the linear association between the two variables: it equals 1 or −1 with perfectly aligned points and 0 with no linear relationship.
    A high \( R^2 \) guarantees nothing

    It is the most widespread mistake in all of applied statistics. An \( R^2 \) of 0.95 can come from a relationship that is not linear, from a single point that drags the line, or from a cloud with no structure plus one distant value. The Anscombe quartet, which is in the lab below, consists of four data sets with the same mean, the same standard deviation, the same line and the same \( R^2 \), and with shapes that look nothing alike. It is the same argument as the histogram in the first unit: you have to look at the data.

    Lab · regression and residuals

    Go through the four Anscombe sets and compare their statistics: they are identical. The residual plot below is what gives them away, and it is the first thing to look at in any fit.

    03Residuals are the diagnostic

    The residual of each point is what the line did not explain: \( e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i \). Plotting them against \( x \) is the most useful diagnostic tool there is, because if the linear model is correct no structure should remain in them: just even noise around zero.

    The residuals showIt meansWhat to do
    A shapeless cloud around zeroThe linear model works wellNothing
    A curveThe relationship is not linearAdd a quadratic term or transform
    A funnel that opens upThe spread grows with \( x \)Weight the fit, or work with the logarithm
    A very distant pointOutlier or influential pointInvestigate it before deciding
    Correlation is not causation, and extrapolating is making things up

    Two different things, and both are common. Two variables being correlated does not mean one causes the other: there may be a third behind them, or pure coincidence. And a line fitted between 0 and 100 °C says nothing about what happens at 300 °C: outside the measured range, the model has no backing from any data. Extrapolating a calibration is one of the most common ways of introducing a large error with a lot of confidence.

    04In the lab

    Activity 1 · Calibrating a sensor

    Measure the output of a signal-conditioned thermistor at eight known temperatures. Fit the line, obtain the gain and offset, and calculate \( R^2 \). Plot the residuals and decide whether the linear model is sufficient.

    Activity 2 · The influential point

    Repeat the fit from the previous activity adding a point measured with a gross error. Compare slope, intercept and \( R^2 \) with and without it, and evaluate how far a single data point moved it.

    Activity 3 · Linearizing

    Take the nonlinear curve of a thermistor and plot \( \ln R \) against \( 1/T \). Fit the line on the transformed variables, obtain the constant \( B \) and verify that the residuals now have no structure.

    05Common mistakes

    • Trusting a high \( R^2 \) without looking at the data. The Anscombe quartet exists for that.
    • Not plotting the residuals. It is the cheapest and most informative diagnostic.
    • Extrapolating outside the measured range. There the model has no data to support it.
    • Reading causation into a correlation. There may be a third variable behind it.
    • Forgetting that the line of \( y \) on \( x \) is not that of \( x \) on \( y \). Vertical distances are minimized.
    • Adding terms until \( R^2 \) goes up. With enough parameters you can fit anything, including the noise.

    06Self-assessment

    What does the least-squares method minimize?

    The sum of the squares of the vertical distances between the points and the line.

    What does \( R^2 \) represent?

    The fraction of the variability of \( y \) that the model explains.

    What is the Anscombe quartet?

    Four data sets with the same line, the same \( R^2 \) and the same mean, and completely different shapes. The proof that you have to plot the data.

    What should a correct residual plot show?

    Nothing: even noise around zero, with no structure.

    What are the gain and the offset of a calibration?

    The slope and the intercept of the fitted line.

    Why should you not extrapolate?

    Because outside the measured range no data support the model, and the error can be large without anything warning you.
