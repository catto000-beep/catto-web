    Residues applied to real integrals

    There are real integrals with no elementary antiderivative that come out in two lines if you take them off the real axis and close them in the complex plane. Among other things, that is where the noise bandwidth of a filter comes from.

    Residues Jordan’s lemma Principal value Noise bandwidth Fourier

    01A real integral, solved from the outside

    Some real integrals have no elementary antiderivative and yet come out in two lines if you take them off the real axis and close them in the complex plane. The maneuver is always the same:

    1. The real variable is replaced by a complex one and an arc is added to close the path.
    2. The residue theorem is applied to the closed contour.
    3. It is shown —with the ML bound— that the arc contributes zero as its radius grows.
    4. What remains is the integral being sought.
    \[ \underbrace{\int_{-R}^{R} f(x)\,dx}_{\text{what is sought}} \;+\; \underbrace{\int_{\Gamma_R} f(z)\,dz}_{\to\,0} \;=\; 2\pi j \sum_{\text{poles above}} \operatorname{Res} f \]

    The third step is the one that needs care, and it is the one the lab lets you see: if the integrand does not decay fast enough, the arc does not vanish and the whole method collapses.

    02First type: integrals of rational functions

    For \( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \dfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\,dx \) with \( Q \) having no real roots, the condition is that the denominator wins by at least two degrees:

    \[ \operatorname{deg}(Q) \ge \operatorname{deg}(P) + 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \left|\int_{\Gamma_R} f\,dz\right| \le \frac{C}{R^2}\cdot \pi R \xrightarrow[R \to \infty]{} 0 \]

    The lead example is the simplest possible one. With \( f(z) = 1/(1+z^2) \), the only pole in the upper half-plane is at \( z = j \), with residue \( 1/(2j) \):

    \[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = 2\pi j \cdot \frac{1}{2j} = \pi \] Which is the same value the antiderivative, \( \arctan x \), gives when evaluated between \( -\infty \) and \( \infty \).

    The appeal shows up when the antiderivative does not exist. With \( 1/(1+x^4) \) no arctangent will do, but the poles in the upper half-plane are \( e^{j\pi/4} \) and \( e^{j3\pi/4} \), and the computation is just as short: the result is \( \pi/\sqrt{2} \).

    04Second type: Fourier integrals

    These are the ones that appear when transforming. They have the form \( \int R(x)\,e^{j a x}\,dx \) and the exponential factor changes the analysis of the arc, because \( |e^{jaz}| = e^{-a y} \) decays for \( y \gt 0 \) when \( a \gt 0 \). The result that formalizes this is Jordan’s lemma:

    \[ \lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\Gamma_R} R(z)\,e^{jaz}\,dz = 0 \quad\text{if } a \gt 0 \text{ and } |R(z)| \to 0 \text{ on the arc} \] With \( a \gt 0 \) you close above; with \( a \lt 0 \), below. It is enough for the rational function to tend to zero; two degrees are not required.

    With that, the transform of a Lorentzian pulse comes out in one line:

    \[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{jax}}{1 + x^2}\,dx = 2\pi j \cdot \frac{e^{ja\cdot j}}{2j} = \pi\,e^{-a}, \qquad a \gt 0 \]

    And taking the real part, \( \int \cos(ax)/(1+x^2)\,dx = \pi e^{-a} \), which is the one in the lab. This family is the one that justifies the title of this topic in the curriculum: they are the Fourier integrals, and the residue method is the standard way of solving them.

    05Third type: integrals of sines and cosines

    For \( \int_0^{2\pi} R(\cos\theta, \operatorname{sin}\theta)\,d\theta \) the trick is different and more elegant: there is no need to invent an arc, because the path is already closed. Setting \( z = e^{j\theta} \), the interval \( [0, 2\pi] \) traces exactly the unit circle:

    \[ \cos\theta = \frac{z + z^{-1}}{2}, \qquad \operatorname{sin}\theta = \frac{z - z^{-1}}{2j}, \qquad d\theta = \frac{dz}{jz} \]

    Applied to a specific case, \( \int_0^{2\pi} \dfrac{d\theta}{2 + \cos\theta} \), it becomes a contour integral with two poles at \( z = -2 \pm \sqrt{3} \), of which only one falls inside the circle, and the result is \( 2\pi/\sqrt{3} \). No trigonometric substitutions or tables.

    When there is a pole on the path

    \( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \dfrac{\operatorname{sin} x}{x}\,dx \) has a problem at \( x = 0 \). The way out is to go around it with a tiny semicircle and take the principal value. A simple pole surrounded by half an arc contributes \( j\pi \) times its residue —half of \( 2\pi j \)— and that is where the value \( \pi \) comes from. That is the result behind the overshoot in the step response of an ideal filter: the Gibbs phenomenon.

    06Where it is used in electronics

    ProblemIntegralWhat it gives
    Inverse transform\( \frac{1}{2\pi j}\int F(s)e^{st}ds \)The time response, pole by pole
    Noise bandwidth\( \int_0^{\infty} |H(j\omega)|^2 d\omega \)The total noise at the output of a filter
    Signal energy\( \int |X(j\omega)|^2 d\omega \)Parseval, from the frequency side
    Kramers-KronigPrincipal value over the axisThe real part of a response from the imaginary part

    The second row is the most everyday one. The equivalent noise bandwidth of a first-order low-pass filter comes from integrating \( 1/(1 + (\omega/\omega_c)^2) \) from zero to infinity, which is half of the integral of the first example on this page. The result, \( B_n = \frac{\pi}{2}f_c \), says that a real filter lets through 57 % more noise than an ideal one of the same width, and it is the number used to compute the noise of an amplifier.

    07In the lab

    Exercise 1 · The four roots

    Solve \( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \dfrac{dx}{1+x^4} \) by locating the four poles, keeping the two in the upper half-plane and adding their residues. Check that it gives \( \pi/\sqrt{2} \approx 2.221 \) and compare it with a numerical integration.

    Exercise 2 · The noise bandwidth, measured

    Build an RC low-pass filter with \( f_c = 1 \) kHz, inject white noise and measure the RMS voltage at the output. Compute the equivalent bandwidth from the measurement and compare it with \( \frac{\pi}{2}f_c = 1571 \) Hz.

    Exercise 3 · The trigonometric integral

    Solve \( \int_0^{2\pi} \dfrac{d\theta}{a + \cos\theta} \) for \( a \gt 1 \) with the substitution \( z = e^{j\theta} \). Check that the result, \( 2\pi/\sqrt{a^2-1} \), tends to infinity when \( a \to 1 \), and explain what happens to the pole.

    08Common mistakes

    • Closing the contour without checking the arc. It is the step that makes everything else valid; the lab shows a case where it fails.
    • Adding up the residues of all four poles. When you close above, only those in the upper half-plane count.
    • Closing above with \( a \lt 0 \). In Fourier integrals the sign of the exponent decides the half-plane.
    • Ignoring poles on the real axis. The contour has to be indented and the principal value kept.
    • Forgetting the \( 1/(jz) \) when substituting \( d\theta \) in trigonometric integrals.

    09Self-assessment

    What condition on the degrees does the first type require?

    That the denominator exceed the numerator by at least two degrees, so that the arc contributes zero.

    What is \( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dx/(1+x^2) \) and why?

    \( \pi \). The only pole above is \( j \), with residue \( 1/(2j) \), and \( 2\pi j \cdot 1/(2j) = \pi \).

    What does Jordan’s lemma add?

    It allows the contour to be closed for integrands that decay more slowly, as long as there is a factor \( e^{jaz} \) with the right sign.

    How is \( \int_0^{2\pi} \) turned into a contour integral?

    With \( z = e^{j\theta} \): the interval traces the unit circle, the sines and cosines are written in terms of \( z \) and \( d\theta = dz/(jz) \).

    What does a simple pole surrounded by half an arc contribute?

    \( j\pi \) times its residue: half of what a full contour would contribute.

    What is the noise bandwidth of a first-order RC?

    \( B_n = \frac{\pi}{2}f_c \approx 1.57\,f_c \). It is the integral of \( |H|^2 \), which comes out by residues.
