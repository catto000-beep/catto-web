    Receivers

    A 200 kHz receiver with a 6 dB noise figure hears down to −105 dBm, but two neighbors at −45 dBm already fill its channel with intermodulation. The dynamic range lies between those two numbers.

    Superheterodyne Sensitivity Noise floor SFDR Blocking

    01The superheterodyne receiver and its successors

    A receiver has to find a weak signal among others that are much stronger. The superheterodyne, invented by Edwin Armstrong in 1918, solves it with a mixer that translates each channel to a fixed intermediate frequency, where a selective filter and an amplifier with automatic gain control do the work. Current integrated receivers also use direct conversion, which brings the channel down to baseband with an I/Q demodulator, or the low-IF approach, and do the channel filtering digitally. Whatever the architecture, three figures describe it: sensitivity, selectivity and dynamic range.

    02Sensitivity

    The weakest signal that the receiver can demodulate with the required quality is set by the thermal noise, the bandwidth and the noise figure of the chain, computed with the Friis formula from the introduction of Communication Systems:

    \[ P_{floor} = -174\ \mathrm{dBm/Hz} + 10\log B + NF \] \[ S_{min} = P_{floor} + SNR_{min} \] −174 dBm/Hz is kT at 290 K. A receiver with a 200 kHz bandwidth and a 6 dB noise figure has a floor of −115 dBm; if the demodulation needs 10 dB of signal-to-noise ratio, its sensitivity is −105 dBm.

    03Dynamic range and intermodulation

    At the upper end, the limit is set by strong signals. Two signals in neighboring channels generate third-order intermodulation products that can fall on the desired channel. Referred to the input, they grow 3 dB for each dB of the signals, and the line meets that of the fundamental at the input intercept point IIP3. The spurious-free dynamic range is the difference between the strongest signal that produces no intermodulation visible above the floor and that floor:

    \[ SFDR = \tfrac{2}{3}\,\big(IIP3 - P_{floor}\big) \] Improving the noise figure lowers the floor and improving the linearity raises IIP3: both enlarge the range, but a low-noise amplifier with a lot of gain at the front worsens the IIP3 of the whole receiver. It is the central trade-off in the design of the chain.

    04Selectivity and blocking

    Selectivity is the ability to reject a strong adjacent channel, and it is provided by IF or digital filters. But a very strong signal can cause trouble even if the filter rejects it: it compresses the input amplifier (blocking), or it mixes with the phase noise of the local oscillator and translates it onto the desired channel (reciprocal mixing). That is why the phase noise of oscillators is a receiver specification.

    05In the lab

    Activity 1 · Sensitivity

    Measure the sensitivity of an FM receiver by lowering the level of a generator until the audio signal-to-noise ratio falls to 12 dB SINAD, and compare it with the calculated one.

    Activity 2 · Intermodulation

    Apply two out-of-channel tones whose third-order product falls in the channel, and measure the level that makes it audible. Calculate IIP3.

    Activity 3 · Blocking

    With a desired signal near the sensitivity, raise a signal 1 MHz away until the desired one degrades by 3 dB.

    06Common mistakes

    • Maximizing the input gain. It improves the noise figure, but worsens the linearity of the whole receiver.
    • Forgetting the bandwidth in the sensitivity. A channel ten times wider has a floor 10 dB higher.
    • Ignoring the phase noise of the local oscillator. With strong neighboring signals, reciprocal mixing masks the weak one.
    • Confusing IIP3 with OIP3. They differ by the gain of the chain.

    07Self-assessment

    What noise floor does a receiver with a 1 MHz bandwidth and a 3 dB noise figure have?

    \( -174 + 60 + 3 = -111 \) dBm.

    With that floor and IIP3 = −5 dBm, what is the SFDR?

    \( \tfrac23 (-5 + 111) \approx 70.7 \) dB.

    What is reciprocal mixing?

    The translation of the local oscillator's phase noise onto the desired channel, caused by a strong neighboring signal.

    What advantage does direct conversion have?

    It has no image frequency and is easily integrated, with channel filtering at baseband.
