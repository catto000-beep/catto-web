    Environmental problems and their evolution

    The electronics industry cleaned its boards with the same compounds that punched a hole in the ozone layer. The Montreal Protocol phased them out, and it is considered the most successful environmental agreement.

    Scales Ozone layer Radiative forcing Climate sensitivity Planetary boundaries

    01From the smokestack to the planet

    Environmental problems have changed scale as human activity has grown. The first ones were local and visible; today’s are global, slow, and unevenly distributed between those who cause them and those who suffer them.

    ScaleExampleFeature
    LocalThe London smog of December 1952, which caused thousands of deaths in a few daysCause and effect in the same place and at the same time
    RegionalAcid rain in Europe and North America in the 1970s and 1980sThe emissions of one country damage the forests and lakes of another
    GlobalThe ozone hole and climate changeEveryone contributes and everyone is affected, with a delay of decades

    In Argentina there are examples at every scale: the pollution of the Matanza–Riachuelo basin, which led to the 2008 ruling of the Supreme Court in the Mendoza case; the loss of native forests in the Chaco, which led to the Forest Law 26,331, and the retreat of the glaciers, protected by Law 26,639. The chemical bases of pollution were covered in chemistry and the environment, in General Chemistry.

    02The ozone layer: a global problem half solved

    Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), used in refrigeration, aerosols and in cleaning electronic boards, release chlorine in the stratosphere and destroy the ozone that filters ultraviolet radiation. In 1985 the measurement of the hole over Antarctica was published, and in 1987 the Montreal Protocol was signed, which progressively phased out their production. It is considered the most successful environmental agreement: scientific assessments foresee the layer recovering by the middle of the century. The electronics industry had to change its cleaning solvents, which is why no-clean fluxes are used today.

    03Climate change

    Carbon dioxide, methane and other gases absorb the infrared radiation emitted by the Earth. As their concentration increases, the surface has to warm up in order to re-emit as much energy as it receives from the Sun. CO₂ went from about 280 parts per million before industrialization to more than 420 today. Its effect is measured with the radiative forcing, the energy imbalance it produces, and the response of temperature with the climate sensitivity:

    \[ \Delta F = 5.35 \ln \frac{C}{C_0}\ \ \mathrm{W/m^2} \] \[ \Delta T_{eq} = S\, \frac{\ln(C/C_0)}{\ln 2} \] \( C_0 \) = 280 ppm. \( S \) is the equilibrium climate sensitivity, the warming when CO₂ doubles; the IPCC estimates 3 °C as the best value, with a likely range of 2.5 to 4 °C. The forcing is logarithmic: each doubling adds the same amount.
    Lab · CO₂ concentration and equilibrium warming

    The equilibrium warming corresponding to CO₂ alone, for a given concentration and climate sensitivity. The band is the range of 2.5 to 4 °C per doubling; the dot, the chosen concentration.

    The warming observed so far, a little more than 1 °C, is different from the equilibrium warming: the oceans take decades to warm up, the aerosols from pollution cool, and other gases such as methane warm. But the logarithmic relationship and the sensitivity are what determine where the system is heading.

    04Planetary boundaries

    In 2009, a group of scientists led by Johan Rockström proposed nine planetary boundaries: processes of the Earth system with thresholds that, if crossed, can lead to abrupt changes. Besides climate and ozone, they include biodiversity loss, the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles, land and freshwater use, ocean acidification, aerosols and novel entities, such as plastics and synthetic compounds. Waste electrical and electronic equipment, which is covered in the unit on ICT and the environment, falls into this last category because of its metals and flame retardants.

    06Common mistakes

    • Confusing the ozone hole with climate change. They are different problems, with different causes and effects.
    • Expecting the warming to be proportional to the concentration. The forcing grows with the logarithm.
    • Reading the equilibrium warming as the current one. The ocean delays the response by several decades.
    • Thinking that a global problem has no local component. Its causes and its damages are local and unequal.

    07Self-assessment

    What forcing does doubling the preindustrial CO₂ produce?

    \( 5.35 \ln 2 \approx 3.7 \) W/m².

    With a sensitivity of 3 °C, what equilibrium warming corresponds to 420 ppm?

    \( 3 \cdot \ln 1.5/\ln 2 \approx 1.75 \) °C, from CO₂ alone.

    Which international agreement phased out CFCs?

    The Montreal Protocol, of 1987.

    Why is acid rain a regional problem?

    Because the emissions travel hundreds of kilometers and damage ecosystems far from the source, often in another country.
