    Electrical properties of matter

    Pure silicon and doped silicon are the same substance, and their resistivity differs by a factor of almost a million. The only thing that changed was one phosphorus atom for every ten million atoms.

    Resistivity Temperature coefficient Constantan Polarization Superconductivity

    01Twenty-four orders of magnitude

    No other physical property spans such a large range. Between silver and quartz there is a factor of \( 10^{24} \), and in the middle is silicon, which can be placed almost wherever you want.

    \[ \rho = \frac{RA}{L} \qquad\qquad \sigma = \frac{1}{\rho} \qquad\qquad \vec{J} = \sigma\vec{E} \] The last one is Ohm's law in local form, which is the one that really holds: the \( V = IR \) version comes from integrating it over a conductor of uniform cross-section.

    The fourth and fifth rows are the same substance. Between them there is a factor of almost a million, and the only thing that changed is one phosphorus atom for every five million silicon atoms. That is the property that makes electronics possible, and no other family of materials has it.

    02What temperature does to it

    Here metals and semiconductors behave in opposite ways, and the reason lies in what limits conduction in each case.

    CarriersOn heatingResult
    MetalFixed: one or two per atom The lattice vibrates more and scatters them\( \rho \) rises
    SemiconductorFew, and they have to be created Thermal agitation promotes many more\( \rho \) drops, exponentially
    \[ \rho(T) = \rho_{20}\left[1 + \alpha\,(T - 20)\right] \] For metals, over a moderate range. The coefficient \( \alpha \) of copper is \( 3.93\times10^{-3} \) per degree: the resistance of a copper coil rises almost 40% between 20 and 120 °C, and this is used to measure the temperature of a motor without fitting a sensor.

    Materials that deliberately do not change

    Constantan —copper with nickel— has an almost zero temperature coefficient, about twenty parts per million per degree, hence its name. That is why it is used in standard resistors, in measurement shunts and in strain gauges: you need the resistance to change with what you want to measure and with nothing else. Nichrome shares that virtue and also withstands red heat, which is why it is used in ovens and toasters.

    03On the insulator side

    An insulator has no free charges, but its molecules do respond to the field: they polarize. There are three mechanisms and they act on different time scales, which explains why permittivity depends on frequency.

    PolarizationWhat movesUp to what frequency it responds
    ElectronicThe electron cloud inside the atomOptical: \( 10^{15} \) Hz
    IonicThe ions within the latticeInfrared: \( 10^{13} \) Hz
    OrientationPolar molecules that rotateMicrowave: \( 10^{10} \) Hz

    The third row is the microwave oven: at 2.45 GHz water molecules can still rotate following the field, and that forced rotation against the friction of their neighbors is what heats. And it is also the reason the permittivity of water falls from 80 at DC to 1.8 at optical frequencies: above a certain frequency the molecules can no longer follow the field.

    That lag between the field and the polarization is what produces dielectric losses, which are reported as \( \tan\delta \). In an FR-4 laminate they are about 0.02, and that is why beyond a few gigahertz it is no longer usable: the trace turns into a heater. Radio-frequency boards use materials with a \( \tan\delta \) ten times lower.

    04The other extreme

    Below a critical temperature, some materials lose all resistance. It is not a very small resistance: it is exactly zero, and a current set flowing in a superconducting ring keeps going for years without decaying.

    Material\( T_c \)Cooled with
    Aluminum1.2 KLiquid helium
    Niobium-titanium9.3 KLiquid helium; it is the one in MRI magnets
    Ceramic YBCO93 KLiquid nitrogen, which is cheap

    The third row changed everything in 1987: going from needing helium to being able to use liquid nitrogen, which costs less than milk, moved superconductors from the laboratory to application. Today there are superconducting cables in some urban power grids, and the Josephson junction —two superconductors separated by a very thin barrier— is the basis of the primary voltage standard and of a good part of quantum computing.

    06Common mistakes

    • Confusing resistance with resistivity. One depends on the geometry; the other belongs to the material.
    • Believing every material increases its resistance when heated. Semiconductors and carbon do the opposite.
    • Using the linear formula for \( \alpha \) far from 20 °C. It is an approximation valid over a moderate range.
    • Assuming permittivity is a constant. It depends on frequency, and strongly.
    • Measuring low resistances with two probes. The leads and the contacts weigh more than what you want to measure: use four wires.
    • Thinking a superconductor has a very low resistance. It has zero, which is something else.

    07Self-assessment

    Why does the resistivity of a metal rise with temperature?

    Because the number of carriers is fixed and the lattice, vibrating more, scatters them more often.

    And why does that of a semiconductor fall?

    Because thermal agitation creates new carriers, and that effect dominates over scattering.

    What is constantan for?

    For standard resistors, shunts and gauges: its temperature coefficient is almost zero.

    Why does the microwave oven work at 2.45 GHz?

    Because at that frequency water molecules still rotate following the field, and that forced rotation heats.

    What does \( \tan\delta \) mean?

    The losses of the dielectric, due to the lag between the field and the polarization. In FR-4 it is about 0.02.

    Why was YBCO so important?

    Because its critical temperature of 93 K allows cooling it with liquid nitrogen, which is cheap, instead of helium.
