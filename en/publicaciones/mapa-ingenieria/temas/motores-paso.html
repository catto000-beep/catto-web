    Stepper motors

    At 300 rpm, a stepper motor driven at its rated voltage keeps 20% of its torque. With a chopper driver at 24 V it keeps more than 80%: the motor is the same; what changes is how the current rises.

    01Positioning without measuring

    A stepper motor turns in fixed increments, typically 1.8° (200 steps per revolution), each time the controller changes the current in its windings. If the load does not exceed its torque, the position is the number of steps sent: it is positioned open-loop, without an encoder. That makes it cheap and simple for 3D printers, small CNC machines, cameras and valves. The basic operation is presented in the technician program, in stepper motors.

    Microstepping improves smoothness, but not accuracy in the same proportion: the torque that holds a microstep against the load is small, and the actual position deviates from the ideal value.

    03Drive: why the torque falls with speed

    Each winding is an inductor with resistance, and the current takes time to establish itself with the time constant \( \tau = L/R \). At a few steps per second it reaches its rated value; at many, it reverses before getting there, and since the torque is proportional to the current, the motor loses strength. There are three ways to drive it:

    DriveHow it worksEffect
    Simple L/RThe rated winding voltage is appliedThe current rises slowly: little torque at high speed
    L/R with series resistorHigher voltage and a resistor that limits the final currentSmaller time constant, but the resistor dissipates power
    Constant-current chopperHigh voltage and an H-bridge that cuts off when the current reaches the set valueFast rise without dissipating: the current standard
    \[ i(t) = \frac{V}{R} + \left(i_0 - \frac{V}{R}\right) e^{-t/\tau} \] At each step change the current starts from its previous value and tends toward \( V/R \). With the chopper, \( V \) is several times larger than the winding’s rated voltage and the current is cut off at the set value: it rises with slope \( V/L \), much faster.

    04The torque-speed curve and step loss

    The manufacturer gives two curves. The pull-in curve indicates up to what speed the motor starts and stops without losing steps with a given load; the pull-out curve, how far one can go by accelerating gradually. Between the two, acceleration ramps are used. If the torque demanded exceeds the available one, the rotor lags more than half a step behind the field and loses steps: the controller thinks it is at one place and the motor is at another, without anyone noticing. At intermediate speeds a mechanical resonance also appears that can make it lose steps even with little load; microstepping and dampers attenuate it.

    06Common mistakes

    • Starting at the final speed without a ramp. The motor loses steps or does not start.
    • Setting the driver current above the rated value. The motor heats up, even if it gives more torque.
    • Trusting the position without a reference. A limit switch has to be found at power-up.
    • Powering the chopper with too little voltage. Without margin over the winding’s, it behaves like an L/R.

    07Self-assessment

    How many steps per second does a 1.8° motor make at 300 rpm in full step?

    \( 300/60 \cdot 200 = 1000 \) steps per second.

    What time constant does a 5 mH, 2 Ω winding have? And with 6 Ω in series?

    2.5 ms; with the resistor, \( 5/8 = 0.625 \) ms.

    How many microsteps per revolution does a 200-step motor give with 16 microsteps?

    3200.

    Why does a chopper need a high voltage?

    Because the rise slope of the current is V/L: the more voltage, the faster it reaches the set value.
