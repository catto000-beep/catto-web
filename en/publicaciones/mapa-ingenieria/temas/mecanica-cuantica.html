    Quantum mechanics: step, barrier and well

    An electron can appear on the other side of a wall that, according to all of classical physics, it should not be able to cross. That result is not a laboratory curiosity: it is the reason a chip cannot be thinned beyond a certain limit.

    Schrödinger Tunneling Infinite well Harmonic oscillator MOSFET

    01The equation that replaces Newton

    If a particle is also a wave, there must be an equation that governs that wave, just as Newton's second law governs a trajectory. That equation was written by Schrödinger in 1926, building directly on the de Broglie hypothesis from the previous topic. In its time-independent form:

    \[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\,\psi = E\,\psi \] \( \psi(x) \) is the wave function; \( |\psi|^2 \) gives the probability density of finding the particle at each point. Different shapes of \( V(x) \) —a step, a barrier, a well, a spring— give completely different behaviors, and they are the four cases of this topic.

    None of the four cases has an exact solution in classical mechanics. All four do have one with this equation, and all four end up being the model of a real device.

    02The step: reflection where there should be none

    A classical particle with energy \( E \) greater than a potential step \( V_0 \) always gets past it, only slower. The Schrödinger equation gives a different answer: part of the wave is reflected, exactly as light is partially reflected on changing medium.

    \[ R = \left(\frac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\right)^2 \qquad k = \frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V)}}{\hbar} \] An electron with \( E = 2 \) eV facing a step of \( V_0 = 1 \) eV is reflected 2.9 % of the time, even though it has more than enough energy to get past. Classically that 2.9 % is impossible.

    It is the same mechanism, in miniature, that makes an electron slow down and partly bounce back on entering a region with a different potential inside a crystal: every interface in a semiconductor device is, in this sense, a step.

    03The barrier: tunneling

    If the step has a finite width \( L \) and then drops again, the most radical of the four results appears: with \( E < V_0 \) —which classical physics forbids without exception— the particle has a nonzero probability of appearing on the other side. The wave function does not cut off abruptly inside the barrier: it decays exponentially, and if the barrier is narrow, some wave is still left when it ends.

    \[ T \approx e^{-2\kappa L} \qquad \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar} \] \[ \kappa[\text{Å}^{-1}] \approx 0.512\sqrt{V_0-E\;[\text{eV}]} \] The formula is valid for “opaque” barriers (\( \kappa L \gg 1 \)), which is the case for all the real devices on this page. \( T \) falls exponentially with \( L \): that is why one angstrom of difference changes the tunneling current by an order of magnitude.

    04The infinite well and the harmonic oscillator: quantized energies

    Confining a particle between two infinite walls —a well of width \( L \)— requires the wave function to vanish at the edges, just like a guitar string fixed at both ends. That allows only certain wavelengths, and therefore only certain energies.

    \[ E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}, \quad n = 1,2,3,\ldots \] An electron confined to 1 nm —the size of a quantum dot— has \( E_1 = 0.376 \) eV and \( E_2 = 1.50 \) eV: the levels are not equally spaced, they grow as \( n^2 \). Note that not even \( n=1 \) gives zero energy: it is the same zero-point energy from the previous unit, seen from another model.

    The harmonic oscillator —a parabola-shaped potential, the model of any small vibration, from a spring to an atom in a crystal lattice— gives a different but equally quantized result:

    \[ E_n = \left(n+\tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega, \quad n = 0,1,2,\ldots \] Here the levels are equally spaced, each one \( \hbar\omega \) from the next. A typical optical phonon in silicon has \( \hbar\omega \approx 63 \) meV, with a zero-point energy of 31.5 meV that the lattice never loses, not even at absolute zero.

    05Where it appears in electronics

    DeviceWhat it usesConsequence
    Scanning tunneling microscope (STM)Tunneling through vacuum Atomic resolution: the current changes by an order of magnitude per angstrom
    Advanced-node MOSFETTunneling through the gate oxide Set a physical limit —not a manufacturing one— on thinning the SiO₂ oxide below 1.2 nm
    Tunnel diode (Esaki)Tunneling through a heavily doped p-n junction Negative resistance: the current decreases as the voltage rises, over one stretch

    SIS junction (superconductor-insulator-superconductor)Tunneling through a 1-2 nm oxide Superconducting gap spectroscopy (Giaever, 1960) and the basis of SQUIDs
    The wall the chip industry ran into

    For twenty years, shrinking a transistor was mainly a lithography problem. Around 2005 it stopped being one: with the gate oxide at 1.2 nm —about four atoms thick— the tunneling leakage current became comparable to the useful current of the transistor. A better factory was not needed; a different physics was. The solution was to replace SiO₂ with dielectrics of higher dielectric constant (high-k), which allow the same capacitance with a thicker layer and less tunneling.

    07Common mistakes

    • Believing that with \( E > V_0 \) there is never any reflection. Quantum mechanically there is always some, even if small.
    • Thinking that tunneling violates conservation of energy. The particle that crosses keeps its energy \( E \); what changes is that the probability of finding it on the other side is no longer zero.
    • Using the formula \( T \approx e^{-2\kappa L} \) with very narrow or very low barriers. It only holds if \( \kappa L \gg 1 \); otherwise the full formula is needed.
    • Forgetting the zero-point energy of the well and of the oscillator. Neither of the two allows zero energy, not even in the ground state.
    • Confusing Fowler-Nordheim tunneling (with field, triangular barrier) with simple tunneling through a rectangular barrier. They are relatives, not the same calculation.
    • Thinking that the gate-oxide limit was a manufacturing problem. It was a physical limit: tunneling exists even if the oxide is perfect.

    08Self-assessment

    What does quantum mechanics predict at a potential step with \( E > V_0 \)?

    That part of the wave is reflected, something classical mechanics does not allow if the particle has energy to spare.

    What does the tunneling probability depend on most strongly?

    On the width of the barrier: \( T \) falls exponentially with \( L \), so one angstrom of difference can change it by an order of magnitude.

    Why did the chip industry stop being able to thin the SiO₂ gate oxide?

    Because below 1.2 nm the tunneling leakage current becomes comparable to the useful current of the transistor: it is a physical limit, not a manufacturing one.

    What do the energies of an infinite well and of a harmonic oscillator have in common?

    Both are quantized and neither allows zero energy: both have a zero-point energy in their ground state.

    How do the energy spacings of the well and the oscillator differ?

    In the well the levels grow as \( n^2 \), ever more widely spaced; in the oscillator they are equally spaced, each one \( \hbar\omega \) from the previous one.

    What does a scanning tunneling microscope measure?

    The current that tunnels through the vacuum between a tip and a surface; since that current depends exponentially on the distance, it resolves relief of atomic size.
