    Stability, compensation, oscillators and slew rate

    To stabilize a follower, adding a new pole leaves less than 1 kHz of bandwidth. Lowering the pole that was already there leaves 845 kHz with the same phase margin.

    Phase margin Dominant pole Miller compensation Slew rate Wien bridge

    01When a feedback amplifier oscillates

    The denominator \( 1 + T(s) \) vanishes if at some frequency the loop gain is exactly −1: magnitude 1 and phase −180°. A signal of that frequency goes around the loop and returns unchanged, with no excitation at all. The Nyquist criterion gives the complete condition; for everyday amplifiers, with a loop gain that decreases with frequency, the margins seen in the previous unit are enough:

    \[ \mathrm{PM} = 180° + \angle T(j\omega_c), \quad |T(j\omega_c)| = 1 \] \[ \mathrm{GM} = -20 \log_{10} |T(j\omega_{180})| \] The phase margin PM is measured at the crossover frequency; the gain margin GM, at the frequency where the phase reaches −180°. Both have to be positive; in practice PM ≥ 45° and GM ≥ 10 dB are required.

    02Compensation

    To compensate is to modify the gain without feedback so that \( |T| \) crosses 1 before the phase approaches −180°. There are three ways to do it, each with a single capacitor:

    MethodWhat it doesCost
    Added dominant poleA new RC network adds a very low poleThe bandwidth falls to almost nothing
    Shifting the first poleA large capacitor at the node of the first pole lowers itMuch smaller: the original pole stops adding phase
    Miller (pole splitting)A capacitor between the input and output of the gain stageIt lowers the first pole and also raises the second

    Miller compensation is that of almost all integrated op amps: the Miller effect that in frequency response was a problem is used to advantage. A capacitor of a few picofarads, multiplied by the gain of the second stage, creates the dominant pole, and the local feedback it introduces pushes the second pole out to higher frequencies.

    03Slew rate

    Compensation has another consequence. In an op amp with Miller compensation, the maximum current that the input stage can deliver to the capacitor \( C_c \) limits the speed at which the output can change:

    \[ SR = \frac{I_{\max}}{C_c} \] \[ f_{\max} = \frac{SR}{2\pi V_p} \] \( f_{\max} \) is the full-power bandwidth: the highest frequency at which a sine wave of amplitude \( V_p \) is obtained without deforming it. With 0.5 V/μs, typical of a 741, a 10 V peak output is deformed above 8 kHz, even though the small-signal bandwidth is 1 MHz.

    Above \( f_{\max} \) the sine wave becomes a triangle. It is a large-signal limitation, which does not appear in the small-signal analysis: an amplifier can have bandwidth to spare and distort all the same.

    04Low-frequency oscillators

    An oscillator is an amplifier that purposely meets the oscillation condition at a single frequency: the Barkhausen criterion, \( T(j\omega_0) = 1 \). In the Wien bridge, a series–parallel RC network has zero phase and a transfer of 1/3 at one frequency, and the amplifier provides a gain of 3:

    \[ f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC} \] \[ A = 1 + \frac{R_2}{R_1} = 3 \] With a gain of exactly 3 the amplitude is indeterminate; with a little more, it grows until it saturates and distorts. Quality oscillators control the gain with an element that depends on amplitude: a lamp, a thermistor or a JFET with a detector.

    The phase-shift oscillator uses three RC sections that add up to 180° and an inverter with a gain of 29. The audio-frequency oscillators in laboratories are Wien-bridge types: the first company of Hewlett and Packard was born with one, stabilized with a lamp. The introduction with the equipment is in oscillators, in the technician program.

    05In the lab

    Activity 1 · Slew rate

    Apply a large square wave to a follower and measure the slope of the output. With a 10 V peak sine wave, raise the frequency until the triangle appears and compare with \( f_{\max} \).

    Activity 2 · Wien bridge

    Build a Wien-bridge oscillator with diode stabilization and measure the frequency and the distortion. Change the gain and observe the start-up.

    Activity 3 · External compensation

    With an uncompensated op amp, such as those that have pins for the compensation capacitor, measure the overshoot as a follower with different capacitors.

    06Common mistakes

    • Compensating for high gain and using the amplifier as a follower. Compensation is designed for the lowest gain of use.
    • Confusing small-signal bandwidth with full-power bandwidth. The latter is set by the slew rate and depends on amplitude.
    • Setting the Wien gain at exactly 3. The oscillator does not start or dies out; a margin and an amplitude control are needed.
    • Forgetting parasitic capacitances. A long cable at the output adds a pole and eats into the phase margin.

    07Self-assessment

    What full-power bandwidth does a 13 V/μs op amp have at 10 V peak?

    \( 13/(2\pi \cdot 10) \) MHz ≈ 207 kHz.

    A Wien bridge uses R = 10 kΩ and C = 10 nF. At what frequency does it oscillate?

    \( 1/(2\pi \cdot 10^{-4}) \approx 1.59 \) kHz.

    Why does shifting the first pole give more bandwidth than adding a new pole?

    Because the original 1 kHz pole stops adding phase: the crossover can be near the second pole instead of below the first.

    If Imax = 20 μA and Cc = 30 pF, what is the slew rate?

    0.67 V/μs.
