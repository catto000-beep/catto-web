    The industrial electrical project

    In a house a calculation error is paid for with a warm cable; in a plant it is paid for with a production stoppage, a burnt transformer or a switchboard that blows up when a breaker is closed. The industrial project follows the same logic as a house —survey, calculate, verify, draw— with three differences: the powers are hundreds of times larger, the short circuit stops being a hypothesis, and the protective devices have to work as a team. This page follows a single example, a metalworking shop fed by a 315 kVA transformer, from the load schedule to the capacitor bank.

    01Power demand and the transformer

    Every project starts with the question “how much power will this plant ask for?”. The answer is not the sum of the motor nameplates: not all equipment is installed at full load, and not all of it runs at the same time. That is why two powers are distinguished:

    Installed power

    The sum of the electrical power of everything connected. It tells what was bought, not how to size the supply.

    Maximum demand

    The greatest power the plant draws at some real moment of its operation, usually averaged over 15 minutes. It is the one that sizes the transformer, busbars and feeders.

    Two factors take you from one to the other. The utilization factor fu tells what fraction of its power each load really uses when it runs (an overhead crane moves little; a furnace runs almost all the time). The diversity (simultaneity) factor fs tells what fraction of the total runs at once: the probability that all ten machines start in the same minute is low. The values come from experience with the type of industry, from manufacturers’ guides and, in an existing plant, from measuring them (see lab 1). Active and reactive power are calculated separately:

    Pmax=fs·∑(Pi·fu,i)Qmax=fs·∑(Qi·fu,i)Pᵢ: installed electrical active power of each load · fᵤ: utilization factor of each load · fₛ: diversity (simultaneity) factor of the group · Qᵢ = Pᵢ · tan φᵢ

    Add kW and kvar, not kVA

    The apparent power of each load has its own angle, so it cannot be added arithmetically. Adding kVA gives a number larger than the real one and the transformer ends up oversized (see Power in alternating current).

    02The single-line diagram, lines, circuits and the environment

    The single-line diagram is the map of the installation: one line stands for the three phases and shows, from source to load, which transformer, breaker, busbar and cable is in each stretch. It is the drawing that is calculated first and the one consulted by anyone who operates or repairs the plant. Figure 1 is the diagram of the example; its four states show, on the same drawing, the design currents, the protections, the conductors and the short-circuit currents.

    Lines and circuits

    In an industrial project a line (or feeder) is the conductor that carries power from one board to another, and a circuit the one that leaves a board towards a load: a motor, a bank of luminaires, a group of sockets. The criterion for splitting the plant into sub-boards is always the same:

    • By zone, so that a feeder does not cross the whole plant and the voltage drop stays reasonable (SDB1 is in the hall, next to the machines).
    • By type of load: heavy-starting motors, lighting and offices do not share a board, because their disturbances and criticality differ.
    • By criticality: what cannot stop (emergency lighting, control) has its own circuit so that a fault in another area does not take it out.
    • With a reserve: each board leaves 20 to 25 % free space to expand without rebuilding it, and each feeder is calculated with that expansion in mind.

    Control and signalling circuits (contactor coils, push buttons, pilot lights, sensors) are drawn separately, in the functional diagram: they are supplied by a control transformer or a 24 V supply, with their own protection. The project of a house, with its outlets, sockets and bill of quantities, is in Installation project; the general rules for raceways, boards and earthing in Electrical installations. What changes in industry is the calculation, which follows.

    Environmental conditions decide the material

    A board that works in a clean office is destroyed in a hall with metal dust and cutting oil. Before choosing enclosures, cables and apparatus the environmental conditions of each room are surveyed: maximum and minimum temperature, dust (conductive or not), humidity and water splashes, chemical or corrosive agents, risk of impact and of explosion. The protection degree of an enclosure is given by the IP code (IEC 60529): two digits, the first against solids (0 to 6) and the second against liquids (0 to 8).

    Temperature

    Cables and breakers are rated at a reference temperature. If the room or the inside of the board is hotter, they carry less current (factor kT in section 03). The room is ventilated, the board moved away from the furnaces, or the equipment oversized.

    Dust and humidity

    Conductive dust (filings) short-circuits terminals; humidity condenses and lowers insulation. They are handled with the right IP, anti-condensation heaters and, in corrosive atmospheres, polyester or stainless-steel enclosures.

    Impact and explosion

    The IK code rates impact resistance. In rooms with flammable gases, vapours or dusts a specific explosive-atmosphere regulation applies (IEC 60079 series), with certified equipment; it is not improvised.

    03Conductors: current and voltage drop

    Each feeder must pass the same checks as in a house (see Installation project): current-carrying capacity, voltage drop, short circuit and coordination with the protective device. The largest of the resulting sizes is adopted. What is new in industry is that current capacity is corrected by two factors that hardly matter in a house.

    Current capacity with correction factors

    The current a cable can carry without exceeding its insulation temperature depends on how it is installed (on a tray, in conduit, buried), on the ambient temperature and on how many neighbouring cables also heat up. The regulation (AEA 90364) and the manufacturer publish tables with the current capacity for a reference installation and correction factors to depart from it. The values on this page are typical of the IEC 60364-5-52 series, on which those tables are based; in a real project they are always taken from the current table and the manufacturer’s datasheet.

    04Short-circuit currents, busbars and busway

    A short circuit is not a rare failure: it is the design case that decides whether the board resists or blows up. It must be known as two values. The maximum current (three-phase fault right at the board) sizes the breaking capacity of the breakers and the stresses on the busbars. The minimum current (fault at the farthest end of the line) checks that the protective device notices the fault and clears it in time. The general method is IEC 60909; what follows is the impedance method, which with a calculator gives the maximum value with good approximation.

    The idea: from the grid to the fault point there is a chain of impedances in series (the grid, the transformer, each stretch of cable). They are added —resistances with resistances and reactances with reactances— and the current is the voltage divided by the total impedance. It is calculated at 400 V, the line voltage on the low-voltage side.

    Xgrid=Un2Ssc,gridequivalent impedance of the medium-voltage grid referred to 400 V; it is almost purely reactive
    ZT=uk·Un2SnRT=PCu·Un2Sn2XT=ZT2−RT2uₖ: short-circuit voltage (per unit) · P_Cu: copper losses at full load · Sₙ in VA
    ZT=0.04·400V2315 000VA=20.3mΩRT=4 500W·400V2315 000VA2=7.3mΩ
    XT=20.32−7.32mΩ=19.0mΩXgrid=400V2250 000 000VA=0.6mΩ

    Busbars

    The copper busbars of the MDB carry the current of all the sub-boards. They are pre-sized by current density —about 1.25 A/mm² as a conservative criterion in a naturally ventilated board— and checked against the manufacturer’s table, which accounts for shape, paint, bar stacking and the temperature inside the board:

    Example · Busbars of the main board

    Current to carry: the main breaker of 500 A (sized for the transformer, which delivers up to 454.7 A). Required cross-section: 500/1.25=400 mm². A 40 × 10 mm copper bar (400 mm²) per phase is adopted.

Two stresses: the dynamic one, because the opposing currents of two parallel bars repel each other with a force proportional to the square of the peak current, and the thermal one, because all the energy of the fault turns into heat before the breaker opens.     Busway

    A busway (busbar trunking system, IEC 61439-6) is a metal enclosure holding insulated copper or aluminium bars, with straight sections, bends and tap-off boxes that plug in at any point. It replaces cable in two cases: when the power is so high that many cables in parallel would be needed, and when the loads are spread along a line and a tap must be added or moved without touching the rest. It is the typical solution for a production hall.

    In the example, inside the hall, SDB1 feeds a 250 A, 40 m busway, with a tap-off box every few metres for the machines (the design current of SDB1 is 215.7 A). It is checked like a feeder, with three differences:

    • Voltage drop is calculated with the resistance and reactance per metre from the catalogue; with illustrative values of 0.20 and 0.08 mΩ/m, at full current over the whole length it comes to 0.77 %.
    • Since the loads are spread along the line, the current falls stretch by stretch and the real drop is about half: 0.39 %. With the 1.85 % of the feeder, the total up to the machines is 2.24 %, within the plan.
    • Its short-time withstand current (Icw) and the peak it withstands must be checked: nameplate data that must exceed the fault at its point of installation (6.0 kA at SDB1).
    Busways are also corrected for the environment

    Their rated current is given for a specific ambient temperature and horizontal mounting. They are corrected for temperature like cables and, where they cross fire-rated walls or floors, the manufacturer’s fire-barrier sections must be used. And like any enclosure they are chosen with the IP suited to the room (IP 54 or 55 in the example hall).

    05Switching, protection and selectivity

    Each circuit needs three functions, which may be in one device or several: isolating (to work without voltage), switching (making and breaking in service) and protecting against overload and short circuit. The general protection criteria are in Electrical protection; here they are applied to the example.

    The circuit breaker and its breaking capacity

    A circuit breaker has a thermal trip (overload, with a delay that depends on how much the current is exceeded) and a magnetic trip (short circuit, instantaneous). In power breakers both are adjustable: Ir (long delay), Isd (short delay, with its time) and Ii (instantaneous). It also has two breaking capacities:

    Icu · ultimate breaking capacity

    The greatest current it can interrupt, once. Afterwards it may be unusable. It must be greater than or equal to the short-circuit current at the point where it is installed.

    Ics · service breaking capacity

    A percentage of Icu (25, 50, 75 or 100 %) after which the breaker is still fit for service. Where an outage cannot be tolerated, a high value is required.

    The key point is where the current is taken: the breaker sees the fault at its own terminals. So the MDB breakers (main and feeders) face 11.0 kA, and those inside SDB1, 6.0 kA:

It is an economical solution, but it must be backed by the manufacturer’s table, not by an assumption.

    Coordinating protections: selectivity

    A well-protected installation is not the one with many breakers, but the one in which, on a fault, only the breaker immediately upstream of the fault opens. This is called selectivity (or discrimination). If the main breaker opens because one machine in the hall failed, the whole plant goes dark. There are three ways to achieve it:

    • Current-based: the upstream device has a magnetic trip threshold higher than the maximum downstream fault. Simple, but it only works where the fault falls with distance.
    • Time-based: the upstream device waits an adjustable instant (short-circuit delay) to give the downstream one time to clear the fault. It requires the upstream device to withstand the short circuit during that time.
    • By manufacturer tables: for combinations of breakers of the same range, the manufacturer guarantees selectivity up to a certain current. It is the usual approach in low voltage.

    Figure 3 shows the curves of two breakers of the example, the 500 A main of the MDB and the 250 A one that protects SDB1. They are typical curves of the breaker type (not of a brand), drawn on a logarithmic scale: current on the horizontal axis; on the vertical axis, the time each takes to trip.

    Example · Who opens on each fault?

    The main is set to Ir = 500 A, Isd = 6 · Ir = 3,000 A with a 0.2 s delay, and no instantaneous trip; the SDB1 breaker (250 A) has a magnetic trip at 10 · In = 2,500 A, acting in under 0.01 s.

    • 700 A overload in SDB1: the 250 A trips thermally in about 55 s; the main would take about 440 s. Only the 250 A opens.
    • 6.0 kA short circuit at the end of the feeder: the 250 A clears in under 0.01 s; the main is still waiting its 0.2 s. Only the 250 A opens.
    • 11.0 kA short circuit right at the output of the 250 A breaker: same, as long as the main withstands 11.0 kA for 0.2 s (its short-time withstand current Icw must be greater).
    • Without delay (if the main tripped instantaneously at 5 kA): at 6.0 kA both would open and selectivity is lost.

    There is a price: while the main waits 0.2 s the fault keeps flowing. The cable and the busbars must take that energy. For the 25 mm² cable of SDB3, protected at its own outlet, the maximum time with the fault at the MDB is:

    t≤(k·SI)2k = 143 for XLPE-insulated copper · S: conductor cross-section in mm² · I: fault current in A

    The SDB3 cable depends on its own (instantaneous) breaker clearing in a fraction of a cycle: if it depended on the main, its 0.2 s would destroy it. That is why coordination is always checked against the cable and the busbars, not only between breakers.

    06Reactive power and power-factor correction

    Motors, transformers and lamps with ballasts need reactive power to magnetize: it goes back and forth between the grid and the load doing no work, but it flows through the cables and the transformer as if it did (see Power in alternating current and Three-phase circuits). The example plant has cos φ = 0.85: out of every 100 amperes flowing, only 85 do work. Correcting it has three benefits:

    • Less current through the transformer and feeders: lower losses, less voltage drop and more power available for expansion.
    • No penalty: utilities surcharge the bill below a reference cos φ (of the order of 0.85; the value and the surcharge appear in the current tariff schedule approved by the provincial regulator).
    • Better use of the investment in copper and in the transformer.

    Reactive power is compensated with capacitors, which supply it where it is consumed. The bank power is calculated from the angles before and after:

    Qc=P·(tanφ1−tanφ2)P: active demand power · φ₁: angle before correction · φ₂: target angle (cos φ₂ = 0.95)
    Example · The capacitor bank

    The plant demands P = 209.4 kW and Q = 128.6 kvar (tan φ₁ = 0.614, cos φ₁ = 0.85). The aim is to bring cos φ to 0.95 (tan φ₂ = 0.329):

    Qc=209.4kW·(0.614−0.329)=209.4kW·0.285=59.8kvar→60kvar

    An automatic 60 kvar bank in steps (for example 6 × 10 kvar) is adopted, with a controller that connects and disconnects capacitors according to the measured cos φ. Result:

    Qend=128.6−60=68.6kvarSend=209.42+68.62kVA=220.4kVAcosφ=209.4220.4=0.95

    The low-voltage current drops from 354.7 A to 318.1 A (10 % less) and the transformer runs at 70 % of its rating. The bank data:

    C=Qc3·ω·Un2capacitance of each phase with the capacitors connected in delta (U = line voltage)
    C=60 000var3·314.2rad/s·400V2=398µF
    Ic=60 000var3·400V=86.6A→1.43·86.6A=123.8A→125A

    The bank breaker is chosen at no less than 1.43 times the rated capacitor current, because capacitors withstand continuously up to 30 % extra current from harmonics and overvoltage, and their real capacitance may exceed the nominal by 10 % (1.3 · 1.1 = 1.43); 125 A is the standard step. 35 mm² cable (table: 185 A · 0.728 = 135 A ≥ 125 A).

    Where to install it

    • Fixed compensation at each large motor: a capacitor in parallel with the motor, switched with it. It corrects only what that motor needs and avoids carrying the reactive power along the line.
    • Centralized compensation at the MDB: an automatic bank that follows demand. This is the case of the example.
    • Mixed: fixed at the large motors and automatic at the MDB for the rest.
    Harmonics: the capacitor’s enemy

    A capacitor presents an impedance that falls as frequency rises; with drives, rectifiers and electronic luminaires it can enter resonance with the transformer, amplify a harmonic and burn out. If more than roughly 20–25 % of the load is non-linear, the bank is installed with a detuning reactor (for example 7 %) in series with each step; see Power quality and machine protection.

    A disconnected capacitor is still charged

    A capacitor keeps its charge after the breaker opens. The bank has discharge resistors, but before touching it you must wait the time the manufacturer states (one to several minutes), verify the absence of voltage with an instrument at the terminals and only then short-circuit and earth them. A capacitor is never short-circuited “just in case” without having opened first. See Occupational health and safety.

    The project is one chained calculation

    Demand fixes the transformer; the transformer fixes the short-circuit current; that fixes the breaking capacity and the strength of busbars and cables; cos φ comes back to the demand. Changing one datum (another transformer, another length, one more motor) forces you to go through the chain again. That is why it is done in a spreadsheet and documented in a calculation report that accompanies the drawings (see Technical drawing and computer-aided design).

    07In the workshop

    Lab 1 · Survey the loads of a board and measure their factors

    Materials: a sub-board of the school, true-rms clamp meter (an analyzer if available), spreadsheet. Steps: with the installation in service, record for each outgoing way the line current at three moments of the day, the voltage and the cos φ; fill in the sheet with P, Q and S of each load. What is measured: real current against the rated current of each load (fu) and the feeder current against the sum of the outgoing currents (fs). Acceptance criterion: the vector sum of P and Q of the outgoing ways matches the one measured at the feeder to within 10 %; the fu and fs obtained are recorded for use in future projects.

    Lab 2 · The example calculation in a spreadsheet

    Materials: spreadsheet and the manufacturer’s cable tables. Steps: enter the load table of this page (with formulas, not numbers), obtain the demand and the transformer; then the three feeders with current and voltage drop; then Isc at the two points; then the bank. Change the SDB1 length from 120 to 60 m and the target cos φ to 0.98. Acceptance criterion: the sheet reproduces the values in the text (315 kVA, 4 × 150 mm², 11.0 and 6.0 kA, 60 kvar) and, with the changes, the results move in the expected direction, which the student explains in writing.

    Lab 3 · Measure the loop impedance and estimate the fault current

    Materials: loop impedance tester (or earth tester with that function), training sub-board. Steps: with the teacher and with the circuit in normal service, measure at the farthest terminal the phase-neutral and phase-earth loop impedance; calculate I=U/Z; compare it with the value the project calculation predicts. What is measured: loop impedance and the estimated fault current. Acceptance criterion: the estimated current greatly exceeds the magnetic threshold of the breaker, so that it would trip instantaneously. An insufficient value points to a cable that is too long or a loose connection.

    Lab 4 · Draw the single-line diagram and the load schedule

    Materials: CAD software and the table of this page. Steps: draw the single-line diagram of the example with standard symbols, labelling in each stretch the cross-section, length, protection and calculated currents; add the load schedule and the title block with the revision. Acceptance criterion: a classmate who has not seen the calculation can reproduce the SDB1 cross-section using only the drawing (see Technical drawing and computer-aided design).

    Measuring on a board is not just looking

    Any measurement with the board energized is made by a trained and authorized person, with dielectric footwear and gloves, face protection and insulated tools; an instrument with a suitable measurement category (CAT III or IV) is used, and nothing is opened or touched unless the teacher has said so. Live work follows Resolution SRT 592/2004 and Law 19,587 with its Decree 351/79.

    08Common mistakes

    MistakeConsequence
    Sizing with installed power or adding kVA arithmeticallyOversized transformer, busbars and feeders, with the cost that implies. Demand is calculated with fu, fs and adding kW and kvar separately.
    Adopting fu and fs arbitrarily, “to make it work”An undersized plant is discovered when the main breaker trips at peak. If in doubt, measure in a similar installation.
    Choosing the cable by current alone in a long runVoltage falls at the far end: motors start poorly and overheat. The drop is always checked.
    Forgetting the temperature and grouping factorsThe cable heats beyond what its insulation withstands; cable life shortens or a fire starts.
    Choosing the breaking capacity with the current at the end of the lineA breaker is sized with the fault at its own terminals. With the end value it falls short and may blow up while clearing.
    Taking the Isc of one board and using it everywhereFault current changes with distance from the transformer: each board has its own.
    Installing “higher-ampere” breakers so they do not tripCable protection and selectivity are lost: on a fault the main opens and the whole plant loses power.
    Ignoring the starting of large motorsA direct start of 37 kW draws as much as the whole transformer: voltage dip across the plant. Star-delta, soft starter or drive is used.
    Installing capacitors without knowing the harmonicsResonance with the transformer and burnt capacitors. With non-linear loads, the bank has detuning reactors.
    An IP 20 enclosure in an atmosphere with conductive dust or humidityShort circuits and insulation failures within months.

    09Self-assessment

    Why is maximum demand lower than installed power, and how is it obtained?

    Because not all loads are at full load or run at the same time. The utilization factor fu is applied to each load, kW and kvar are added separately, and the total gets the diversity factor fs. Apparent power then follows from Pythagoras. In the example, 367.7 kW installed give 209.4 kW of demand.

    A 90 m feeder carries 120 A at 400 V three-phase, with cos φ = 0.85 (sin φ = 0.527). Does 35 mm² comply if the limit is 2 %? (r = 0.022/S, x = 0.08 mΩ/m)

    r = 0.022/35 = 0.629 mΩ/m → R = 56.6 mΩ; X = 7.2 mΩ. ΔV = √3 · 120 · (0.0566 · 0.85 + 0.0072 · 0.527) = 207.8 · (0.0481 + 0.0038) = 10.8 V, i.e. 2.7 %: it does not comply. With 50 mm²: R = 39.6 mΩ, ΔV = 207.8 · (0.0337 + 0.0038) = 7.8 V = 1.95 %: it complies. Even if 35 mm² may be enough for current, voltage drop defines the size.

    Why is a breaker’s breaking capacity chosen with the short-circuit current at its terminals and not with that at the end of the line it protects?

    Because the worst case for the breaker is a fault right after it, where the downstream impedance is minimal and the current maximal. The fault at the far end is smaller and serves another check: that the magnetic trip detects it.

    A 400 kVA, 4 % transformer fed from an infinite-power grid. What short-circuit current is there at the 400 V terminals?

    In = 400,000/(√3 · 400) = 577.4 A; Isc = In/uk = 577.4/0.04 = 14.4 kA. With a real grid, a little less; and in practice the next standard breaking-capacity step is chosen.

    What does it mean for two breakers to be selective, and how is it achieved by delay?

    That on a fault only the breaker immediately upstream of it opens. With delay, the upstream breaker waits an adjustable time (for example 0.2 s) before tripping on short circuit, giving the downstream one, which is instantaneous, time to clear. You must check that it withstands the fault during that time (Icw) and that the cable and busbars can take it.

    A plant draws P = 150 kW at cos φ = 0.80. What capacitor bank brings it to cos φ = 0.95?

    tan φ₁ = 0.75 (cos 0.80) and tan φ₂ = 0.329 (cos 0.95). Qc = 150 · (0.75 − 0.329) = 150 · 0.421 = 63 kvar; a 60 or 70 kvar bank is adopted. Current falls from 150/(√3 · 0.4 · 0.80) = 270.6 A to 150/(√3 · 0.4 · 0.95) = 227.9 A.

    Which environmental conditions are surveyed before choosing the board, and what is done with them?

    Temperature (corrects the current capacity of cables and devices), dust (IP of the enclosure, first digit), humidity and water (second digit, anti-condensation heaters), chemical agents (enclosure material), impact (IK) and explosion risk (specific regulation). In a hall with machining dust and coolant, IP 54 or 55 is adopted.
