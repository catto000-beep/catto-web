    Power generation and generating sets

    When the power goes out in a hospital, a factory or an antenna site, an engine starts by itself and, within a few seconds, brings the voltage back. Behind it there is an alternator that makes the electricity, an engine that turns it at constant speed, two regulators —one for voltage, one for speed— and a switchboard that decides when to connect the load. Understanding how they work together is understanding any power plant, from a 5 kVA set to a dam.

    01The synchronous alternator in service

    All serious generation, from a dam to a construction-site set, relies on the same principle: a rotating electromagnet (the rotor, fed with direct excitation current) induces an alternating voltage in the fixed windings of the stator (Magnetism and electromagnetism). It is synchronous because the frequency is tied to the speed: f = p · n / 60, with p pole pairs. The relation and the speed table for 50 Hz are in Electrical energy; here it is enough to remember that a common four-pole set turns at 1,500 rpm.

    Rotor typeWhat it looks likeWhere it is used
    Salient poleMany poles standing out from the shaft, large diameter and short length. Turns slowly.Hydro generators and 1,500 rpm generating sets.
    Round (cylindrical) rotorSolid slotted cylinder, two or four poles, long and slender. Withstands the centrifugal force at 3,000 rpm.Steam and gas turbo-generators.

    In modern sets the excitation is provided without brushes or slip rings: an exciter (a small alternator whose armature rotates with the rotor) feeds alternating current to a rotating diode bridge, which supplies the main field. Regulating the exciter current regulates the whole generator. Many machines add a permanent-magnet generator to power the regulator, so the alternator keeps its excitation even when the output voltage collapses in a short circuit.

    Two variables, two regulators

    In a set there are two things to control, and they hardly affect each other. The voltage is set by the excitation and governed by the automatic voltage regulator (AVR). The frequency is set by the engine speed and governed by the speed regulator (governor). The two powers also separate: the active power (kW) is set by how hard the engine pushes; the reactive power (kvar), by the load and the excitation.

    Set rating: kVA, kW and the engine limit

    The nameplate of an alternator says kVA: this is what it can deliver without overheating its windings, whatever the power factor of the load. What is usefully consumed is kW, and that comes from the engine shaft (Power in alternating current, Three-phase circuits):

    S=3·U·IP=S·cos⁡φS in kVA is the apparent power the alternator can deliver; P in kW is the active power, which comes from the engine shaft.

    By convention, sets are rated at power factor 0.8: a 100 kVA set is an 80 kW set. The rated output current is:

    In=S3·U
    The weaker of the two sets the limit

    A 45 kVA, 36 kW set is still a 36 kW set if the load has a power factor of 1: the alternator could give 45 kW, but the engine does not have it. With a low power factor the opposite happens: the engine has power to spare and the alternator overheats before the engine reaches its rating. To choose a set, both must be checked: kW against the engine and kVA against the alternator. Strongly capacitive loads (uncharged capacitor banks, some electronic equipment) deserve a separate note: they can make the voltage rise without the AVR being able to control it.

    Voltage and frequency regulation

    With the AVR in service the voltage is held within a small margin —on the order of ±1 % in steady state— between no load and full load. The hard part is the transient: when a large load is connected suddenly, the voltage sags and takes a fraction of a second to recover, and the engine loses speed until the governor increases the fuel. The larger the load step relative to the set rating, the deeper the sag; that ratio drives the sizing (section 06).

    Frequency can be regulated in two modes. In droop mode the speed falls slightly as the load increases, which is what allows the power to be shared among several sets in parallel (section 05). In isochronous mode the regulator is electronic and holds the exact speed at any load; it is used on a single isolated set, and in parallel it needs a communication line between regulators to share the load. The droop is defined as:

    R=fno-load−ffullfn·100%With fn = 50 Hz, a 4 % droop means the frequency falls 2 Hz between no load and full load.

    Alternator efficiency

    The alternator loses energy in the copper (Joule heating in stator and rotor), in the iron (hysteresis and eddy currents) and in friction and windage. Its efficiency is the ratio between the power it delivers and the power it receives:

    ηalt=PoutPout+PCu+PFe+PmechPCu: copper losses in the stator and rotor; PFe: iron losses; Pmech: friction and windage.

    In the alternators of small and medium sets, efficiency is around 85 to 95 %, better the larger the machine and the closer to full load. The difference leaves as heat, which the alternator fan must carry away: hence the importance of letting the cooling air circulate freely (section 04).

    02Turbines: types and characteristics

    The turbine is the prime mover of large plants: it converts the energy of a moving fluid —steam, hot gas or water— into the rotation of a shaft. It is more compact and smoother than a reciprocating engine, but it needs a pressurized fluid to be available; at small ratings the reciprocating engine dominates. The plants of each kind and their efficiencies are in Electrical energy; here the point is what each turbine looks like and what characterizes it.

    P=η·ρ·g·Q·Hρ = 1,000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s², Q flow in m³/s, H net head in m; the result is in W.
    Example · A micro hydro plant on a mountain stream

    A drop uses a net head of 45 m with a flow of 2.5 m³/s. The overall efficiency of turbine and generator is 0.88. What power is obtained and which turbine suits? If the generator is coupled directly to a 300 rpm turbine, how many pole pairs does it need for 50 Hz?

    P=0.88·1000kg/m³·9.81m/s²·2.5m³/s·45m=971kW

    With a 45 m head and a medium flow, the natural choice is the Francis (a Kaplan would be possible with a larger flow and a smaller head). For the poles:

    p=60·fn=60·50300rpm=10pole pairs

    This is a salient-pole machine with twenty poles, as befits a slow generator.

    A steam turbine is not started all at once

    The rotor and the casing expand with heat, and if they heat at different rates they rub. That is why the shaft is turned very slowly with a turning gear before steam is admitted, and speed and temperature are raised in stages, following a procedure. The same precaution applies when stopping: a shaft that stops hot would bow if it did not keep turning slowly.

    03Internal combustion engines, fuels and lubricants

    Below a few MW, the usual drive for a generator is a reciprocating internal combustion engine: pistons that turn the pressure of burned fuel into crankshaft rotation. In practice there are two families:

    • Diesel engine (compression ignition): it draws in and compresses only air, with a compression ratio of about 16:1 to 22:1; at the end of the compression the air is so hot that the injected diesel ignites on its own. It is the engine of medium-power and emergency sets: it consumes little, withstands a lot of work and its fuel is less flammable.
    • Otto engine (spark ignition): it compresses an air-fuel mixture (petrol, natural gas or LPG) with a compression ratio of 8:1 to 12:1 and a spark plug ignites it. It is the engine of small portable sets (1 to 10 kVA) and of gas-fuelled sets.

    Both are usually four-stroke: intake, compression, expansion (the only one that delivers work) and exhaust, in two crankshaft turns. The engine must hold the alternator speed (1,500 rpm) even when the load changes: that is the governor’s job. The shaft power is the product of torque and angular speed:

    P=M·ω=M·2·π·n60P in W, M (torque) in N·m, n in rpm. At 1,500 rpm, 40 kW corresponds to a torque of 255 N·m.

    The efficiency of the engine is obtained from the specific consumption b (grams of fuel burned per kWh delivered at the shaft) and the heating value of the fuel:

    ηm=3.6MJ/kWhb·LHVb: specific consumption in kg/kWh; LHV: lower heating value of the fuel in MJ/kg (3.6 MJ = 1 kWh).

    With b = 230 g/kWh and a diesel of 42.7 MJ/kg, the efficiency is 3.6 / (0.230 · 42.7) = 36.7 %. The specific consumption gets worse at low load, because the mechanical losses are almost fixed: an engine working at 25 % load burns considerably more per kWh than one at full load (a table with measured values is in section 06).

    What a set engine includes

    Besides the block, cylinders and crankshaft, a set engine has its auxiliary systems, which are the ones that fail most often:

    • Fuel: tank, filters (with water separator), pump and injection system.
    • Air: air filter and, on larger engines, a turbocharger with an intake air cooler, which raises power per displacement.
    • Cooling: water circuit with antifreeze, pump, thermostat and radiator with fan; small engines are air-cooled.
    • Lubrication: oil pump, filter and, sometimes, an oil cooler; oil pressure is the parameter that most protects the engine.
    • Starting: 12 or 24 V electric starter and batteries, with charger.
    • Exhaust: manifold, flexible joint, silencer and a duct to the outside.

    Rating categories

    The nameplate power is not a single value: it depends on the kind of duty for which the manufacturer guarantees the machine. The ISO 8528-1 standard defines continuous power (COP, at constant load with no limit on hours), prime power (PRP, at variable load with no limit on hours and a limited average load) and limited-time power (LTP), and manufacturers often add emergency standby power (ESP, for backing up the grid a few hours per year). The same engine has a different value in each category, and the emergency rating is the highest: it must not be used to size a set that works every day. In addition, the values apply at reference conditions (in ISO 8528-1, 100 kPa, 25 °C and 30 % relative humidity); at higher altitude or temperature the engine delivers less, and the manufacturer gives the derating curve.

    Lubricants

    Engine oil does five jobs at once: it lubricates, cools the zones the coolant does not reach, cleans (detergent-dispersant additives keep soot in suspension), seals between rings and cylinder and protects against corrosion. It is chosen by its viscosity, given as an SAE grade (a set diesel usually takes a multigrade such as SAE 15W-40), and by its API service level (the “C” series, for diesel). Oil and filters are changed by running hours or by time, whichever comes first, following the manufacturer’s manual; an emergency set, even if it runs few hours, still needs the time-based change because the oil ages. A periodic analysis of the used oil (viscosity, water, soot, wear metals) is a predictive-maintenance tool, like thermography (Maintenance of electrical components). The general criteria for choosing fuels and lubricants in the workshop are in Electrical workshop supplies.

    A diesel that hardly works gets sick

    A diesel running for a long time at low load (below roughly 30 % of its power) does not reach working temperature: the fuel burns poorly and leaves soot and unburned diesel in the exhaust and cylinders. That is why emergency sets are tested under load, not at no load, and why oversizing a set “to be safe” is rejected.

    04The medium and small generating set

    A generating set (motor-generator) is an engine and an alternator coupled on a common bedplate, with their auxiliaries and a control panel. “Small” is the portable or site set, a few kVA, almost always single-phase and petrol-fuelled; “medium” is the three-phase diesel set, fixed or trailer-mounted, from some tens to a few hundred kVA, which backs up a building, a plant or an antenna site.

    The generator room

    A well-chosen set can fail because of its room. It needs combustion and cooling air in quantity (an inlet and an outlet louvre of the size the manufacturer gives), an exhaust that discharges outdoors away from openings, fuel that is contained and away from heat sources, space for maintenance and a base that supports the weight and the vibration. General maintenance of the switchboard components is explained in Maintenance of electrical components, and planning in Maintenance management.

    A set in automatic starts by itself

    Before touching a set whose panel is in automatic mode, it must be prevented from starting: turn the selector to “out of service”, disconnect the batteries and hang the padlock and tag. Only then can the fan, the belt or the coupling not move with hands inside. Also: exhaust carbon monoxide is lethal, so a set is never tested in a closed room; the exhaust and the manifold burn; batteries give off hydrogen (explosive), and the noise calls for hearing protection.

    The automatic transfer switchboard

    A backup set is connected to the installation through an automatic transfer switchboard (ATS). It contains two contactors or motorized breakers —one for the grid, one for the set—, interlocked mechanically and electrically so they are never closed at the same time, and a controller that measures the voltage and frequency of the grid and runs the sequence. It has three modes: automatic (it serves the grid), manual (the operator switches) and test (it starts the set and transfers on purpose, to exercise it).

    TimerWhat it doesUsual value
    Failure delayWaits before giving the start order, to ignore micro-outages.1 to 5 s

    Backfeed: the risk you cannot see

    If the set were connected to an installation still tied to the utility grid, it would energize the street cables through the distribution transformer, at high voltage, with a worker believing them dead. That is why the connection is made only through the interlocked ATS and never with a plug or a jumper. The case is also discussed in generator testing. The AEA 90364 regulation asks that a standby source cannot be paralleled with the grid by mistake; and a permanent parallel connection with the utility grid needs authorization and special protections.

    The ATS design also defines what happens with the neutral: a four-pole switchboard also switches the neutral and prevents the return current from taking unplanned paths; the set’s neutral is earthed according to the earthing system of the installation, and the set’s earthing and its continuity are measured (Earthing, insulation and generator testing).

    05Paralleling and synchronizing

    Two or more sets can feed the same busbar. It is done for redundancy (if one fails, the other keeps running), to be able to maintain a set without cutting the service, to grow by adding a machine, and so that only the necessary sets run, close to their best efficiency. The price is complexity: the breaker of each set must be closed at a precise instant and then the load must be shared.

    The four conditions to close

    A generator can be connected to a live busbar only when the voltage (RMS), the frequency, the phase sequence and the phase angle all match those of the busbar. The sequence is checked once, at commissioning, with a phase-sequence meter; the other three are adjusted every time, and watched with a voltmeter, a frequency meter and a synchroscope, whose pointer turns at the speed of the frequency difference (toward “fast” if the incoming set is faster) and points to 12 o’clock when the waves are in phase.

    There are two ways to do it. Manual synchronizing uses the synchroscope, or three lamps: in the “dark lamp” method the set is closed when all three go out together; if they are cross-connected the light “rotates” and its direction of rotation says whether the set is faster or slower. Automatic synchronizing is done by a controller that adjusts the speed and the voltage and closes the breaker when the conditions are met and hold for a few moments (a synchronism-check relay).

    Closing out of synchronism breaks machines

    If the breaker is closed with the waves out of phase, the instantaneous voltage difference produces a short-circuit current and a sudden torque on the coupling and the crankshaft: shafts, couplings and windings have been broken. A breaker is never closed while the synchroscope is spinning fast, and the control system must include synchronism checking (synchronism-check relay, function 25).

    Sharing the active power: droop

    Once in parallel, the machines turn together at the same frequency. How they share the active power is decided by the speed characteristic of each engine: with droop, the frequency falls as the load rises. If two sets have the same droop in percent, they take the load in proportion to their rating; if one’s setting is raised, that one takes more. If the internal voltages of the alternators differ, a reactive current circulates between them that gives no useful power but heats them. In practice, the sets on one busbar are chosen with compatible regulators, and their fine adjustment is left to a technician with instruments (a network analyzer), not to trial and error.

    Reverse-power protection (the set that, instead of delivering, “motors” dragged by the others) and the other generator protections are explained in Supply quality and machine protection.

    06Sizing, testing and protecting a set

    Choosing a set is solving a problem with several possible answers; a small one sags when motors start, an enormous one always runs at low load and gets sick. The method has six steps:

    1. Survey the loads the set must feed (the essential ones, or the whole installation): power, power factor, efficiency and type of starting.
    2. Add kW and kvar; the total apparent power is not the sum of the apparent powers, but the composition of the active and reactive ones.
    3. Add a reserve margin (for growth and to avoid running at full load), of about 20 %.
    4. Check the starting of the largest motor: the transient voltage dip must be acceptable.
    5. Correct for altitude and temperature and choose the duty category (standby or prime) and the next commercial size.
    6. Check the fuel and the runtime the service requires.

    Equipment tests

    Before accepting a set, an acceptance test is made. The electrical tests of the alternator (winding resistance, insulation, no-load and short-circuit characteristics, synchronous impedance, load test with a bank of resistors and inductors) and the earthing measurement are developed in Earthing, insulation and generator testing; those of motors and transformers are in Motor and transformer tests. Here come those that look at the whole: the engine, the transfer and the real service.

    • Starting and load pick-up: time from the order to stable voltage and frequency, and to taking 100 % of the load.
    • Load by steps (25, 50, 75 and 100 % of the rating) with a load bank, recording at each step voltage, frequency, current, kW, temperatures, oil pressure and fuel consumption.
    • Step response: sag and recovery of voltage and frequency when a large load is connected suddenly.
    • Transfer sequence: times of each ATS step, interlock and return.
    • Protections: trip on low oil pressure, high temperature, overspeed and emergency stop, simulated.

    Generator protection

    Besides the protections of the output circuit (a thermal-magnetic or circuit breaker with overload and short-circuit settings), a generator needs its own protections: overload, short circuit, reverse power, over- and undervoltage, over- and underfrequency, loss of excitation and stator earth fault; on large machines, differential protection. They are developed in Supply quality and machine protection; the fundamentals of low-voltage protection are in Electrical protection.

    What to take away from this page

    A set is a chain: fuel, engine, alternator, switchboard. Voltage is set by the excitation and frequency by the speed. It is chosen by kW (the engine) and kVA (the alternator), with a margin and checking motor starting. And it is connected to the installation only through an interlocked switchboard, and to another set only in synchronism.

    07In the workshop

    Practice 1 · Survey a generating set

    Materials: a workshop set (stopped, with the batteries disconnected), form, flashlight, multimeter, insulation tester. Steps: copy the nameplates of the engine and the alternator (power, voltage, current, power factor, rpm, duty category); identify each system in the table of section 04; check oil, coolant and fuel levels; measure battery voltage and density; measure the alternator insulation between phases and to frame (method in generator testing). Acceptance criterion: complete form, no fluid leaks, insulation above the minimum of the IEEE 43 rule (Un in kV plus 1 MΩ: 1.4 MΩ for 400 V) and charged batteries.

    Practice 2 · Load test with a resistor bank

    Materials: set, three-phase resistive load bank, network analyzer or clamp with voltmeter and frequency meter, fuel measuring cylinder or flow meter, infrared thermometer. Steps: start the set and let it run unloaded for a couple of minutes; load it in steps of 25, 50, 75 and 100 %; at each step wait for stabilization and record voltage, frequency, currents, kW, oil pressure, coolant temperature and consumption; compute the g/kWh of each step; reduce the load and let it cool. Acceptance criterion: voltage within ±5 % of 400 V and frequency between 49 and 51 Hz at all steps (the contractual value is that of the manufacturer’s specification); no alarms; consumption close to the catalogue value.

    Practice 3 · Transfer switchboard sequence

    Materials: training ATS with a low-power set, lamp load, stopwatch, multimeter, phase-sequence meter. Steps: with the grid present, check the phase sequence of both sources; open the supply breaker to simulate the failure; time with the stopwatch how long until the set starts and until the lamps light again; restore the grid and time the return delay and the cool-down; with the switchboard de-energized and the teacher present, try to close both contactors by hand and check that the interlock prevents it. Acceptance criterion: transfer with no simultaneous closing in any test, times within the settings and a complete record.

    Practice 4 · Synchronize two small sets

    Materials: two single-phase or three-phase sets of a few kVA with an AVR, synchroscope or three lamps, voltmeters, frequency meters, clamp ammeter, resistive load, with the teacher in charge and each set’s own protections. Steps: start G1 and load it; check the sequence of G2; start G2 and adjust voltage and frequency to those of G1, with G2 slightly faster; watch the synchroscope and close the G2 breaker when the pointer passes slowly through 12 o’clock; share the load by adjusting the speed of each engine, and measure each set’s current. Acceptance criterion: closing without a noticeable current jerk on the clamp, and load sharing between equal sets with a difference of less than 10 % of the rated current.

    In all four practices

    The set is tested outdoors or with the exhaust discharged outside the workshop; nobody works on rotating parts or near the hot exhaust; and before touching wiring or connections the set is stopped, the batteries are disconnected and the absence of voltage is verified with an instrument. Nothing is loaded or connected to the workshop supply without the teacher.

    08Common mistakes

    MistakeConsequence
    Sizing by kW only (or by kVA only)A set with an alternator to spare and an engine that falls short, or the reverse: the voltage sags or the machine overheats.
    Forgetting motor startingThe voltage drops too much, contactors drop out and the motor does not start: the load is left without service just when it is needed.
    Using the emergency rating for daily serviceThe engine works above what the manufacturer guarantees and ages quickly.
    Testing the emergency set at no loadSoot and unburned diesel build up; when needed, it will not take the load. It is tested under load.
    Connecting the set to the installation with a plug or a jumperBackfeed to the utility grid: risk of death for whoever works on the line.
    Closing the breaker of a paralleled set without watching the synchroscopeShort-circuit current and a mechanical blow to the coupling: broken shafts and windings.
    Leaving the tank nearly empty and undrainedNo fuel at the moment of the outage, and water and sediment that clog the filters.
    Changing the oil only by running hoursOn an emergency set the hours are few, but the oil ages anyway: it is also changed by time.
    Installing the set in an unventilated roomOverheating, loss of power and, in the worst case, carbon monoxide in the building.

    09Self-assessment

    What does the AVR regulate and what does the governor of a generating set regulate?

    The AVR regulates the voltage, acting on the alternator’s excitation current. The governor regulates the frequency, acting on the fuel to keep the engine speed constant.

    A three-phase set of 100 kVA, 400 V and power factor 0.8. How many kW does it deliver and what is its rated current?

    The active power is:

    P=100kVA·0.8=80kW

    and the rated current:

    I=100000VA3·400V≈144A

    The engine must also be able to deliver those 80 kW at the shaft (plus the alternator losses).

    Why does a four-pole set turn at 1,500 rpm?

    Because in a synchronous alternator the frequency is tied to the speed. For 50 Hz and p = 2 pole pairs:

    n=60·502=1500rpm
    How do a Pelton, a Francis and a Kaplan turbine differ?

    In the kind of drop they use. The Pelton is an impulse turbine, for high heads and small flows; the Francis, a reaction turbine, for medium heads and flows; the Kaplan, a reaction turbine with axial flow and adjustable blades, for low heads and large flows.

    List the steps of an automatic transfer switchboard sequence upon a grid failure.

    The controller detects the loss of voltage and waits for a delay; it gives the start order and opens the grid contactor; when the set is stable it closes the set contactor (never both at once). When the grid returns, it waits for a delay, retransfers and lets the set cool unloaded before stopping it.

    Which four conditions must be met to synchronize a set with a busbar?

    Equal voltage, equal frequency, equal phase sequence and equal phase angle. The synchroscope shows the last two: the pointer spins if the frequencies differ and points to 12 o’clock when the waves are in phase.

    Two sets in parallel with 4 % droop (51 Hz at no load): one of 60 kW and one of 40 kW. The load rises to 90 kW. At what frequency do they run and how much does each take?

    With the same droop, each takes the same fraction of its full load: 90 / 100 = 0.9. The common frequency is:

    90kW100kW=0.9→f90 %=51−0.9·2=49.2Hz

    G1 takes 0.9 · 60 = 54 kW and G2 takes 0.9 · 40 = 36 kW.

    A set has an engine with 36.7 % efficiency and an alternator with 90.5 %. How much fuel is needed per electrical kWh?

    The overall efficiency is:

    ηoverall=0.367·0.905=0.332

    For every kWh at the terminals, 1 / 0.332 = 3.0 kWh of fuel is burned, that is, about 0.30 L of diesel.
